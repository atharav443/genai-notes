How to teach a machine to draw by making it play a game it is supposed to lose — and why the game is so hard to keep fair

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. Each box is numbered so you can find it again, the questions are collected at the end, and the same list ships as a plain-text file you can import into any spaced-repetition system. On screen, each Part ends with a deck that deals its cards to you one at a time.

Everything here follows pages 7–42 of an IIT Madras generative-AI course: the handwritten notes. A few short passages are marked beyond the notes: those go further than the pages do, and the marking is there so you can always tell which is which. What follows is one long argument, and it has a shape: we want a number that measures how wrong a generator is, we cannot compute that number, we bound it, the bound turns into a game, the game breaks, and a softer number fixes it.

Part I — A number that says how wrong you are

1 · Two distributions and one number

Here is the shape of the idea, before any notation. Stand at a single point in the space and ask one question: is this point more typical of the real data or of the fake? The honest answer is a ratio of two densities. Now walk over every point and collect those ratios into a field. An f-divergence is nothing more than a rule for boiling that field down to a single number, and the function f is the rule. Change f and you change which disagreements you care about — which is why two rulers can look at the same pair of distributions and disagree about which pair is further apart.

You have a pile of real images. You have a machine that makes fake ones. You would like to know how far the fakes are from the real thing — not image by image, which is hopeless, but distribution to distribution, as a single number you can drive to zero.

There is no unique such number. There is a whole family of them, and almost every method in this essay is a choice from that family. The family is called f-divergence. Pick a distribution for real data, call its density \(P_x\), pick the generator's distribution, call its density \(P_\theta\), and define

\[ D_f(P_x \| P_\theta) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} P_\theta(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{P_x(x)}{P_\theta(x)}\right) dx \]

where \(f: \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}\) is convex, left-semicontinuous, and satisfies \(f(1) = 0\). That last condition is the one to remember. Inside the integral sits a ratio of densities. Where the two distributions agree, the ratio is 1, and \(f(1) = 0\) makes that spot contribute nothing. The divergence is built to read zero exactly when there is nothing to report.

Question  1.1 In the f-divergence, why does the definition insist that \(f(1) = 0\)?

Answer Because the argument of \(f\) is the density ratio \(P_x/P_\theta\). When the two densities are equal the ratio is 1 everywhere, and we want the divergence to be zero there. Any convex \(f\) with \(f(1)\neq 0\) would report a constant offset even for a perfect match.

Question  1.2 Why is \(D_f \ge 0\) for any admissible \(f\)?

Answer Jensen's inequality. \(f\) is convex and the ratio integrates to 1 against \(P_\theta\), so \(D_f = \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}[f(u)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}[u]) = f(1) = 0\). Non-negativity is not an extra assumption; it falls out of convexity plus \(f(1)=0\).

Question  1.3 Which generator function \(f(u)\) turns \(D_f(P_x\|P_\theta)\) into the forward KL divergence?

Answer \(f(u) = u\log u\). Substituting gives \(\int P_x \log(P_x/P_\theta)\,dx = D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta)\).

Question  1.4 Which \(f\) gives the total variation distance?

Answer \(f(u) = \tfrac{1}{2}|u-1|\), giving \(\tfrac12\int|P_x - P_\theta|\,dx\).

Question  1.5 Is an f-divergence symmetric in its two arguments?

Answer Not in general. KL is the standard counter-example: \(D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta) \ne D_{KL}(P_\theta\|P_x)\). Jensen–Shannon and total variation happen to be symmetric members of the family.

The asymmetry, in numbers

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

Take two coins. The real one is fair, \(P_x = (0.5, 0.5)\); the generator's lands heads nine times in ten, \(P_\theta = (0.9, 0.1)\). Forward KL averages the log-ratio under the data:

\[ D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta) = 0.5\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.9} + 0.5\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.1} = -0.294 + 0.805 = 0.511 \text{ nats} \]

Reverse KL averages under the model:

\[ D_{KL}(P_\theta\|P_x) = 0.9\log\tfrac{0.9}{0.5} + 0.1\log\tfrac{0.1}{0.5} = 0.529 - 0.161 = 0.368 \text{ nats} \]

Same two coins, two different distances. The forward direction is larger because its expensive term is the one where the data puts real mass (tails, 0.5) and the model puts almost none (0.1): forward KL punishes a model for missing what the data does. Reverse KL weights by the model, so it mostly asks whether what the model does is plausible. That one asymmetry is the seed of the mode-covering versus mode-seeking behaviour you will meet in every later essay.

Question  1.6 Forward KL \(D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta)\) takes its expectation under which distribution, and what does that make it punish most?

Answer Under the data \(P_x\). Its big terms are where the data has mass and the model has little, so it punishes the model for missing parts of the data.

The one idea worth carrying forward Every density ratio is a test. If you could evaluate the ratio \(P_x/P_\theta\) at a point, you would know whether that point is more real than fake. Almost the whole essay is about learning that ratio instead of evaluating it.

Part II — The only thing you can actually compute

2 · What a generative model is asked to do

Picture a machine with a dial on the front. You turn the dial to a random setting and a picture comes out the back. The dial is a vector of standard normal noise; the machine is a neural network. You want the pictures leaving the back to be indistinguishable from the ones on your hard drive — not one at a time, but as a population. Get that right and the machine stops being a copier and becomes a factory: it can make pictures it has never seen, because it draws from the distribution rather than from the dataset.

Give the generator a knob: a vector \(z\) drawn from a standard normal. Push it through a neural network \(g_\theta\). Out comes a sample \(\hat{x} \sim P_\theta\). The dream is that \(P_\theta = P_x\), at which point your sampler is the data distribution. Formally,

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} D_f(P_x \| P_\theta) \]

And here is the difficulty, stated plainly: that expression contains two densities, and you know neither of them. You cannot write down \(P_x\) for photographs. You cannot even write down \(P_\theta\) — it is the distribution of outputs of a neural network with a random input, and nobody has a formula for that.

What you do have is samples. From \(P_x\): your dataset \(D = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}\). From \(P_\theta\): the outputs \(g_\theta(z)\) for as many \(z\) as you care to draw. So the real objective cannot be "minimise this integral". It has to become "minimise something I can estimate from samples".

Question  2.1 State precisely what we have and what we lack, when trying to minimise \(D_f\).

Answer We have samples from both distributions — the dataset for \(P_x\), generator outputs for \(P_\theta\). We lack the density functions themselves. So any usable objective must be expressible as an expectation over the two distributions, because expectations can be approximated by averages.

Question  2.2 Which theorem licenses replacing an integral with an average, and what exactly does it say?

Answer The law of large numbers. For iid \(x_i \sim P_x\), \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} h(x_i) \to \mathbb{E}_{P_x}[h(x)]\) as \(n \to \infty\). It converts \(\int h(x) P_x(x) dx\) into a sum you can actually compute — which is why the entire next Part is spent rewriting \(D_f\) as a pair of expectations.

Question  2.3 In a GAN, where does a sample \(\hat{x} \sim P_\theta\) come from?

Answer Draw \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and push it through the generator network: \(\hat{x} = g_\theta(z)\). We can sample \(P_\theta\) freely but never evaluate its density.

Part III — Turning a divergence into an expectation

3 · The conjugate function

There is a way to describe a convex curve that sounds perverse until the moment you need it. Instead of listing the height of the curve at every point, list the straight lines that touch it and never cross it. A convex curve is the upper envelope of its own tangents, so the tangents carry exactly the same information as the curve — just stored differently. The conjugate function is the bookkeeping for that second description. We are about to trade a formula we cannot evaluate for a pile of straight lines that we can.

The obstacle is that \(f\) is applied to a ratio we cannot compute. The way out is a piece of convex analysis that looks like a detour and turns out to be the whole trick. For convex \(f\) there exists a conjugate

\[ f^*(t) = \sup_{u} \{\, u t - f(u) \,\} \]

The conjugate is itself convex, and it is an involution in the sense that \(\left[f^*(t)\right]^* = f(u)\), which gives the recovery formula

\[ f(u) = \sup_{t} \{\, t u - f^*(t) \,\} \]

Now substitute the ratio in as \(u = P_x/P_\theta\) and push this inside the integral:

\[ D_f = \int P_\theta(x) f\!\left(\frac{P_x(x)}{P_\theta(x)}\right) dx = \int P_\theta(x) \sup_{t}\left\{ t\frac{P_x(x)}{P_\theta(x)} - f^*(t) \right\} dx \]

For each \(x\) the maximising \(t\) is different — it depends on where you are in the space. So the supremum is not over a single number, it is over a function \(T(x)\) that returns the right \(t\) at each point. Replace \(t\) by \(T(x)\), swap the supremum outside the integral, and \(P_x/P_\theta\) cancels against the \(P_\theta\) multiplying it:

\[ D_f \;\ge\; \sup_{T} \left[\, \mathbb{E}_{P_x} T(x) - \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta} f^*(T(x)) \,\right] \]

Read that line slowly, because everything after it is machinery. Both terms are expectations — one over real samples, one over generated samples — so both can be estimated by averaging. The unknown densities are gone. What survives is a lower bound, and the price of removing the densities is that we now have to optimise over a space of functions.

One conjugate, worked by hand

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

The definition is easier to trust once you have turned the handle. Take \(f(u) = u\log u\), the KL generator. For a fixed slope \(t\), maximise \(ut - u\log u\) over \(u\): the derivative is \(t - \log u - 1\), which is zero at \(u = e^{t-1}\). Substitute back and

\[ f^*(t) = e^{t-1}(t) - e^{t-1}(t-1) = e^{t-1} \]

So the KL bound reads \(D_{KL} \ge \sup_T\,\mathbb{E}_{P_x}T - \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}e^{T-1}\). And the reason it is a lower bound is a single line. Because \(f(u)\) is the supremum over \(t\), any particular \(t\) can only fall short:

\[ f(u) \;\ge\; tu - f^*(t) \quad \text{for every } t \qquad\text{(Fenchel--Young)} \]

A critic that is not the best possible critic is just a particular, suboptimal choice of \(t\) at each point, so it under-reports the divergence. It never over-reports.

Question  3.1 What is the convex conjugate of \(f(u) = u\log u\)?

Answer \(f^*(t) = e^{t-1}\). Set the derivative of \(ut - u\log u\) to zero, giving \(u = e^{t-1}\), and substitute back.

Question  3.2 Write the variational lower bound on an f-divergence in terms of a critic \(T\).

Answer \(D_f(P_x\|P_\theta) \ge \sup_T\left[\mathbb{E}_{P_x}T(x) - \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}f^*(T(x))\right]\).

Question  3.3 Why can an imperfect critic only under-estimate the divergence, never over-estimate it?

Answer Fenchel–Young: \(f(u) \ge tu - f^*(t)\) for every \(t\). A suboptimal critic is a suboptimal \(t\) at each point, so each pointwise term falls below its true value.

Question  3.4 Why does the bound become a supremum over functions rather than over a scalar?

Answer Because the inner problem is solved separately at each \(x\): the optimal \(t\) is a function of \(x\). If our function class is rich enough to contain that optimum we get equality; in general we get a lower bound, since we may be maximising over a restricted set \(T(x) \in \mathcal{T}\).

Question  3.5 In one sentence, what did the conjugate function buy us?

Answer It moved \(f\) off the density ratio. Instead of evaluating a convex function of \(P_x/P_\theta\) — impossible, no densities — we get an expectation of \(T(x)\) under real data minus an expectation of \(f^*(T(x))\) under generated data, both of which are sample averages.

Part IV — From a bound to a game

4 · Two networks, one objective, opposite directions

Before the algebra, the story — because the story is what you will keep. One network is a forger. It never sees a real banknote; it only ever sees the detective’s reaction to its last attempt. The other is a detective who has seen both real notes and forgeries. The forger improves by making the detective wrong; the detective improves by being right. Neither can win outright, and the contest stops only when the detective is reduced to guessing — at which point the theory says the forger is producing the real thing.

We still have a supremum over a function class, which is not something you can run gradient descent on directly. So parametrise: let \(T\) be a neural network \(T_w(x)\) with weights \(w\). We are minimising over the generator's parameters \(\theta\) and maximising over the critic's weights \(w\):

\[ \theta^*, w^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \arg\max_{w} \left[\, \mathbb{E}_{P_x} T_w(x) - \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta} f^*(T_w(x)) \,\right] \]

This is a saddle-point problem, not a minimisation, and that single fact explains most of the grief later. One network pushes the quantity up, the other pushes it down, and you are looking for the place where neither can improve.

One detail keeps the construction honest: \(T_w\) must land in the domain of \(f^*\). The notes handle this by writing \(T_w(x) = \sigma_f(V_w(x))\), where \(V_w\) is an ordinary network into \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\sigma_f\) is an activation chosen for the particular \(f\) so that the output lands where \(f^*\) accepts it.

5 · Choosing \(f\) gives you GANs

Now make a choice. Take

\[ f(u) = u\log u - (u+1)\log(u+1) \]

which is a close cousin of Jensen–Shannon. Its conjugate is \(f^*(t) = -\log(1 - e^{t})\) with domain \(\mathbb{R}^-\), so the critic must output negative numbers — and the matching activation is \(\sigma_f(v) = -\log(1 + e^{-v})\), a softplus flipped upside down. Write \(D_w(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-V_w(x)}}\) for the sigmoid. Substituting all of this through produces, after the dust settles,

\[ J_{\text{GAN}}(\theta, w) = \mathbb{E}_{P_x}\left[\log D_w(x)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}\left[\log\left(1 - D_w(x)\right)\right] \]

That is the original GAN objective, and it was never invented out of thin air. It is a specific f-divergence, run through a conjugate, with a sigmoid sitting where the activation is required to sit.

What the critic converges to

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

Hold the generator still and ask what the best possible discriminator is. The objective is an integral, \(\int P_x\log D + P_\theta\log(1-D)\,dx\), and nothing couples one point to another, so maximise it pointwise. At a single \(x\) write \(a = P_x(x)\), \(b = P_\theta(x)\): maximise \(a\log D + b\log(1-D)\). The derivative \(a/D - b/(1-D)\) vanishes at

\[ D^*(x) = \frac{P_x(x)}{P_x(x) + P_\theta(x)} \]

The ideal discriminator is a density ratio in disguise — the thing Part I said we would end up learning. Put it back in, with \(M = \tfrac12(P_x + P_\theta)\):

\[ J_{\text{GAN}}(\theta, D^*) = D_{KL}(P_x\|M) + D_{KL}(P_\theta\|M) - \log 4 = 2\,\mathrm{JS}(P_x\|P_\theta) - \log 4 \]

So a generator facing a perfect discriminator is minimising Jensen–Shannon divergence, and the game's value bottoms out at \(-\log 4\) exactly when \(P_\theta = P_x\), where \(D^* = \tfrac12\) everywhere: the detective reduced to a coin flip.

Question  4.1 Maximise \(a\log D + b\log(1-D)\) over \(D \in (0,1)\). What is the maximiser?

Answer \(D = a/(a+b)\). This one-line calculation, done at every \(x\) with \(a = P_x(x)\), \(b = P_\theta(x)\), gives the optimal discriminator.

Question  4.2 What is the optimal GAN discriminator \(D^*(x)\) for a fixed generator?

Answer \(D^*(x) = P_x(x)/(P_x(x) + P_\theta(x))\) — the probability that \(x\) is real, which is a function of the density ratio.

Question  4.3 Against the optimal discriminator, which divergence is the generator minimising?

Answer Jensen–Shannon: \(J_{\text{GAN}}(\theta, D^*) = 2\,\mathrm{JS}(P_x\|P_\theta) - \log 4\).

Question  4.4 When \(P_\theta = P_x\), what does \(D^*\) output and what is the value of the game?

Answer \(D^* = \tfrac12\) everywhere, and \(J_{\text{GAN}} = -\log 4\). The discriminator can do no better than chance.

Question  4.5 How is the critic output \(T_w\) related to the discriminator probability \(D_w\) in the original GAN?

Answer \(T_w = \sigma_f(V_w) = \log D_w\), and \(f^*(T_w) = -\log(1 - D_w)\). Substituting into \(\mathbb{E}T - \mathbb{E}f^*(T)\) gives \(\mathbb{E}\log D + \mathbb{E}\log(1-D)\).

Question  4.6 Why does the critic need a special activation \(\sigma_f\) instead of being a plain network?

Answer Because \(f^*\) is only defined on a restricted domain. For the GAN choice that domain is \(\mathbb{R}^-\), so the critic must be forced to emit negative values. \(\sigma_f(v) = -\log(1+e^{-v})\) is exactly such a squashing, and composing it with a sigmoid is what turns the abstract bound into \(\log D + \log(1-D)\).

Question  4.7 The familiar GAN loss is usually presented as "classifier log-loss". What is it really?

Answer A variational lower bound on one particular f-divergence, with the f-divergence chosen so that \(f^*\) has domain \(\mathbb{R}^-\). The classifier interpretation is true and useful, but it is a reading of the formula, not the derivation.

Question  4.8 The objective is written \(\min_\theta \max_w\). Why can it not be written as a single minimisation?

Answer Because we are bounding a divergence from below and then tightening: the critic maximises the bound, the generator minimises it. The solution is a saddle point, and gradient descent on a saddle point is not guaranteed to converge the way it is for a minimum.

Part V — Training it

6 · Two update rules, and a term that disappears

Notice what the forger is actually told. Not “make something beautiful”, and not “match the data” — but “make the detective wrong”. While the detective is competent those two instructions coincide. While it is not, they come apart, and the forger will cheerfully learn the detective’s blind spots instead of the structure of the data. Hold that thought: it is the same failure, seen from the other side, that returns in Part VI.

In practice you never optimise the expectations. You sample. Draw a batch \(x_1, \dots, x_{B_1}\) from the data, draw \(z_1, \dots, z_{B_2}\) from the normal, and form

\[ J_{\text{GAN}} \approx \frac{1}{B_1}\sum_{i=1}^{B_1} \log D_w(x_i) + \frac{1}{B_2}\sum_{j=1}^{B_2} \log\left(1 - D_w(g_\theta(z_j))\right) \]

Training the critic is a gradient ascent on that expression with \(\theta\) frozen. Training the generator is a descent with \(w\) frozen — and here something quietly important happens. The first sum contains no \(\theta\) at all, so it drops out of the gradient. The generator only ever sees the second term,

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \frac{1}{B_2}\sum_{j=1}^{B_2} \log\left(1 - D_w(g_\theta(z_j))\right) \]

The generator is never told "make this look like the data". It is told "make the critic wrong". Those are not the same instruction, and the difference matters when the critic is imperfect.

Question  5.1 Why does the generator's objective contain only one of the two terms?

Answer The term \(\mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log D_w(x)\) contains no \(\theta\), so its gradient with respect to \(\theta\) is exactly zero. The generator therefore sees only \(\mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}\log(1 - D_w(g_\theta(z)))\). This is also why the non-saturating variant of the loss exists — but that variant is not in these notes.

Question  5.2 In the two update rules, which parameter is held constant in each?

Answer Critic step: \(\theta\) is constant, \(w\) moves up the gradient of \(J_{\text{GAN}}\). Generator step: \(w\) is constant, \(\theta\) moves down. The generator's samples are computed with the fixed \(\theta\) during the critic's step, which is what makes the alternation well defined.

The non-saturating loss

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

In Part V the generator minimised the log-probability that its output is fake. When the critic is confident, that probability is near zero and the expression flattens — so the gradient dies precisely when the generator is worst, which is the worst possible time for it to die. The fix is a sign flip: instead of minimising the log-probability that the sample is fake, maximise the log-probability that it is real. Same fixed point, entirely different gradient near the start. The original paper does this, and most people never notice they are training a different objective from the one the derivation hands them.

The numbers make the point. Write the discriminator as a sigmoid of a logit, \(D = \sigma(v)\). The minimax generator loss \(\log(1 - \sigma(v))\) has slope \(-\sigma(v)\) in \(v\); the non-saturating loss \(-\log\sigma(v)\) has slope \(-(1 - \sigma(v))\). Early in training the critic rejects a fake with \(D = 0.01\). The first slope is \(-0.01\), the second \(-0.99\): the same situation, a gradient ninety-nine times larger.

Question  5.3 What does the non-saturating generator loss maximise instead of minimising \(\log(1 - D(g_\theta(z)))\)?

Answer \(\log D(g_\theta(z))\): the log-probability that the critic calls the fake real. Same fixed point, much stronger gradient when the critic is winning.

Question  5.4 The critic gives a fake \(D = 0.01\). Per unit of critic logit, how strong is the generator gradient under the minimax loss versus the non-saturating loss?

Answer Minimax: slope \(\sigma(v) = 0.01\). Non-saturating: \(1 - \sigma(v) = 0.99\). The minimax gradient vanishes exactly when the generator is worst.

Mode collapse

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

The failure nobody in these pages warns you about, and the one practitioners complain about most. Notice what the objective actually asks for: not “cover the data distribution”, but “fool the critic”. If one image fools it reliably, the cheapest strategy is to produce that image, or a handful of variations, forever. The generator has found a region where the critic is wrong and stopped exploring. You notice it because the sample grid shows the same face forty times, while the loss keeps improving — which is another way of saying the number you are optimising has stopped measuring the thing you care about.

The patches are unsentimental: let the critic see a batch at once so it can notice samplers repeating themselves; swap in an objective that never saturates; or add a little noise to the critic’s inputs so that memorising one output is no longer a stable strategy.

Question  5.5 What is mode collapse, and why does the GAN objective permit it?

Answer The generator emits one output or a few variations for every \(z\). The objective only asks it to fool the critic, and one reliably-fooling image does that without covering the data.

Convolutional and conditional GANs

Two practical variants close this part of the notes. A DCGAN replaces the generator's fully connected layers with transpose (up-) convolutions, so a short code such as \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{16}\) is stretched into an image tensor of shape \(3 \times r \times c\). The theory is untouched; the network simply knows that images are made of local patches.

A conditional GAN wants \(P_{x\mid y}\) rather than \(P_x\): "a picture of a 7", not "a picture". The data come in pairs \((x_i, y_i)\), and the label — a one-hot vector or a text embedding — is fed to both networks:

\[ J(\theta, w) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P_{xy}}\log D_w(x, y) + \mathbb{E}_{y,\ \hat{x}\sim P^\theta_{x\mid y}}\log\left(1 - D_w(\hat{x}, y)\right) \]

The discriminator must see \(y\) too. If it judged images alone, a perfectly realistic 3 would pass as an answer to "draw a 7", and the generator would learn to ignore its label.

Question  5.6 In a DCGAN, what layer type grows the latent code into an image?

Answer Transpose (up-)convolutions, which upsample a small code such as \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{16}\) to a \(3 \times r \times c\) image tensor.

Question  5.7 In a conditional GAN, which network receives the condition \(y\)?

Answer Both. The generator computes \(g_\theta(z, y)\) and the discriminator scores the pair \(D_w(x, y)\).

Question  5.8 Why must a cGAN discriminator see the label as well as the image?

Answer Otherwise any realistic image fools it regardless of class, so the generator has no reason to obey \(y\). Judging the pair is what forces the image to match the label.

Part VI — Why the game breaks

7 · A failed classifier is not a match

Here is the appealing story. A perfect critic separates real from fake. Train the generator until the critic can no longer separate them. Then the two distributions must be the same. The first two steps are sound. The conclusion is not.

Put two clusters of real data in the plane and let the generator place a third cluster somewhere between them. A linear classifier can fail badly on that arrangement while the distributions remain obviously different. What follows is

\[ P_x = P_\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \text{classifier fails} \qquad\text{but}\qquad \text{classifier failure} \;\nRightarrow\; P_x = P_\theta \]

So the critic cannot be a fixed judge. It has to move along with the generator, each chasing the other — which is precisely why the min and the max are intertwined in one expression instead of being solved in sequence.

Question  6.1 Does "the classifier can no longer tell real from fake" imply \(P_x = P_\theta\)?

Answer No. Equal distributions make every classifier fail, but a particular classifier can fail on different distributions (e.g. a linear one against clusters it cannot separate). Hence the critic must keep adapting.

8 · The manifold hypothesis and the saturation problem

Picture two sheets of paper floating in a dark room. One is the data. The other is what the generator makes. Both are vanishingly thin, and two thin sheets placed at random in a large space almost never touch. The detective only has to say which sheet a point lies on, and with the sheets apart a competent detective can do that perfectly — without ever learning what is printed on them. That is the whole failure. The game is won by a classifier that has understood nothing about images, and the number we chose in Part I stops moving while the generator is still terrible.

The deeper reason GANs are unstable is geometric. Real images do not fill \(\mathbb{R}^d\); they lie on a much lower-dimensional surface inside it. A \(28\times 28\) photograph of white noise lives in 784 dimensions, but the set of photographs that look like anything occupies a vanishing sliver of that space. So the support of \(P_x\) and the support of \(P_\theta\) are two thin sheets that, with very high probability, do not intersect at all.

When the supports are disjoint, an ideal critic can separate them perfectly. Once it does, the f-divergence stops depending on \(\theta\) — the gradient the generator needs vanishes, and training stalls. This is saturation. The number we chose in Part I was built to be zero exactly at a match, and it turns out to be uninformative everywhere else.

The f-divergences only know whether the points coincide. Move the generator from \(\theta = 10\) to \(\theta = 0.001\) and Jensen–Shannon reports \(\log 2\) the whole way, a flat landscape with a single hole at the answer. The earth-mover distance shrinks smoothly as the points approach, which is exactly a gradient pointing home.

Question  6.2 Real data is a point mass at 0, the generator a point mass at \(\theta \neq 0\). What is their Jensen–Shannon divergence?

Answer \(\log 2\), for every \(\theta \neq 0\): the maximum, because the supports do not overlap. Its gradient in \(\theta\) is zero.

Question  6.3 Same two point masses. What is their Wasserstein distance?

Answer \(|\theta|\) — the mass must travel distance \(|\theta|\). It shrinks as the generator approaches, so it gives a useful gradient.

Question  6.4 Why, geometrically, do the supports of \(P_x\) and \(P_\theta\) usually fail to overlap?

Answer The manifold hypothesis: both live on thin low-dimensional surfaces inside \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and two such surfaces in general position almost never intersect.

Question  6.5 Explain why a perfect discriminator halts training.

Answer With disjoint supports the critic can be 100% accurate, so \(D_f(P_x \| P_\theta)\) no longer varies with \(\theta\). The generator's gradient is zero even though the generator is terrible. Training has saturated on a number that has stopped carrying information.

Question  6.6 What is the fix, stated as a requirement on the divergence?

Answer Use a softer metric that does not saturate when the supports fail to align — one that still reports a meaningful gradient even for disjoint distributions. That requirement is what leads to the Wasserstein metric.

9 · Moving earth instead of measuring overlap

Wasserstein’s move is to stop asking “do these overlap?” and start asking “what would it cost to turn one into the other?” Imagine each distribution as a pile of sand. A transport plan says how much sand to shovel from each spot in the first pile to each spot in the second, and the work is mass times distance. Piles far apart cost a great deal; piles close together cost a little. Crucially the cost keeps changing as they approach, even while they still do not overlap — and a number that keeps talking while the distributions converge is exactly what a training signal needs.

The Wasserstein idea is to stop asking "do these distributions overlap?" and start asking "how much work would it take to turn one into the other?" A transport plan \(\Pi(x, \hat{x})\) is a joint distribution whose marginals are the two distributions. You can picture it as a table: rows indexed by the real sample points, columns by the fake ones, and each cell saying how much probability mass is shipped from that row to that column. Row sums reproduce \(P_x\); column sums reproduce \(P_\theta\).

Shipping mass a distance costs. Move \(\Pi(x,\hat{x})\) units across a gap of \(\|x - \hat{x}\|\) and the work is the product. The average work of a plan is

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\Pi(x,\hat{x})} \|x - \hat{x}\| \]

Many plans are available; take the cheapest one:

\[ W_p(P_x \| P_\theta) = \min_{d \in \Pi} \mathbb{E}_{d(x,\hat{x})} \|x - \hat{x}\|_p \]

This is the Wasserstein metric, and it has the property we needed: it does not saturate when supports are disjoint, because moving mass is always expensive in proportion to the distance.

A table makes it concrete. Let the real data be half its mass at 0 and half at 1; the generator's, half at 2 and half at 3. One plan ships 0→2 and 1→3, a distance of 2 each: average work 2. Another ships 0→3 and 1→2, distances 3 and 1: average work, again, 2. In one dimension every monotone matching ties, and \(W_1 = 2\), which is also the distance between the two means. Slide the generator one unit left and the answer becomes 1. The number moves with the distributions, which is everything the f-divergences refused to do.

Question  6.7 \(P_x\) puts mass \(\tfrac12\) on each of \(\{0, 1\}\); \(P_\theta\) puts \(\tfrac12\) on each of \(\{2, 3\}\). What is \(W_1\)?

Answer 2. Ship 0→2 and 1→3: each unit of mass travels 2, so the cheapest average work is 2.

10 · The duality that makes it trainable

Minimising over every possible shovelling plan is not something a neural network can do, but there is a second way to ask the same question. Instead of enumerating plans, ask every possible inspector for a quote, under one restriction: an inspector’s price may not rise faster than distance does. The best such quote equals the cheapest plan. That equivalence is the duality, and it is why the critic in a WGAN is not squashed into a probability — it is merely forbidden from being too steep.

Minimising over all transport plans is not something a neural network can do. The Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality converts the problem into a maximisation over functions with a slope constraint:

\[ W(P_x \| P_\theta) = \max_{\|T_w(x)\|_L \le 1} \left[\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_x} T_w(x) - \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim P_\theta} T_w(\hat{x}) \,\right] \]

The condition \(\|T_w\|_L \le 1\) is 1-Lipschitz: the function may not change faster than its input, so \(\|T_w(x_1) - T_w(x_2)\| / \|x_1 - x_2\| \le 1\). Look at the shape of that objective next to the GAN objective from Part IV. It is the same \(\max_w\) of an expectation difference — except the critic is no longer squashed through a sigmoid into \([0,1]\), and the constraint is a slope bound rather than a domain restriction. That is the Wasserstein GAN.

Why is the slope bound necessary at all? Because without it the maximisation runs away. If some critic \(T\) scores real data higher than fake data by any margin, then \(cT\) scores it higher by \(c\) times that margin, and \(c\) can be as large as you like. The supremum is infinite and measures nothing. The Lipschitz constraint is what pins the scale.

The notes enforce it by normalising the critic's weights so that \(\|w\|_2 = 1\) after each gradient step; the original WGAN paper instead clipped every weight into a small box \([-c, c]\). Either way the critic is kept gentle, and WGAN training is markedly more stable than the original game.

Question  6.8 What is a transport plan, concretely?

Answer A joint distribution over pairs \((x, \hat{x})\) whose marginals are \(P_x\) and \(P_\theta\). As a table, entry \((i,j)\) says how much mass moves from real point \(x_i\) to fake point \(\hat{x}_j\). Row sums are \(P_x\), column sums are \(P_\theta\).

Question  6.9 What happens to the WGAN critic objective if the 1-Lipschitz constraint is dropped?

Answer It becomes unbounded: scaling any critic that separates the data by \(c\) multiplies the objective by \(c\), so the supremum is \(\infty\) and carries no information.

Question  6.10 State the 1-Lipschitz condition on the critic.

Answer \(|T_w(x_1) - T_w(x_2)| \le \|x_1 - x_2\|\) for all \(x_1, x_2\): the critic’s output may not change faster than its input.

Question  6.11 Name two crude ways to keep the WGAN critic Lipschitz after each gradient step.

Answer Clip every weight into \([-c, c]\) (the original paper), or renormalise the weights so \(\|w\|_2 = 1\) (the notes).

Question  6.12 Write the WGAN critic objective (Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual).

Answer \(\max_{\|T_w\|_L \le 1}\ \mathbb{E}_{P_x}T_w(x) - \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}T_w(\hat{x})\). No sigmoid, no log — just a difference of means.

The gradient penalty

Beyond the notes — not in pages 7–42.

Clipping and renormalising both work, and both are hacks: clipping crowds the weights onto the corners of a box and can cripple the critic until it is barely a critic at all. The later replacement drops the projection and adds a cost. Pick a random point on the segment between a real and a generated sample, \(\tilde{x} = \epsilon x + (1-\epsilon)\hat{x}\), and penalise the critic's slope there for straying from one:

\[ \lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}}\left(\|\nabla_{\tilde{x}}T_w(\tilde{x})\|_2 - 1\right)^2 \]

Same constraint, expressed as something the optimiser can trade off instead of something enforced by force.

Question  6.13 In WGAN-GP, where is the gradient penalty evaluated, and what does it penalise?

Answer At random interpolates \(\tilde{x} = \epsilon x + (1-\epsilon)\hat{x}\) between real and fake samples; it penalises \((\|\nabla_{\tilde{x}}T_w\|_2 - 1)^2\).

Part VII — Running the machine backwards

11 · Inversion: given the output, find the input

A trained GAN is a function from latent space to data space. Nothing about it is invertible — it is a many-to-one map, and there is no formula for going back. But you often want to: find the \(z\) that produced a particular real image. That \(z\) is a compressed description, so it works as a feature vector; and editing \(z\) and re-generating gives you an edit of the image.

The bidirectional GAN adds a second network to make this possible. Alongside the generator \(g_\theta : Z \to X\) and the discriminator, train an encoder \(E_\phi : X \to Z\). Then change what the discriminator looks at: instead of classifying \(x\) versus \(g_\theta(z)\), classify the tuples \((x, E_\phi(x))\) versus \((g_\theta(z), z)\). To win that game the encoder must supply a \(z\) that looks like it belongs with \(x\) — which is exactly the inverse we wanted.

A cheaper alternative in the same notes There is also a straightforward version: keep the ordinary adversarial loss and add a regression penalty \(\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim P_\theta}\|z - E_\phi(\hat{x})\|_2^2\) so the encoder is trained to recover the \(z\) that generated \(\hat{x}\). Same goal, less elegance, easier to get running.

Question  7.1 Why is a trained GAN generator not invertible by formula?

Answer \(g_\theta: Z \to X\) is a many-to-one neural map with no closed-form inverse, so recovering \(z\) from \(x\) needs a learned encoder or an optimisation.

Question  7.2 What extra loss term does latent-regression GAN inversion add?

Answer \(\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim P_\theta}\|z - E_\phi(\hat{x})\|_2^2\): the encoder must recover the \(z\) that generated each fake.

Question  7.3 In a BiGAN, what does the discriminator actually compare?

Answer Tuples, not samples. It sees \((x, E_\phi(x))\) from the real-plus-encoder side and \((g_\theta(z), z)\) from the generator side. The optimum is reached when \(P_{\hat{z}x} = P_{z\hat{x}}\) — the joint distribution of data with its code matches the joint of code with its generated image.

12 · Borrowing the adversary for something else entirely

The last idea in these pages is that this machinery is not really about images. Suppose you train a classifier on source data and then deploy it where the data comes from a different distribution. It will fail, and it will fail quietly, because the model has no way to notice that the world changed.

The domain-adversarial fix inserts a feature extractor \(\Phi\) that feeds both a classifier and a discriminator. The classifier wants features that predict labels; the discriminator wants to tell source features from target features; and \(\Phi\) is asked to satisfy both — predict well, and make the two feature distributions indistinguishable. When the discriminator can no longer separate them, the classifier's input looks statistically the same in both domains, and the same classifier transfers.

Question  7.4 In domain-adversarial training, what are the two objectives of the feature extractor \(\Phi\)?

Answer (a) Classification: features must predict source labels through \(h_\psi\). (b) Adversarial: features must make source and target indistinguishable to a domain discriminator.

Question  7.5 In domain-adversarial training, why does making the discriminator fail help the classifier?

Answer If the discriminator cannot tell source features from target features, then \(P_{f_s} = P_{f_t}\): the feature distribution is domain-invariant. A classifier trained on source features therefore sees inputs drawn from the same distribution at test time on the target domain.

Part VIII — How would you know it worked?

13 · Frechet Inception Distance

Comparing two choirs is hopeless if you listen to individual singers, and tractable if you compare the average pitch and how spread out the voices are. FID does that to images, except the “pitch” is the statistics of what a pretrained network says about them. Which also tells you where it can mislead: a generator that has learned the statistics without learning the pictures will score well, and the assumption that those statistics are Gaussian is doing quiet, unpaid work throughout.

You have a trained generator. Is it any good? Comparing pixels is useless — a one-pixel shift destroys a per-pixel metric while leaving the image essentially identical. So compare in a space where meaning lives: run both real and generated images through a pretrained Inception network, take the activations at some layer \(l\), and summarise each set by its mean and covariance.

Then assume each set of features is Gaussian and measure how far apart the two Gaussians are. The ruler is a cousin of the one from Part VI: the squared 2-Wasserstein distance (cost \(\|x - \hat{x}\|^2\) rather than \(\|x - \hat{x}\|\)), also called the Fréchet distance, which for Gaussians has a closed form:

\[ \text{FID} = \|\mu_{\text{true}} - \mu_{\text{gen}}\|_2^2 + \text{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\text{true}} + \Sigma_{\text{gen}} - 2(\Sigma_{\text{true}}\Sigma_{\text{gen}})^{1/2}\right) \]

The first term is a scalar measuring how far the centres drifted; the second is a matrix term measuring how the shapes disagree. Lower is better.

In one dimension it becomes transparent. With feature means \(\mu_1, \mu_2\) and standard deviations \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\), the trace term is \(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_1\sigma_2 = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2\), so

\[ \text{FID}_{1\text{-D}} = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2 \]

Real features with mean 0 and spread 1, generated with mean 0.5 and spread 0.5: FID \(= 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\). A generator that gets the average right but produces too little variety — mode collapse, seen through features — is caught by the second term even when the first is zero.

Question  8.1 Why not compare generated and real images pixel by pixel?

Answer Per-pixel distance is dominated by trivial misalignments and says nothing about whether the images are plausible. FID instead compares statistics of deep features, where semantic content is represented and small spatial shifts cost little.

Question  8.2 What does the first FID term, \(\|\mu_{\text{true}} - \mu_{\text{gen}}\|_2^2\), measure?

Answer How far the centre of the generated features has drifted from the centre of the real ones — a shift in average content.

Question  8.3 What does the trace term of FID measure?

Answer Whether the covariances match: the spread and correlation structure of the features. Too little variety (mode collapse) shows up here.

Question  8.4 What does FID reduce to in one dimension?

Answer \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2\).

Question  8.5 Real features: mean 0, s.d. 1. Generated: mean 0.5, s.d. 0.5. What is the 1-D FID?

Answer \(0.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 0.5\).

Question  8.6 What assumption does FID make that you should keep in mind before quoting it?

Answer That the layer-\(l\) features of each dataset are Gaussian, and that one pretrained Inception network on ImageNet is an appropriate judge. Both are convenient fictions; FID numbers are comparable within one setup, not across different feature extractors or layer choices.

Part IX — Footnotes to the notes

14 · Four places the notes need a footnote

The handwritten pages are a lecture, not a paper, and a lecture compresses. None of what follows makes the notes wrong in spirit, but each would trip you up if you took the page literally into an implementation or an exam.

1. The generator loss they derive is not the one anyone trains. The minimax form \(\log(1 - D(g(z)))\) saturates exactly when the generator is bad (Part V). Every working implementation uses the non-saturating form.

2. "Normalise so \(\|w\|_2 = 1\)" needs to be per layer. A network's Lipschitz constant is bounded by the product of its layers' operator norms (for 1-Lipschitz activations such as ReLU). A single global norm on all the weights at once bounds nothing useful. Read the instruction as layer-wise normalisation — spectral normalisation is the precise version, dividing each layer by its largest singular value.

3. The Wasserstein in WGAN and the Wasserstein in FID are different distances. WGAN uses \(W_1\) (cost \(\|x-\hat{x}\|\)), because the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality with a 1-Lipschitz critic holds only for \(W_1\). FID uses \(W_2^2\) (cost \(\|x-\hat{x}\|^2\)), because that is the one with a closed form between Gaussians.

4. The variational bound is tight only for a rich enough critic. \(D_f \ge \sup_T[\dots]\) becomes an equality when \(T\) can represent the optimum \(f'(P_x/P_\theta)\) everywhere. A finite network cannot, so in practice a GAN optimises a lower bound on its divergence whose gap depends on the critic's architecture. That is part of why GAN losses are poor progress meters.

Question  9.1 Why does the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual (a 1-Lipschitz critic) give \(W_1\) and not \(W_2\)?

Answer The duality with a Lipschitz critic is a theorem about the cost \(\|x - \hat{x}\|\), i.e. \(W_1\). FID uses \(W_2^2\) instead because it has a closed form for Gaussians.

Question  9.2 How is a multi-layer network’s Lipschitz constant bounded, and what does that imply for weight normalisation?

Answer By the product of each layer’s operator norm (with 1-Lipschitz activations). So normalisation must act per layer — spectral normalisation divides each by its largest singular value.

Question  9.3 Why is a GAN’s training loss a poor measure of sample quality?

Answer It is a lower bound whose gap depends on how good the critic currently is, and the critic keeps changing. A falling loss may mean a weaker critic, not a better generator.
