A

Why a VAE has to exist

The goal we want, the bet we make, and the wall we slam into.

The goal

We want a model we can sample new data from — i.e. learn the true distribution Px. Not just recognise data. Create it. Everything that follows exists to make that one thing trainable.

The bet: a hidden code

Real data is thin inside its own space. A 28×28 image is 784 numbers, but the set of plausible handwritten digits is a thin sheet inside ℝ784. There is enormous redundancy.

So we posit that every x was produced from a short hidden code z:

z ∈ ℝk, k ≪ d→ x (your data)
Careful — this is a bet, not a fact

We invent z. We never observe the real one — that is exactly why it is called latent. If the data has no such structure, the bet fails no matter how well we optimise.

The wall

Having a latent variable means our model has to marginalise z out:

Pθ(x)  =  ∫ Pθ(x, z) dz

An integral over all of latent space, sitting inside a log. There is no closed form, and you would need it at every single gradient step. So we cannot compute the likelihood — which kills maximum likelihood outright.

And it takes the posterior down with it, because the posterior needs that same quantity:

Pθ(z|x)  =  Pθ(x|z) · Pθ(z)  /  Pθ(x)    ← the thing we just said we cannot compute
Why this is a wall and not a speed bump

Training changes θ — the parameters. It does not change the fact that Pθ(x) is defined by an intractable integral. Swap in the perfect θ* and the integral is still intractable. You cannot train your way out of it.

Sanity check that it really is about the integral: a Gaussian Mixture Model has the same philosophy, but its z is discrete — so the marginal is a finite sum of M terms, not an integral. That is why EM works perfectly there and collapses completely here.

Phase A in one line: we want to generate data → we bet on a hidden code → marginalising that code out is intractable → so the likelihood and the posterior are both out of reach.
B

Bound it instead of computing it

What we do when the exact quantity is unreachable: squeeze it from below.

The move

We cannot compute log Pθ(x). So we stop trying, and instead build something we can compute that is guaranteed to sit underneath it.

Introduce any distribution q(z|x) — your own stand-in for the posterior. Multiply by 1 inside the integral, then apply Jensen's inequality:

log Pθ(x)  ≥  Eq[ log( Pθ(x,z) / q(z|x) ) ]  ≡  ELBO

That right-hand side is the Evidence Lower Bound. It contains only things we can evaluate: the model Pθ, and our own q.

But here is the sharper way to see it

The relationship is actually an exact identity — no inequality needed:

log Pθ(x)  =  ELBO  +  DKL( q(z|x) ‖ Pθ(z|x) )
log Pθ(x)= ELBO+ gap = DKL(q ‖ true posterior)

the gap is how wrong our q is — it is what separates what we want from what we can compute.

Read it carefully, because it explains everything that follows:

  • The ELBO is the log-likelihood minus how wrong q is.
  • Since a KL is never negative, the ELBO is always a lower bound. ✓
  • The bound is tight (gap = 0) exactly when q equals the true posterior.
The tension that defines the whole subject

We introduce q precisely to avoid the true posterior — but the bound is only good if q is close to the true posterior. We want Pθ(z|x); we cannot have it; so we approximate it and try to make the approximation as tight as we can.

Why that gap never fully closes

Our q can never exactly equal Pθ(z|x) — for two independent reasons:

  • q is confined to a family. We only search over Gaussians whose μ and σ come out of a network. The true posterior need not be Gaussian, nor representable by that net at all.
  • The target moves. Pθ(z|x) depends on θ, so every gradient step changes it — q is a function approximator chasing something that never stands still.

So the gap is narrowed, never closed. Note the two distinct layers: computing Pθ(z|x) at all is structurally impossible, whereas matching it is merely imperfect.

This is why it is called variational — we converted an intractable inference problem into an optimisation problem.

Phase B in one line: we can't compute the likelihood, so we compute a lower bound to it instead — and the distance between them is exactly how bad our approximation q is.
C

Two networks fall out of the bound

Splitting the ELBO in two gives us an encoder and a decoder — and tells us which one is the real model.

Split the ELBO

ELBO  =  Eq(z|x)[ log Pθ(x|z) ]  −  DKL( q(z|x) ‖ P(z) )           ↑ reconstruction         ↑ regularisation

Two objects appear, and each one is a network:

x→ ENCODER  q(z|x)→ μφ(x), σφ(x)→ z→ DECODER  Pθ(x|z)→ x̂θ(z)

The critical detail — not the thing you'd expect

A neural network outputs numbers. It cannot output a distribution. So each network outputs the parameters of a distribution, and you supply the randomness separately:

Encoder outputs

μφ(x) and σφ(x) — the mean and spread of a Gaussian over z.
It does not hand you a z. It hands you a blob, and you sample from it.

Decoder outputs

x̂θ(z) — the mean of a Gaussian over x.
Again not a sample — the centre of a distribution. This becomes the reconstruction.

And the detail that usually gets taught backwards

The two networks are completely separate — separate weights, no shared layers. They touch only through the sampled z handed between them. And crucially:

The decoder IS the generative model. It is what produces x. The encoder is scaffolding — a computational tool we invented to help us train, which is thrown away at generation time.

At generation you sample z ~ N(0,I) and push it through the decoder. The encoder never runs.

Why an "auto"-encoder?

Because during training the pair is asked to squeeze x into z and then rebuild it — which looks like an autoencoder. But the mechanism is entirely different, and the KL term is what makes it something more than a compressor. (That's Phase E.)

Phase C in one line: the bound factors into "rebuild the input" and "keep the code well-behaved", giving a decoder (the real model) and an encoder (the training aid) — both of which output parameters, not samples.
D

Sampling broke the gradient

A new problem, created by the fix in Phase C — and the one clever trick that resolves it.

The new problem

The encoder tells us where z lives. But to get an actual z we must draw a random sample from that distribution — and that draw sits directly in the path from the encoder's parameters to the loss:

z  =  sample( N(μφ(x), σφ(x)²) )

When the backward pass reaches that sampling step, it has no derivative rule to apply. It's an opaque node — a black box: parameters go in, randomness happens, a number comes out. The gradient chain is severed, and φ never learns.

The trick: move the randomness out of the parameters

Both of these produce exactly the same distribution for z:

✗  What blocks the gradient
sample( μ, σ )→z

The randomness is inside the thing whose parameters we must differentiate. Opaque → chain broken.

✓  What lets it flow
ε ~ N(0,1)→ z = μ + σ·ε

Randomness now lives in ε, a separate input. z is built from + and × — both differentiable.

Why are they the same? Because a Gaussian stays a Gaussian under shifting and scaling: if ε ~ N(0,1), then μ + σ·ε ~ N(μ, σ²). Same distribution, different procedure.

The one distinction that matters

ε is an input, not a parameter. Think of it exactly like the data x — something that feeds the network rather than something we tune.

• It receives no gradient (we never update ε).
• But it absolutely shapes the gradient to σ: since z = σ·ε, we get ∂J/∂σ = (∂J/∂z)·ε. A bigger |ε| means σ had a bigger lever on z, so it earns a bigger gradient.

No gradient flows into ε; but ε very much affects the gradient that flows elsewhere. Those are not contradictory.

What actually made the gradient computable was not ε itself — it was replacing an opaque sample() primitive with differentiable arithmetic. ε is merely the piece of that arithmetic that holds the randomness.

Consequence worth carrying forward

Not every distribution can be written as z = g(ε, φ) with ε from a fixed base. The ones that can are called reparameterizable — Gaussians, uniforms, exponentials. That is why the encoder's q is a Gaussian. It isn't just convenience; the trick demands it.

Phase D in one line: sampling from qφ is opaque and kills the gradient, so we rewrite the same random z as μ + σ·ε — moving the randomness into a fixed input and leaving a differentiable path behind.
E

Turning the bound into a loss you can train

Both terms become ordinary, familiar quantities — and the reconstruction loss stops being a mystery.

Term 1 — where MSE actually comes from

Recall the decoder defines a Gaussian over x: Pθ(x|z) = N(x; x̂θ(z), σ²I)

A Gaussian peaks at its mean, so "make x probable" means move the mean x̂ onto x:

log Pθ(x|z)  =  −(d/2)log(2πσ²) − ‖x − x̂θ(z)‖²/(2σ²)   ∴  maximising likelihood  ⇔  minimising ‖x − x̂θ(z)‖²
The realisation

The "reconstruction loss" is not an invented design choice — it is the log-likelihood of a Gaussian, rewritten. Change the likelihood (to Bernoulli, say, for binary pixels) and MSE becomes cross-entropy. Choose the likelihood; the loss writes itself.

(The dropped term −(d/2)log(2πσ²) contains no x, no z and no θ — it is a constant, so removing it cannot move the optimum. Maximising f(θ)+5 is the same as maximising f(θ).)

Term 2 — the KL, in closed form

DKL( q(z|x) ‖ N(0,I) )  =  ½ Σj ( μj² + σj² − 1 − log σj² )     ← no sampling, no integral — just arithmetic on μ and σ

And so the whole thing becomes a plain loss to minimise:

LOSS  =  ‖x − x̂θ(z)‖²  +  ½ Σj ( μj² + σj² − 1 − log σj² )

What the two terms are actually fighting over

Reconstruction pulls

"Put maximum information about x into z." Its cheapest win is to memorise — give every x its own isolated code, and drive σ → 0 so no noise blurs it.

KL pulls back

"Keep q(z|x) near N(0,I)." It pushes σ up toward 1, turning each code into a fuzzy blob and forcing neighbouring blobs to overlap. Note the −logσ² term explodes as σ → 0, so the KL actively punishes certainty.

Why you must keep both — and why overlap is the whole point

A plain autoencoder (no KL) drives σ → 0, so codes become isolated points in mostly-empty space. Reconstruction is excellent — but sample a random z ~ N(0,I) and you land in a gap, and the decoder outputs garbage. The KL term is what converts a compressor into a generator: overlapping blobs tile the space, so any z lands somewhere meaningful.

Phase E in one line: the first term becomes MSE, the second a closed-form KL — and their tug-of-war is what makes the latent space both informative and samplable.
