Random variables,
and the six distributions
worth knowing by name

A random variable is a function, a distribution is a story, and telling the story correctly is how you recognise which one you are looking at — including the one a language model emits fifty thousand numbers at a time

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the third of seven essays on probability. It needs the first two — sample spaces, counting, and conditioning — and it is the last one that stays discrete. Read it before the essay on expectation, and both before anything in the generative-AI series, where the output of every model is a distribution of exactly the kind named here.

Part I — A random variable is a function

1 · A function wearing a number's clothes

You roll two dice and add them. Call the total \(X\). What is \(X\)?

“Seven” is one answer, and it is the answer to a different question. \(X\) is not a number: it is the rule that takes an outcome and returns a number. Applied to the outcome \((3,4)\) it returns 7. Applied to \((5,5)\) it returns 10. The randomness lives in the outcome; \(X\) itself is completely deterministic.

\[ X : S \to \mathbb{R} \]

That is the entire definition, and it is worth being fussy about because two of the most useful moves in probability are consequences of it. The first: several random variables can live on one sample space. On the same two dice, let \(X\) be the sum and \(Y\) the maximum. Then \(X\big((3,4)\big) = 7\) and \(Y\big((3,4)\big) = 4\). Nothing changed about the dice; we looked at the outcome through two different lenses. This is exactly how a dataset works — one underlying thing, many recorded variables.

The second: a random variable does not have to be numeric in origin. Toss a coin and let \(X = 1\) for heads, \(0\) for tails. \(X\) is a function from \(\{H, T\}\) to \(\{0,1\}\), and it will turn out to be the single most useful random variable in the subject.

Because \(X\) is a function, “\(X = 7\)” is shorthand for an event — the set of outcomes where the function takes the value 7. Saying \(P(X = 7) = 1/6\) is saying that six of the 36 outcomes land in that set. Nothing new was introduced. A random variable is a way of naming events compactly.

Question  1.1 Define a random variable, precisely.

Answer A function from the sample space to the real numbers: \(X : S \to \mathbb{R}\).

Question  1.2 Two dice are rolled. Give two different random variables on the same sample space.

Answer \(X\) = the sum, \(Y\) = the maximum. Both are functions on the 36 ordered pairs; for the outcome \((3,4)\), \(X = 7\) and \(Y = 4\).

Question  1.3 Where does the randomness in a random variable actually sit?

Answer In the outcome, not in the variable. The function is deterministic; which outcome occurred is what was uncertain.

2 · The PMF, and the CDF that never lies

Toss two fair coins and count the heads. Three possible counts, and they are not equally likely. How do you write the whole distribution down in one object?

Two standard answers, and they are two views of the same information. The probability mass function gives the weight of each value directly. The cumulative distribution function gives the total weight at or below each value.

\[ p_X(x) = P(X = x) \qquad\qquad F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \sum_{y \le x} p_X(y) \]

Going from PMF to CDF is running a total. Going back is subtracting neighbouring values: \(p(x) = F(x) - F(x^-)\), the jump at \(x\). The diagrams are complementary — a PMF is a set of spikes, a CDF is a staircase, and the height of each step is the spike.

Mass and accumulation. The PMF places mass at each count. The CDF adds those masses from left to right. Filled dots show that F(x) includes the mass at x.

The CDF has three properties, and they are worth memorising as a checklist because they are what makes it a safer object than the PMF: it is nondecreasing, it is right-continuous (it jumps up at a value, and the value itself belongs to the step above), and its limits are \(0\) at \(-\infty\) and \(1\) at \(+\infty\). The CDF also survives the move to continuous variables in the fifth essay, where the PMF does not — there, the staircase simply becomes a smooth ramp.

Question  2.1 Define the PMF and the CDF of a discrete random variable, and say how they relate.

Answer \(p(x) = P(X = x)\) and \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\); the CDF is the running sum of the PMF.

Question  2.2 Two fair coins, \(X\) = number of heads. What are \(p(1)\) and \(F(1)\)?

Answer \(p(1) = 1/2\) (two of four outcomes) and \(F(1) = 3/4\) (everything up to and including 1).

Question  2.3 Name the three properties of a CDF.

Answer Nondecreasing, right-continuous, with limits 0 at \(-\infty\) and 1 at \(+\infty\).

Question  2.4 Given a CDF, how do you recover the PMF?

Answer Take the jump at each value: \(p(x) = F(x) - F(x^-)\), where \(F(x^-)\) is the limit from the left.

3 · i.i.d.: three letters that assume a lot

A minibatch of 32 images. A sequence of coin tosses. The noise added at each diffusion step. All three are called i.i.d., and the abbreviation is doing two jobs at once.

Independent means the joint PMF factors: \(p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i)\). No one draw tells you anything about another. Identically distributed means they all come from the same PMF. Together: independent and identically distributed.

The independence half is the one that gets quietly dropped, and it is the half that earns its keep. Individually the draws are exchangeable copies; jointly they factorise, which is what turns a product into a sum after you take logarithms, and a sum is what a training loop can average. Without it, the likelihood of a dataset is one giant joint distribution and nothing decomposes.

Question  3.1 What does i.i.d. commit you to, in two parts?

Answer Independence — the joint distribution factorises into a product — and identical distribution, the same PMF for every draw.

Question  3.2 Why does independence matter so much for a training loop?

Answer It factorises the joint likelihood into a product, so the log-likelihood is a sum that can be averaged over a minibatch.

Part II — Five stories with names

4 · Bernoulli: one yes-or-no question

One trial, two outcomes. \(X = 1\) with probability \(p\), \(0\) with probability \(1 - p\). That is the whole distribution, and it is the atom out of which everything else in this essay is assembled.

\[ p_X(1) = p, \quad p_X(0) = 1-p \qquad\qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = p, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = p(1-p) \]

Its most important special case is the indicator of an event, written \(1_A\): the function that is 1 when \(A\) happens and 0 otherwise. Indicators are useful because of one identity, sometimes called the fundamental bridge,

\[ P(A) = \mathbb{E}[1_A] \]

which is obvious once stated — the average of a 0/1 variable is the fraction of ones — and which converts probability questions into addition questions. Every hard expectation computation in the next essay starts by writing the thing you want as a sum of indicators.

With \(p = 0.3\): the mean is 0.3 and the variance is \(0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21\). A variance of 0.21 for a variable that only takes values 0 and 1 sounds large, and it is: the maximum possible variance of a Bernoulli is \(1/4\), attained at \(p = 1/2\).

Question  4.1 What are the mean and variance of a Bernoulli(\(p\)) variable, and where is the variance largest?

Answer Mean \(p\), variance \(p(1-p)\), maximised at \(p = 1/2\) where it is \(1/4\).

Question  4.2 State the fundamental bridge and explain in one line why it holds.

Answer \(P(A) = \mathbb{E}[1_A]\): the average of an indicator is the fraction of trials on which it is 1.

5 · Binomial: n trials, count the successes

A free-throw shooter hits 30% of the time. She takes ten shots. What is the chance she makes exactly three?

The trouble is that “exactly three” is not one sequence of events but many, and they must not be double-counted. Enumerate by pattern: any one specific pattern — say hit, hit, hit, miss, miss, miss, miss, miss, miss, miss — has probability \(0.3^3 \times 0.7^7\), because the shots are independent. Every pattern with three hits has that same probability. So the answer is that number times the number of patterns.

\[ p(k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k} \]

The binomial coefficient is there for exactly one reason: it counts which \(k\) of the \(n\) trials succeeded. The \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\) is the weight of any one of those patterns, and because the trials are independent that weight does not depend on which pattern you picked. This is the multiplication-and-counting habit from the first essay, assembled into a distribution.

For \(n = 10\), \(p = 0.3\), \(k = 3\):

\[ \binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7 = 120 \times 0.027 \times 0.08235 \approx 0.267 \]

The mean is \(np = 3\) and the variance is \(np(1-p) = 2.1\). Note that the mean is not “the most likely value” by definition — here they coincide, but for \(n = 10\), \(p = 0.5\) they do not, and confusing the two is a common slip.

Why the probabilities sum to one, which is a story proof

\(\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k} = \big(p + (1-p)\big)^n = 1\). The left side counts: it is the total probability of everything that can happen, and it must be 1 by the axioms. The right side is the binomial theorem. So the binomial theorem is the statement that the Binomial distribution is a distribution — one identity, proved two ways, and the two ways are “because probabilities sum to one” and “because \((a+b)^n\) expands like that”.

Question  5.1 Why does the Binomial PMF contain the factor \(\binom{n}{k}\)?

Answer It counts which \(k\) of the \(n\) trials succeeded; every such pattern has the same probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\).

Question  5.2 \(n = 10\), \(p = 0.3\). What is \(P(X = 3)\)?

Answer \(\binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7 = 120 \times 0.027 \times 0.08235 \approx 0.267\).

Question  5.3 What are the mean and variance of \(\text{Bin}(10, 0.3)\)?

Answer \(np = 3\) and \(np(1-p) = 2.1\).

6 · Hypergeometric: the same count, without replacement

Twenty elk live in a reserve and five of them have been tagged. You catch four. What is the chance exactly two are tagged?

The algebra is similar to the Binomial and the model is not, and the difference is the whole point of the section. A Binomial requires the trials to be independent, which requires the chance of success to be the same on every draw. Catching an elk and not putting it back changes the composition of the herd, so the draws are dependent. The Binomial is simply the wrong story here.

\[ p(k) = \frac{\binom{5}{k}\binom{15}{4-k}}{\binom{20}{4}} \]

The shape of that formula is worth reading aloud: choose which tagged animals you caught, then which untagged ones, and divide by every possible catch. For \(k = 2\):

\[ \frac{\binom{5}{2}\binom{15}{2}}{\binom{20}{4}} = \frac{10 \times 105}{4845} = \frac{1050}{4845} \approx 0.217 \]

Compare the Binomial story that ignores the tagging, \(\text{Bin}(4, 0.25)\): \(\binom{4}{2}(0.25)^2(0.75)^2 = 6 \times 0.0625 \times 0.5625 \approx 0.211\). Close, and deliberately so. When the catch is a small fraction of the population, removing four elk out of twenty barely shifts the proportions, and the two models converge.

That gives a working rule rather than a formula to memorise: if you are sampling less than about a tenth of the population, sampling without replacement is close enough to sampling with replacement that the Binomial will do. If you are sampling half of it, it will not.

Question  6.1 Write the Hypergeometric PMF for drawing \(k\) successes in \(n\) draws from a population of \(N\) containing \(K\) successes.

Answer \(\binom{K}{k}\binom{N-K}{n-k} \big/ \binom{N}{n}\).

Question  6.2 5 tagged of 20 elk, catch 4. What is \(P(\text{exactly two tagged})\)?

Answer \(10 \times 105 / 4845 \approx 0.217\).

Question  6.3 When may you replace a Hypergeometric with a Binomial, and why?

Answer When the sample is a small fraction of the population — roughly under a tenth — so that removing items barely changes the success proportion and dependence is negligible.

7 · Geometric and Negative Binomial: waiting for something

A coin comes up heads with probability \(p\). How many tosses until the first head?

There is a clean derivation and it is worth having, because it is the template for every “waiting time” argument. To need exactly \(k\) tosses, the first \(k-1\) must all fail and the \(k\)-th must succeed. Those \(k\) events are independent, so multiply.

\[ p(k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \dots \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{p}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{1-p}{p^2} \]

The most useful fact about it is not the PMF but the tail, and the tail has a one-line derivation: “more than \(n\) tosses” happens exactly when the first \(n\) all fail.

\[ P(X > n) = (1-p)^{n} \]

For \(p = 0.2\): \(P(X = 3) = 0.8^2 \times 0.2 = 0.128\), and \(P(X > 5) = 0.8^5 \approx 0.328\). The mean is \(1/0.2 = 5\) tosses, which is a good sanity check — a one-in-five event taking five tries on average is what “on average” ought to mean.

The generalisation is the Negative Binomial: how many trials until the \(r\)-th success? The last trial must be a success, and the preceding \(k-1\) must contain exactly \(r-1\) successes.

\[ p(k) = \binom{k-1}{r-1} p^{r} (1-p)^{k-r}, \qquad k = r, r+1, \dots \]

For \(p = 1/2\), \(r = 3\), \(k = 5\): \(\binom{4}{2}(1/2)^3(1/2)^2 = 6/32 = 0.1875\).

Where the analogy misleads “Waiting for the first head” is not “waiting for two heads in a row”. The geometric distribution applies when the trials are independent and each has the same chance of success, so the state you are in is just “how many failures so far”. For two heads in a row the state includes whether the last toss was a head, the memoryless structure is gone, and the answer is not geometric — the previous essay computed it as six tosses by first-step analysis. Check the independence before reaching for the formula.

Question  7.1 Write the Geometric PMF and derive it in one sentence.

Answer \((1-p)^{k-1}p\): the first \(k-1\) trials must fail and the \(k\)-th must succeed, and by independence the probabilities multiply.

Question  7.2 For a Geometric variable, what is \(P(X > n)\) and why?

Answer \((1-p)^n\): exceeding \(n\) means the first \(n\) trials all failed.

Question  7.3 With \(p = 0.2\), compute \(P(X = 3)\) and \(P(X > 5)\).

Answer \(0.8^2 \times 0.2 = 0.128\) and \(0.8^5 \approx 0.328\).

Question  7.4 Why does the Negative Binomial carry \(\binom{k-1}{r-1}\) rather than \(\binom{k}{r}\)?

Answer The last trial is forced to be the \(r\)-th success, so the remaining \(r-1\) successes are placed among the first \(k-1\) trials.

8 · Poisson: many chances, each unlikely

A call centre gets three calls an hour on average. What is the chance of exactly five?

The honest model is a huge number of tiny independent chances — every instant, any one of many potential callers might dial — and the Binomial cannot be evaluated with \(n\) in the millions and \(p\) in the millionths. The Poisson is what the Binomial becomes in that limit, and that is its justification.

\[ p(k) = e^{-\lambda}\,\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0,1,2,\dots \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda \]

The derivation is worth doing once because it explains the shape. Take \(\text{Bin}(n, \lambda/n)\) and let \(n \to \infty\), holding the mean \(\lambda\) fixed.

\[ \binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{k}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k} = \underbrace{\binom{n}{k}\frac{\lambda^k}{n^k}}_{\to \lambda^k/k!}\; \underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n}}_{\to e^{-\lambda}}\; \underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}}_{\to 1} \]

Three pieces, three limits. The first works because \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)/n^k \to 1\) for fixed \(k\). The second is the standard limit defining \(e^{-\lambda}\). The third is 1 because the exponent is fixed and the base tends to 1. Multiply them and the \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\) falls out.

For \(\lambda = 2\): \(P(X = 3) = e^{-2} \cdot 8/6 \approx 0.1353 \times 1.3333 \approx 0.180\). Check against the Binomial it came from, \(\text{Bin}(100, 0.02)\): \(\binom{100}{3}(0.02)^3(0.98)^{97} = 161{,}700 \times 8\times 10^{-6} \times 0.1409 \approx 0.182\). Two models, two answers, agreeing to two decimal places.

That the mean and variance are both \(\lambda\) is the Poisson's signature and its weakness: real count data almost always has variance larger than its mean, which is called overdispersion and which is the reason the Poisson is a starting point rather than a destination.

Question  8.1 Write the Poisson PMF and give its mean and variance.

Answer \(p(k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\), with mean and variance both equal to \(\lambda\).

Question  8.2 What is the Poisson's generative story?

Answer The limit of \(\text{Bin}(n, \lambda/n)\) as \(n \to \infty\) with the mean held fixed — many independent chances, each individually very unlikely.

Question  8.3 For \(\lambda = 2\), what is \(P(X = 3)\)?

Answer \(e^{-2} \cdot 8/6 \approx 0.180\).

Question  8.4 Which single limit supplies the \(e^{-\lambda}\) in the Poisson PMF?

Answer \((1 - \lambda/n)^n \to e^{-\lambda}\), from the \((1-p)^{n-k}\) factor of the Binomial.

Part III — One table, and the distribution a language model outputs

9 · The six, on one page

The point of naming distributions is not to memorise formulas. It is that a story you can tell in one sentence identifies the distribution, and the distribution gives you the formula, the mean and the variance for free. Tell the story wrong and no amount of algebra will save you.

The habit worth building is the first move of the table: before writing any formula, say the story out loud. “Independent trials, count successes” gets you the Binomial. “Draw without replacement” gets you the Hypergeometric. “Until the first success” gets you the Geometric.

Question  9.1 Which distribution is “trials until the \(r\)-th success”, and what is its mean?

Answer The Negative Binomial, with mean \(r/p\).

Question  9.2 Two distributions in the table have equal mean and variance. Which, and what does that symmetry cost you in practice?

Answer The Poisson, with both equal to \(\lambda\). Real count data is usually overdispersed (variance above the mean), so the Poisson understates the spread.

Question  9.3 What is the one-sentence story that identifies the Hypergeometric rather than the Binomial?

Answer Drawing without replacement — the success probability changes after each draw, so the trials are not independent.

10 · The discrete uniform, and the categorical underneath the softmax

A fair die is the simplest distribution in the world: six values, each with weight \(1/6\). The discrete uniform. Scale it to a vocabulary of 50,000 tokens and give each a different weight, and you have the distribution a language model emits at every step.

\[ \mathrm{Categorical}\big(x; p_1,\dots,p_m\big) = p_x, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i = 1, \quad p_i \ge 0 \]

The categorical is the general form of a single draw from a finite set of labels. The uniform is the special case where every \(p_i = 1/m\). This is not a new object mathematically — it is the PMF of a random variable whose values are not numbers but labels — but it is the most important distribution in this essay, because every token a model ever produces is one draw from one.

Temperature, in numbers

A model produces three logits: \(2, 1, 0\). Softmax exponentiates and renormalises, which gives the categorical probabilities. Dividing the logits by a temperature \(T\) before the softmax reshapes the distribution without changing its support.

Temperature redistributes mass. The three segments represent the same tokens in the same order. Bar lengths use the exact softmax values; displayed probabilities are rounded.

Check one row. At \(T = 0.5\) the rescaled logits are \(4, 2, 0\); exponentiating gives \(54.598\), \(7.389\), \(1.000\); the total is \(62.987\); and \(54.598/62.987 = 0.867\). Same procedure as the untouched row, with the logits doubled by halving the temperature.

Top-\(k\) and top-\(p\) are surgeries on this PMF

Top-\(k\) keeps the \(k\) largest probabilities, sets the rest to zero, and renormalises. With \(k = 2\) at \(T = 1\) the kept weights are \(0.665\) and \(0.245\), summing to \(0.910\), so the renormalised pair is \(0.731\) and \(0.269\). Top-\(p\), or nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds \(p\). At \(p = 0.9\) the cumulative totals run \(0.665\), \(0.910\), so two tokens are kept and the outcome is identical here.

Both moves are the same two operations: truncate the support, then divide by the new total. The second step is not optional. Forget it and your sampler is drawing from a measure that no longer sums to one — which is exactly the conditional-probability normalisation from the previous essay, applied to a set of tokens rather than a set of people.

Question  10.1 How does the categorical distribution relate to the discrete uniform?

Answer The uniform is the special case where every label has probability \(1/m\); the categorical allows arbitrary weights summing to one.

Question  10.2 Logits \((2, 1, 0)\). What is the categorical distribution at \(T = 0.5\)?

Answer Rescale to \((4, 2, 0)\), exponentiate to \((54.598, 7.389, 1)\), normalise to \((0.867, 0.117, 0.016)\).

Question  10.3 What two operations does top-\(k\) sampling perform, and which one is easy to forget?

Answer Truncate to the \(k\) largest weights, then renormalise to sum to one — the renormalisation is the step that is easy to forget.

One genuinely honest note about scope. Of the six distributions in the table of section 9, the Poisson appears nowhere in the four algorithm essays. It is included because it is the canonical rare-event model and because every serious treatment of probability contains it — not because a diffusion model needs it. Saying so is better than manufacturing a connection.

The habit to carry forward is the one this whole essay is built on: a distribution is a story about how the data came to be, and the formula is a summary of the story. When a generative model's loss looks arbitrary, ask what distribution it is the negative log-likelihood of. A Gaussian, a Bernoulli, or a categorical — those three cover almost all of it, and each corresponds to one sentence about how the output was produced.

Question  11.1 What distribution does a language model's final softmax define, and over what support?

Answer A categorical distribution over the vocabulary: one probability per token, summing to one across \(|V|\) entries.

Question  11.2 Why is temperature not a change to the model, but a change to the distribution?

Answer It divides the logits by \(T\) after the network has produced them and before the softmax, so the weights are unchanged and only the shape of the categorical PMF moves.

Question  11.3 A trajectory probability in RLHF is a product of categoricals. Which rule from essay one made that a product?

Answer The multiplication rule: the joint probability of a sequence of actions is the product of the conditional probabilities along the tree.

Question  11.4 Why is a negative log-likelihood loss usually a Gaussian, Bernoulli or categorical loss in disguise?

Answer Because each is \(-\log p(y \mid x)\) for one of those three output distributions: squared error for a Gaussian, binary cross-entropy for a Bernoulli, softmax cross-entropy for a categorical.
