Densities, bell curves,
and the noise
we add on purpose

Why a density is not a probability, why there is only one bell curve, why the bus never remembers how long you have waited, and why a variational autoencoder writes its random sample as a formula instead of drawing it

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the fifth of seven essays on probability, and the first that needs calculus. It needs the third and fourth — random variables, and expectation — and it is the essay that makes diffusion legible, because diffusion is a story about Gaussian noise added a thousand times and removed a thousand times.

Part I — A density is not a probability

1 · A spinner, and a probability of zero

A spinner is a dial with a needle that stops uniformly anywhere on the circle. What is the chance it stops exactly on the mark at the top?

Zero. Not small — zero. There are uncountably many places it could stop, each as likely as the next, and a millionth of the circle is not a number you can assign to a single point without the total becoming infinite. Yet the chance it stops in the top quarter is \(1/4\), which is not zero.

The resolution is that probability is now an area, not a height. The object that gives heights is the probability density function, and its height at a point is not the probability of that point. It is the rate at which probability accumulates there.

\[ P(X = x) = 0 \quad\text{for every } x, \qquad P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \] 
  Uniform(0,1):  f(x) = 1 on [0,1], 0 elsewhere

  f
  1 |  +---------------+          area under the whole thing = 1
    |  |               |
    |  |               |          P(0.25 <= X <= 0.5)
    |  |   ####        |          = width 0.25 x height 1
    +--+---####--------+---- x    = 0.25
       0   0.25  0.5   1

  any single point is a line of zero width, hence zero area
Area, not height. A density may exceed 1 without anything breaking, because it is a rate; only the integral has to be a probability.

Every consequence of the previous essays survives, with sums replaced by integrals. The density is non-negative and integrates to one. Averages become integrals. And the phrase “with probability one” enters the language, because a continuous variable can fail to take any particular value yet still have to take some value.

Question  1.1 For a continuous random variable, what is \(P(X = x)\)?

Answer Zero, for every \(x\): probability lives in intervals, not points.

Question  1.2 A density has height 3 at some point. Is the probability of that point 3?

Answer No. The density is a rate of accumulation; only its integral over an interval is a probability.

Question  1.3 What two conditions must a density satisfy?

Answer \(f(x) \ge 0\) everywhere, and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\).

2 · From density to probability, and back

Two functions carry the whole distribution, and each is the other's calculus partner.

\[ F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(u)\,du \qquad\qquad f(x) = F'(x) \]

The CDF is the running total, so the density is its slope. That sentence replaces the staircase of the discrete case with a ramp: where the density is high, the CDF climbs steeply; where the density is zero, the CDF is flat.

Take \(f(x) = 2x\) on \([0,1]\) and zero elsewhere. Check it is a density: the integral is \(x^2\) from 0 to 1, which is 1. The CDF is \(F(x) = x^2\) on that interval. Then

\[ P(0.3 < X < 0.6) = F(0.6) - F(0.3) = 0.36 - 0.09 = 0.27 \]

Note the density exceeding 1 does not happen here, but it does in the next section, and it is worth pre-empting the objection now. A density of 2 on a support of width \(1/2\) integrates to 1 perfectly well. Height is not probability, and a density above 1 is not an error — it just means probability is being laid down faster than one unit per unit of \(x\).

Question  2.1 How do the CDF and the density relate?

Answer \(F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(u)\,du\) and \(f(x) = F'(x)\) — the density is the slope of the CDF.

Question  2.2 With \(f(x) = 2x\) on \([0,1]\), compute \(P(0.3 < X < 0.6)\).

Answer \(0.6^2 - 0.3^2 = 0.36 - 0.09 = 0.27\).

Question  2.3 Can a density exceed 1? Give a case.

Answer Yes. \(\mathrm{Unif}(0, 0.5)\) has density 2 on its support, which integrates to 1 over a width of one half.

Question  2.4 Why is \(P(a \le X \le b) = P(a < X < b)\) for a continuous variable?

Answer Because the endpoints are single points and each carries probability zero, so including or excluding them changes nothing.

3 · The uniform, and why it needs the axioms

The simplest continuous distribution is the one the spinner gave us: constant density on an interval.

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a} \text{ on } (a,b) \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} \]

On \((0,1)\) the mean is \(0.5\) and the variance is \(1/12 \approx 0.0833\). That \(\sqrt{1/12} \approx 0.289\) as a standard deviation is worth remembering, because it is the noise floor of every uniform random number generator and every quantization error you will ever analyse.

The uniform is also the reason the axioms of the first essay had to be strengthened. The naive definition of probability counted outcomes, and there are uncountably many points in \([0,1]\); no counting argument can produce this distribution. Countable additivity can, and this is precisely what it was for.

Question  3.1 What are the mean and variance of \(\mathrm{Unif}(a,b)\)?

Answer Mean \((a+b)/2\), variance \((b-a)^2/12\).

Question  3.2 Why can the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) not be justified by the naive definition?

Answer Because the interval is uncountable, so there is no finite sample space to count; only the axioms of countable additivity can assign it probabilities.

Part II — Three curves with names

4 · The normal, and the fact that there is only one

Measure anything with many small independent errors added together — a person's height, a manufactured part's diameter, the position of a pollen grain in water — and the histogram keeps coming out the same shape. Symmetric, peaked at the centre, tails falling off fast.

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

That shape is a family with two dials: a location \(\mu\) and a width \(\sigma\). And the first surprising fact is that there is only one bell curve. Every normal distribution is the standard one, stretched and slid. The change of variables

\[ z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x = \mu + \sigma z \]

is called standardisation, and it is used so often that the standard normal has its own table. If \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 0.5^2)\) and \(x = 2.6\), then \(z = (2.6-2)/0.5 = 1.2\), and \(P(X > 2.6) = P(Z > 1.2) \approx 0.115\). One table answers every question about every normal distribution, because there is only one curve.

The three numbers worth memorising

Within one standard deviation of the mean sits \(68.27\%\); within two, \(95.45\%\); within three, \(99.73\%\). Those three numbers explain a large fraction of the folklore about outliers, and the last one explains why a four-sigma event is newsworthy rather than impossible.

Why this shape and not another? Two answers, one shallow and one deep. The shallow one is the central limit theorem, in the seventh essay: sums of many independent small errors are approximately normal, whatever the errors were. The deep one is that among all distributions with a given variance, the normal is the one with the largest entropy — the least opinionated choice you can make when you know only a mean and a spread. That is why a Gaussian prior is the default everywhere in machine learning.

Where this breaks “Normally distributed” is an assumption, and the tails are where it fails. Financial returns, file sizes, and the time between earthquakes are routinely modelled as normal and are not: their extreme events are far more common than \(99.73\%\) within three sigma suggests. The bell curve is the maximum-entropy answer, which means it is what you should assume when you know nothing but a mean and a variance, and exactly what you should stop assuming the moment you have seen the data.

Question  4.1 Write the standardisation formula and say what it buys you.

Answer \(z = (x-\mu)/\sigma\); it converts any normal into the standard normal, so one table serves every normal distribution.

Question  4.2 \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 0.5^2)\). Standardise \(x = 2.6\).

Answer \(z = (2.6 - 2)/0.5 = 1.2\).

Question  4.3 What fraction of a normal distribution lies within one, two and three standard deviations?

Answer \(68.27\%\), \(95.45\%\) and \(99.73\%\).

Question  4.4 Given \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), how do you build a \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) variable?

Answer \(X = \mu + \sigma Z\) — the location-scale construction.

5 · The exponential, and the memoryless bus

A bus arrives on average every ten minutes, at no fixed schedule. You have already waited seven. Are you due?

No, and the mathematics is emphatic about it. Waiting time for a memoryless process has the exponential distribution,

\[ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \ge 0 \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \]

and its defining property is that the past is irrelevant:

\[ P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t) \]

Here is why it holds, in two lines. The tail is \(P(X > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). So

\[ P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t) \]

The \(e^{-\lambda s}\) cancels. That cancellation is memorylessness, and the exponential is the only continuous distribution that has it.

With \(\lambda = 0.5\) — a mean wait of two minutes — the chance the next bus is more than three minutes away is \(e^{-1.5} \approx 0.223\). And if you have already waited four minutes, the chance of waiting three more is still \(0.223\). You are not due. The bus has not been keeping count, because the process that generates arrivals does not have a memory to keep count in.

The discrete counterpart is the Geometric distribution from the third essay, and the correspondence is exact: \(P(X > n) = (1-p)^n\) there, \(e^{-\lambda t}\) here. Both say that the future is independent of the past, one in ticks and one in continuous time.

Question  5.1 Write the exponential density and state its mean and variance in terms of \(\lambda\).

Answer \(f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x \ge 0\); mean \(1/\lambda\), variance \(1/\lambda^2\).

Question  5.2 State memorylessness as an equation.

Answer \(P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\) for all \(s, t \ge 0\).

Question  5.3 With \(\lambda = 0.5\), what is \(P(X > 3)\), and what is \(P(X > 7 \mid X > 4)\)?

Answer Both are \(e^{-1.5} \approx 0.223\), which is the point of memorylessness.

Question  5.4 Which discrete distribution is the exponential's counterpart, and what is the shared property?

Answer The Geometric; both are memoryless, with \(P(X>n) = (1-p)^n\) and \(P(X>t) = e^{-\lambda t}\).

6 · Symmetry, and the ties that never happen

Two people draw independently from the same continuous distribution. Who is more likely to draw the larger value?

Neither, and the reason is worth more than the answer. By symmetry the two are equally likely to be larger, and by continuity the chance of an exact tie is zero, so the two cases exhaust the probability and split it evenly:

\[ P(X < Y) = P(X > Y) = \tfrac12, \qquad P(X = Y) = 0 \]

This is the i.i.d. symmetry of §5.7, and it is a workhorse. It says that the ordering of independent continuous draws is uniformly random, which is why the minimum and maximum of a sample are clean objects and why so many algorithm analyses start with “pick a random element, by symmetry it is equally likely to be any of them”.

Extend it to three: \(P(X_1 \text{ is the smallest}) = 1/3\), since all six orderings are equally likely and two of them have \(X_1\) smallest. With \(n\) draws, each has probability \(1/n\) of being the maximum.

The “ties never happen” clause looks like a technicality and is not. The moment you discretise — round the numbers, bucket them — ties acquire positive probability and every such argument needs a tie-breaking rule. That is one reason quantisation is treated as a separate error from sampling.

Question  6.1 For i.i.d. continuous \(X, Y\), what is \(P(X < Y)\) and why?

Answer \(1/2\), by symmetry, with \(P(X = Y) = 0\) so the two strict orderings exhaust the probability.

Question  6.2 Why is the probability of a tie zero, and when does that stop being true?

Answer Single points carry probability zero for a continuous variable; discretising or rounding gives ties positive probability.

Question  6.3 Three i.i.d. continuous draws. What is the probability that \(X_1\) is the smallest?

Answer \(1/3\) — all six orderings are equally likely.

Part III — Getting numbers out of a distribution

7 · Universality of the uniform

A computer gives you one thing for free: a uniform number in \([0,1]\). How does that become a draw from an exponential, or a normal, or anything else?

The answer is the most useful theorem in simulation, and it is called the universality of the uniform. Let \(U \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) and let \(F\) be any CDF with an inverse. Then

\[ X = F^{-1}(U) \quad\text{has CDF } F \]

The proof is two lines and reads itself:

\[ P(X \le x) = P\big(F^{-1}(U) \le x\big) = P\big(U \le F(x)\big) = F(x) \]

where the last step uses that \(U\) is uniform, so the probability of falling below a number is that number. The picture is a horizontal line at height \(u\) cutting the CDF and dropping down to the axis: the inverse CDF turns a uniform height into the quantile at that height.

  F(x)
   1 |            +------------
     |        +---+
   u |    +---+      <- draw u uniformly on the vertical axis
     |  +-+ |
     +--+---+-+--------- x
            ^
            x = F^{-1}(u)

  uniform in height  ->  correct density in x
Inverse-CDF sampling. Draw a height uniformly, then read across to the CDF and down. Where the CDF is steep, more height maps to the same width, so the density is high there.

For the exponential, \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\), so \(F^{-1}(u) = -\ln(1-u)/\lambda\). Since \(1 - U\) is also uniform on \((0,1)\), the practical form is

\[ X = -\frac{\ln U}{\lambda} \]

With \(\lambda = 2\) and \(U = 0.3\): \(X = -\ln(0.3)/2 = 1.204/2 = 0.602\). Check it landed correctly by putting it back through the CDF: \(F(0.602) = 1 - e^{-1.204} = 1 - 0.3 = 0.7\), which is \(1 - U\). The one-line inverse maps your uniform \(u\) to the quantile at \(1-u\), which is still uniform, so the sampler is right.

This is exactly how a categorical distribution is sampled too: draw \(U\), walk the cumulative probabilities until you pass \(U\), and emit that label. The softmax's cumulative sum is the CDF, and sampling a token is inverse-CDF sampling with the support being a finite list instead of an interval.

Beyond the book — not in §5.3.

The Gumbel-max trick is the same idea exploited differently: add independent Gumbel noise to each logit and take the argmax, and the result is exactly a draw from the softmax. It replaces a sequential scan of the cumulative sum with a fully parallel argmax, which matters when the support has fifty thousand entries and the sampler runs on a GPU. Same distribution, different arithmetic.

Question  7.1 State the universality of the uniform, and give the one-line proof.

Answer If \(U \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) then \(F^{-1}(U)\) has CDF \(F\), because \(P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)\).

Question  7.2 What is the inverse-CDF formula for the exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda\)?

Answer \(X = -\ln(U)/\lambda\), using that \(1-U\) is also uniform.

Question  7.3 \(\lambda = 2\), \(U = 0.3\). What is the sample, and how do you check it?

Answer \(-\ln(0.3)/2 \approx 0.602\); feeding it back gives \(F(0.602) = 1 - e^{-1.204} = 0.7 = 1-U\).

Question  7.4 How does inverse-CDF sampling produce a token from a language model?

Answer Draw \(U\) uniformly, walk the cumulative probabilities of the softmax output until you pass \(U\), and emit that token — the same construction with a finite support.

8 · Reparameterization: the sample is a function of the noise

You want to train a network that outputs a distribution over \(z\), and you want gradients to flow back through a sample from it. There are two ways to write the sample down, and only one of them survives backpropagation.

Way one: draw \(z\) from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The draw is a black box; you cannot differentiate it with respect to \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\), because they were arguments to a random number generator rather than inputs to a formula.

Way two: draw \(\varepsilon\) from the fixed standard normal, then compute

\[ z = \mu + \sigma\,\varepsilon \]

and now \(z\) is an ordinary differentiable function of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). Nothing about the distribution changed — \(z\) is still \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), because that is what adding a location and scaling a spread does to a normal. What changed is that the randomness has been moved outside the parameters.

The gradients are then one line each: \(\partial z/\partial \mu = 1\) and \(\partial z/\partial \sigma = \varepsilon\). With \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.5\), \(\varepsilon = -1.2\), the sample is \(z = 1 + 0.5(-1.2) = 0.4\), and the two partials are 1 and \(-1.2\).

The general name for this is the location-scale family, and the normal is the family member that makes it exact rather than approximate. A uniform could be reparameterized as \(a + (b-a)U\) just as easily. An exponential can be written \(-\ln(U)/\lambda\) as in the last section, which is the same move in different clothes. What cannot be reparameterized so cheaply is a discrete distribution — you cannot write a categorical draw as a differentiable function of its probabilities without adding a relaxation, and that is precisely why discrete latent variables are awkward.

  score function                    reparameterization
  ------------------------------    ------------------------------
  z ~ N(mu, sigma^2)                eps ~ N(0, 1)
                                    z = mu + sigma * eps
  dz/dmu  : not available           dz/dmu  = 1
  dz/dsigma: not available          dz/dsigma = eps

  unbiased, high variance           unbiased, low variance
Two estimators, one distribution. Both give the same expected value; the right-hand column has orders of magnitude less noise, which is the whole reason variational autoencoders train at all.

Question  8.1 If \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\), what is the distribution of \(z\)?

Answer \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) — the location-scale family.

Question  8.2 \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.5\), \(\varepsilon = -1.2\). What is \(z\)?

Answer \(1 + 0.5(-1.2) = 0.4\).

Question  8.3 Why does the reparameterized form admit gradients when a direct draw does not?

Answer Because \(z\) becomes an ordinary differentiable expression in \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), with the randomness in an independent \(\varepsilon\) outside the parameters.

Question  8.4 Which kind of distribution resists reparameterization, and what does that force you to do?

Answer Discrete ones such as the categorical; you must use a relaxation (or the score-function estimator) instead of an exact differentiable transform.

Part IV — Where this lands in generative AI

9 · The Gaussian at the centre of everything

Diffusion models add Gaussian noise to an image a thousand times and then learn to remove it. Variational autoencoders push data into a Gaussian latent space and sample from it. Both are built on the three facts of this essay: a density is not a probability, there is only one bell curve, and a random sample can be written as a differentiable formula.

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

That is the forward diffusion process in one line, and it is reparameterization applied to a Gaussian: a deterministic scaling of the signal plus a deterministic scaling of standard noise. Because it is written this way, it is differentiable in \(x_0\), and because the noise is standard, the same \(\varepsilon\) can be reused as a regression target. The whole "predict the noise" training objective depends on being able to write the sample as a formula rather than as a black-box draw.

One honest caveat about the Gaussian's prominence. It is there because it is the maximum entropy choice given a mean and a variance, and because it is closed under the operations the models need — sums, conditioning, and affine maps. It is not there because data is Gaussian; images are emphatically not. The Gaussian enters generative modelling as a convenient prior and a convenient noise, not as a claim about the world. Where the claim is made — a VAE decoder that models pixels with a fixed-variance Gaussian — it is the weakest part of the model, and that is why discrete or learned output distributions are preferred when the data allows them.

Question  9.1 Why is the isotropic Gaussian prior what makes the VAE's KL term a sum over coordinates?

Answer Because \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) factorises across coordinates, so the divergence separates into one independent term per latent dimension.

Question  9.2 Write the diffusion forward process and say which construction from this essay it is.

Answer \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) — reparameterization of a Gaussian, a scaled signal plus scaled standard noise.

Question  9.3 Why does diffusion's training objective depend on the sample being a formula rather than a black-box draw?

Answer Because the same \(\varepsilon\) can then serve as a known regression target, which turns the bound into a supervised denoising problem.

Question  9.4 Why is a Gaussian a reasonable prior but a poor model for images?

Answer It is the maximum-entropy choice given a mean and variance and is closed under the needed operations, but it is not what image pixels look like; a fixed-variance Gaussian decoder is usually the weakest part of a VAE.
