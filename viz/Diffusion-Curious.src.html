Diffusion models,
for the very curious

How to generate an image by learning to undo a thousand small acts of vandalism — and why the whole thing is a VAE that has been stretched very, very long

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

Say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. Each box is numbered, the questions are collected at the end, and the same list ships as a plain-text file. On screen, each Part ends with a deck that deals its cards to you one at a time.

Everything here follows pages 72–110 of an IIT Madras generative-AI course. A few short passages are marked beyond the notes: those go further than the pages do. The plan: define the model, write its bound, discover that the bound collapses into a regression, then notice that the same regression has three different readings — which is where most of the field's vocabulary comes from.

Part I — A VAE stretched over time

1 · The model, and what it borrows

Imagine a machine that can answer only one question: given this damaged photograph, what was the damage? That is the entire training task. You take a picture, vandalise it in a way you choose and therefore know, and ask the network to name the vandalism. Do that at a thousand levels of damage, and something surprising becomes possible — because undoing damage in small steps, a thousand times over, turns out to be a way of inventing a photograph out of nothing.

Recall the shape of a VAE: data \(x\) goes through an encoder to a latent \(z\), and a decoder brings it back. One hidden layer, one bottleneck. A hierarchical VAE stacks several: \(x \to z_1 \to z_2 \to \dots \to z_T\) going up, and back down again.

A denoising diffusion probabilistic model is a hierarchical VAE with three specific commitments:

(a) many latent spaces, indexed by \(t = 1, \dots, T\), with \(T\) around a thousand; (b) every latent has the same dimension as the data — no bottleneck at all, \(\dim(x_t) = \dim(x_0)\); and (c) the encoder is fixed and not learned, so only the decoder carries parameters.

That third commitment is the one that makes the method work. In a VAE you must learn where to put the latent space. Here the forward direction is a fixed rule for adding noise, so there is nothing to learn about it and nothing to get wrong. All of the learning is in going backwards.

Question  1.1 List the three ways a DDPM differs from a plain VAE.

Answer It has many latent variables rather than one; each latent has the same dimensionality as the data, so there is no compression; and the encoding distribution is fixed and non-learnable, so only the decoding direction is learned.

Question  1.2 In the diffusion literature, what do \(x_0\) and \(x_1, \dots, x_T\) denote?

Answer \(x_0\) is the clean data point. \(x_1 \dots x_T\) are the latent vectors obtained from it by progressive noising — not dataset indices, which is the notation an earlier essay in this series would have led you to expect.

Part II — Adding noise on a schedule

2 · The forward process

Photocopy a photograph. Copy the copy. Copy that. Long before the thousandth generation you stop seeing a picture and see only grain. The interesting part is that the grain is not noise bolted on at the end — it is the accumulated result of a schedule someone chose in advance. Diffusion fixes that schedule, runs it forward without learning anything at all, and spends every parameter on the reverse.

The forward process is one line:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Read it as a weighted average of signal and noise, with the weights chosen so the overall scale stays put. With \(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_T\) fixed in \([0,1]\), \(x_0\) starts as an image, \(x_1\) is a noisy image, and by \(x_T\) essentially every trace of the original has been replaced. This is a first-order Markov chain, so the conditional is Gaussian and known:

\[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_t;\, \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\; (1-\alpha_t)I\right) \]

3 · The jump-to-any-time formula

You never have to make those copies. Each step is a fixed weighted average plus independent Gaussian noise, and a sum of independent Gaussians is itself Gaussian — so there is a closed form that takes you straight from the original to the thousandth generation. That one line is why training is cheap. One data point, one random noise level, one exact sample, instead of a thousand-step simulation for every training example.

Unrolling that recursion one step at a time is wasteful, and it turns out you never have to. Substitute twice:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\epsilon'\right) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\epsilon \]

The two noise terms combine, because a linear combination of independent Gaussians is Gaussian with the variances added. Keep going and the product of the \(\alpha\) values accumulates into a single symbol:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\, \epsilon, \qquad \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{i=1}^{t} \alpha_i \]

so that

\[ q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0,\; (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)I\right) \]

This is the single most useful line in the subject. It says you can jump from a clean image to its state at step \(t\) in one shot, in closed form, without simulating the chain. Every training step below depends on it.

Question  2.1 Why is \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\) Gaussian, and what are its parameters?

Answer Because the forward step is \(\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}\) plus Gaussian noise, and adding a constant-scaled Gaussian keeps it Gaussian. The mean is \(\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}\) and the covariance is \((1-\alpha_t)I\).

Question  2.2 Derive, in outline, why \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) has the closed form it does.

Answer Unroll the recursion: each substitution multiplies the previous signal by another \(\sqrt{\alpha}\) and adds independent Gaussian noise. Products of independent Gaussians are Gaussian with added variances, so the accumulated signal coefficient is \(\sqrt{\prod_i \alpha_i} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\) and the accumulated noise variance is \(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t\).

Question  2.3 What is \(q(x_T)\) in the limit, and why does that matter for generation?

Answer With the schedule chosen so \(\bar{\alpha}_T \to 0\), \(x_T\) is essentially pure noise, distributed as \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). That is the stationary distribution of the chain, and it is what makes generation possible: sampling can start from the prior, which you already know how to sample.

Part III — Learning to walk back

4 · The reverse direction is the model

It is worth being clear about what the network is not. It is not a picture-making machine. At each step it estimates two things: the average of the slightly-less- damaged image, and how much uncertainty to leave in. That is all. Pictures emerge because those estimates are composed a thousand times, not because anything at any single step understood the picture.

The model is defined in the reverse direction, as a Markov chain of Gaussians with learned parameters:

\[ P_\theta(x_{0:T}) = P(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} P_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t), \qquad P_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t)\right) \]

The joint over all latents factorises because we assumed the reverse chain is first-order Markov with Gaussian steps. That assumption is a modelling choice sitting exactly where the VAE put its decoder: a family of distributions we are willing to consider. The parameters \(\mu_\theta\) and \(\Sigma_\theta\) are what the network will produce.

Question  3.1 What justifies writing \(P_\theta\) as a product of Gaussians?

Answer Nothing deeper than a modelling assumption: we choose the reverse process to be a first-order Markov chain with Gaussian transitions, and put neural networks on the means and covariances. The factorisation is a definition, not a theorem.

Part IV — The bound splits into three

5 · The ELBO, algebraically

A thousand-step process sounds like a thousand-term loss. It collapses into three jobs. The last step must reproduce the data. The first must land on pure noise, which is the one distribution you already know how to sample. Every step in between must undo exactly one step of the vandalism. Only the middle job trains anything, and only the middle job is hard.

Same bound as the VAE, now with a whole chain of latents inside the expectation:

\[ J_\theta(q) = \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)} \log\frac{P_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)} \]

Substitute both factorisations — the numerator from Part III, the denominator from the Markov forward process. Then peel off the \(t=1\) term to make the sums line up, and apply Bayes' rule to \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\), which under the chain equals \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0)\):

\[ q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)} \]

When you multiply that over \(t\), the \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) denominators and numerators telescope — the \(q(x_2\mid x_0)\) produced at one step cancels against the one consumed at the next — and everything survives except the two ends. What is left rearranges into three named pieces:

\[ J_\theta(q) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\log P_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{KL}\left(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,P(x_T)\right)}_{\text{prior matching}} - \underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q(x_t\mid x_0)}\left[D_{KL}\left(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,P_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\right)\right]}_{\text{denoising matching}} \]

Three terms, three jobs. The first asks whether the last step reconstructs the data. The second asks whether the noised-to-death distribution matches the prior we sample from — and it contains no \(\theta\), so it is a constant we can ignore. The third is where all the learning happens: at every noise level, compare the true reverse step with the model's reverse step.

Question  4.1 Name the three terms of the DDPM objective.

Answer Reconstruction, \(\mathbb{E}_{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\log P_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)\); prior matching, \(D_{KL}(q(x_T\mid x_0)\|P(x_T))\), which has no \(\theta\) and is dropped; and denoising matching over \(t = 2\dots T\), the only term that trains the network.

Question  4.2 Where does the telescoping happen, and what does it buy?

Answer Applying Bayes' rule to the forward conditional produces a \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)\) in the numerator and a \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) in the denominator. Across the product over \(t\) every intermediate term cancels, leaving only \(q(x_1\mid x_0)\) and \(q(x_T\mid x_0)\). Those two ends are what become the reconstruction and prior-matching terms.

Part V — The hard term becomes a regression

6 · Everything in the KL is now computable

The denoising-matching KL compares two things. The model's side, \(P_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\), is ours to choose. The other side, \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\), looks like a monster, but with all the Gaussian ingredients from Parts II and III it has a closed form. Completing the square gives

\[ q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_{t-1}; \mu_q(x_t,x_0), \Sigma_q\right) \]

with

\[ \mu_q = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})x_t + \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}(1-\alpha_t)x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}, \qquad \Sigma_q = \frac{(1-\alpha_t)(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} I \]

Now choose the model's covariance to be the same \(\Sigma_q\). A KL between two Gaussians with identical covariance collapses to a squared distance between their means — the trace and log-determinant terms cancel — leaving

\[ D_{KL} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_q^2}\left\|\mu_\theta(x_t) - \mu_q(x_t,x_0)\right\|_2^2 \]

That is the pivot of the whole essay. A divergence between distributions has become a mean-squared error between two vectors. The target, \(\mu_q\), is computable from \(x_t\) and \(x_0\). The prediction, \(\mu_\theta\), comes from a network. Put a U-net in that slot and you have a trainable objective.

Question  5.1 Why does the KL between these two Gaussians reduce to a squared error on the means?

Answer Because we set \(\Sigma_\theta = \Sigma_q\). The log-determinant ratio is zero and the trace of \(\Sigma_q^{-1}\Sigma_q\) is just the dimension, so both constant terms drop and only \((\mu_\theta-\mu_q)^T\Sigma_q^{-1}(\mu_\theta-\mu_q)\) survives — which for a scalar covariance is \(\|\mu_\theta-\mu_q\|_2^2\) up to the factor \(1/2\sigma_q^2\).

Question  5.2 What is the network asked to predict, in this formulation?

Answer The mean of the reverse step, \(\mu_\theta(x_t)\). But since \(\mu_q\) is affine in \(x_0\) and \(\mu_\theta\) is forced to share that affine form with \(\hat{x}_\theta\) in place of \(x_0\), predicting the mean is equivalent to predicting the clean image \(x_0\) — hence a denoiser.

Learning the reverse covariance

Beyond the notes — not in pages 72–110.

We sidestepped something. Setting the reverse covariance equal to the forward one is a choice, and it is the reason the divergence collapsed into a regression on means — matching covariances is what makes the other terms cancel. Learn the covariance as well and the bound tightens, at the cost of a second output head and a much fussier optimisation. In practice most systems keep the fixed covariance and buy their quality with reweighting instead. Fewer moving parts, better results — a recurring pattern in this subject.

Part VI — One network, three descriptions

You can describe a hillside by its height at each point, by its slope, or by the direction water would run down it. All three carry the same information and any one of them gives you the others. The diffusion network is trained on only one of these — the noise — yet it turns out to have learned all three. That is why three separate literatures, with three vocabularies, can each look at this object and correctly claim it as their own.

7 · Predicting \(x_0\), noise, or score

Because \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon\) is invertible for \(x_0\), every one of these targets determines the others:

the network outputshow you get therewhat it means
\(\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)\)directpredict the clean image
\(\hat{\epsilon}_\theta(x_t)\)solve for \(\epsilon\)predict the noise that was added
\(S_\theta(x_t)\)divide by \(-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\)predict the score, \(\nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t)\)

The third is the one worth understanding. Tweedie's formula relates the mean of a noisy observation to its score, and applying it here gives \(\nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t) = -\epsilon_t/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\). The true score is the added noise, negatively scaled. So a network trained to regress on noise is, without anyone arranging it, learning the score function of the data distribution. That is why the diffusion and score-based literatures are the same subject wearing different clothes.

Question  6.1 Why are "predict the noise" and "predict the score" the same task?

Answer Because \(\nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t) = -\epsilon_t/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\): the score is a fixed negative rescaling of the noise. Regressing on \(\epsilon_t\) and regressing on the score differ only by a known positive factor, so they have the same minimiser.

Question  6.2 What does the network see besides \(x_t\), and why is that necessary?

Answer The time step \(t\), encoded as a sinusoidal positional embedding. One network is shared across all noise levels, so without knowing \(t\) it could not tell a barely-noised image from one that is almost pure noise, and the optimal denoiser differs enormously between those cases.

Part VII — Training it and sampling from it

8 · Two loops

Training is almost embarrassingly simple, and the reason is the closed form for \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\):

\[ \text{repeat: } t \sim \text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,T\}; \quad x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon; \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \beta\nabla_\theta\left\|\hat{x}_\theta(x_t) - x_0\right\|_2^2 \]

Take a data point, pick a random noise level, noise the point directly to that level, ask the network to undo it, and step against the squared error. No adversary, no ELBO estimate, no sampling of a chain.

Sampling is the expensive half. Start from \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and walk down one step at a time, each step drawing from \(P_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\), which means one forward pass through the network per step — a thousand of them for a thousand-step model. Each step is a mean from the network plus fresh noise of size \(\sigma_q\):

\[ x_{t-1} = \mu_{\theta^*}(x_t) + \sigma_q\,\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I) \]

Question  7.1 Why can training noise a single data point all the way to step \(t\) in one go?

Answer Because \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) is Gaussian with known parameters, so \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon\) produces an exact sample from it. There is no need to simulate the intermediate steps during training, which is what makes each iteration cheap.

Question  7.2 Why is sampling a thousand times more expensive than one training step?

Answer Training needs one forward pass per iteration regardless of \(T\). Sampling needs a full pass at every step of the reverse chain, from \(t=T\) down to \(t=1\) — so \(T\) passes for a single generated sample. That asymmetry is precisely what DDIM later attacks.

Reweighting the loss

Beyond the notes — not in pages 72–110.

The notes keep the coefficient the derivation gives them in front of every term. That coefficient is large at low noise and small at high noise, so the plain objective spends most of its attention on the easy, nearly-clean images and almost none on the hard, nearly-random ones — exactly backwards. Delete it. Weight every noise level equally and the network has to work hardest where the structure is actually decided. That deletion, of a coefficient the mathematics told you to keep, is most of the difference between a diffusion model that trains and one that produces good pictures.

Part VIII — Steering it, shrinking it, speeding it up

9 · Conditional generation

Think of two voices in the network’s head. One knows what pictures look like in general; the other knows what a picture of this particular thing looks like. Asking for an image of a dog is asking for the difference between them — the direction that turns “a picture” into “a picture of a dog”. Guidance is what happens when you stop merely following that difference and start amplifying it with a dial, buying fidelity to the prompt with variety.

Suppose you want \(P(x_0\mid y)\) rather than \(P(x_0)\) — an image given a label or a text embedding. Since the network is a score predictor, the honest way to ask is: what is the conditional score? Bayes' rule splits it:

\[ \nabla\log P(x_t\mid y) = \nabla\log P(x_t) + \nabla\log P(y\mid x_t) \]

the \(\log P(y)\) term vanishing because it has no \(x_t\) in it. So: take the unconditional score the diffusion model already gives you, and add the gradient of a classifier's log-probability. That is classifier guidance — and its weakness is in the recipe, since the classifier has to be trained to work on inputs at every noise level, which is unpleasant.

Classifier-free guidance removes the classifier by rewriting \(\nabla\log P(y\mid x_t) = \nabla\log P(x_t\mid y) - \nabla\log P(x_t)\) and then interpolating between the two ends with a knob \(\lambda\):

\[ \nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t\mid y) = \lambda \,\nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t\mid y) + (1-\lambda)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log P(x_t) \]

You train one network to do both jobs by dropping the condition on some fraction of examples (a "null" label), and at sampling time you evaluate it twice and combine. Large \(\lambda\) buys fidelity to the prompt at the cost of variety.

Question  8.1 Where does the classifier's gradient come from in classifier guidance?

Answer From Bayes' rule: \(\nabla\log P(x_t\mid y) = \nabla\log P(x_t) + \nabla\log P(y\mid x_t)\), with the \(\P(y)\) term dropping out. The diffusion model supplies \(\nabla\log P(x_t)\) and a separately trained classifier supplies \(\nabla\log P(y\mid x_t)\).

Question  8.2 What problem does classifier-free guidance solve, and at what extra cost?

Answer It removes the need for a separate classifier that must be robust at every noise level — instead one model is trained with the condition randomly dropped so it can also emit the unconditional score. The cost is two forward passes per sampling step instead of one.

10 · Latent diffusion

Diffusing in pixel space is wasteful, because most of an image's bits describe detail nobody asked for. The fix is architectural: train an autoencoder first, then run the entire diffusion process in its latent space. Encode \(x_0\) to \(z_0\), diffuse over \(z_0\), and at the end decode a sample with the autoencoder. The diffusion model never sees a pixel. VQ-VAE is the encoder these notes suggest.

Question  8.3 What exactly does a latent diffusion model diffuse over, and who turns it back into pixels?

Answer It diffuses over the latent code \(z_0 = E_{\phi^*}(x_0)\) produced by a pretrained autoencoder. Generation samples \(z_{novel}\) by reverse diffusion and then applies the decoder, \(x_{novel} = D_{\theta^*}(z_{novel})\).

11 · DDIM: same training, better sampling

If the trained network always points in the direction of less noise, you do not have to walk a thousand tiny steps. You can take a dozen long ones along the same path. That is DDIM: the training is untouched, only the stride changes. And once the strides are deterministic rather than random, the path can be walked in reverse — which is how you recover the noise that would have produced a given image.

Two complaints about DDPM sit side by side: a thousand steps per sample is slow, and the forward process is stochastic, so a given \(x_0\) has no single latent \(x_T\) — which kills any hope of inverting an image to its noise.

DDIM defines a family of inference distributions indexed by \(\sigma \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), with a forward process that is deliberately non-Markovian. The crucial observation is that the ELBO depends only on \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), and this family keeps that quantity identical to DDPM's. So the bound — and therefore the trained network — is unchanged. You are free to pick a different reverse process after training.

Two settings matter. One choice of \(\sigma_t\) reproduces DDPM exactly. Setting \(\sigma_t = 0\) makes the reverse process deterministic:

\[ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\left[\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\right] + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta^*} \]

with the only randomness left being the initial \(x_T\). With no noise injected along the way, the map from noise to image is invertible — so you can also run it backwards and recover the noise that would generate a given image. That is DDIM inversion, and it is the trick behind a great deal of image editing.

Question  8.4 DDIM changes the forward process to be non-Markovian. Why does that not invalidate the trained network?

Answer Because the ELBO depends on \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) alone, and the DDIM family keeps that conditional identical to DDPM's. The bound is the same, so the regression target is the same, so training a DDPM has implicitly trained every model in the family. Only the sampling procedure changes.

Question  8.5 What does setting \(\sigma_t = 0\) buy you?

Answer A deterministic reverse process: the only randomness is \(x_T\). Determinism gives two things — faster sampling, because the chain can be taken in large jumps rather than a thousand tiny ones, and unique invertibility, since a deterministic map can be run backwards to recover the noise for a given image.

Why DDIM shares the bound

Beyond the notes — not in pages 72–110.

The claim that a non-Markovian family has the same bound sounds like a trick. It is not, and the reason is narrow enough to state plainly: the bound depends on the forward process only through the distribution of a single noisy step given the clean image. DDIM builds its family precisely so that this conditional is unchanged. Everything else about the process — the joint, the Markov property, the noise injected along the way — never enters. That is why you may replace the sampler after training and why the trained network never has to be told.
