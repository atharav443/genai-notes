The chapter in one sentence. You cannot supervise-train a model to be “good”, because “good” is a scalar over whole sequences — so you frame the LM as a policy, take the gradient of expected reward, estimate that gradient with the policy-gradient theorem, stabilise it with importance sampling and clipping (PPO), and source the reward either from a learned Bradley–Terry preference model (PPO) or implicitly from the policy itself (DPO).

§1  Why alignment is an RL problem at all

1.1  The three paradigms t=3:39

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{supervised:} && (x,y)\sim p(x,y) &&\Longrightarrow\; \text{learn } p(y\mid x)\\[2pt] &\text{unsupervised:} && x\sim p(x) &&\Longrightarrow\; \text{learn } p(x)\\[2pt] &\text{reinforcement:} && \text{no dataset} &&\Longrightarrow\; \text{learn } \pi_\theta(a\mid s) \end{aligned} \]

Supervised learning imitates a label. RL optimises a scalar obtained by acting. There is no dataset of correct actions — there is an environment that returns a number.

1.2  Alignment is a training-time operation, not an inference-time one t=1:42

The professor is explicit: alignment is not achieved by top-k, temperature, or a system prompt. The parameters themselves are changed so that the distribution the model samples from is aligned:

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} \quad\text{becomes}\quad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \]

with the goal, written on p128:

\[ \text{goal of alignment: make } p_\theta \text{ compatible with a desired, pre-defined rule / behaviour,} \] \[ \text{specified by human annotations or by fixed rules.} \]

1.3  Why not just supervised fine-tuning?

  1. Format blocker. Preference data is comparative, not generative. A human ranks two answers reliably; they cannot write the ideal answer. There is no target string, so there is nothing to put through cross-entropy.
  2. Locality blocker. “Helpful” is a property of the whole sequence — a derivation is correct or not, a refusal is appropriate or not. You cannot assign a per-token label to that. The signal lives at the end.

So you need an objective on complete sequences, optimised in expectation over your own sampling distribution. That is an MDP with a policy. Hence RL.

§2  The Markov Decision Process p129, t=7:27

symbolnameform
\( \mathcal{S} \)state space\( \{s_1,\dots,s_M\} \) — finite, for this course
\( \mathcal{A} \)action space\( \{a_1,\dots,a_n\} \)
\( p \)transition kernel\( p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \) — first-order Markov \(\Rightarrow\) “MDP”
\( r \)reward\( r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R} \), scalar, may be negative

“MDP” explained t=9:38 — “it's called Markov because the transition kernel is first-order Markov by definition.” The next state depends only on the current state and action, never on history: \( p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t,\dots,s_0)=p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \).
Agent ≡ policy t=13:27 — “if there is an agent there is a policy, because that's what defines what an agent is.” The two words are interchangeable for the rest of the chapter.

2.1  What \( \gamma \) is actually for

The notes introduce \( \gamma \) with no justification at all. The lecture's answer t=14:02: \( \gamma^t \) controls how much weight distant reward gets. \( \gamma\to 0 \) → only the immediate reward matters; \( \gamma\to 1 \) → all reward equally weighted, and the infinite sum may diverge.

ERRATUM The lecture's phrasing — “if you want the recent rewards to have the highest impact, scale \( \gamma \) accordingly” — is loose. Mechanically \( \gamma^t \) decays with \( t \), so reward far in the future is squashed. The right word is discounting the future, not “recency”. Say it that way and you never confuse yourself.

§3  Trajectory, return, objective p130, t=10:04

\[ \tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots,s_T,a_T) \] \[ p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \]

Three factors, and exactly one of them contains \( \theta \) — the middle one. Hold onto this; it is the engine of §4.

\[ R(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t) \qquad\text{(discounted return)} \] \[ J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big]=\int p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau, \qquad \theta^\star=\arg\max_\theta J(\theta) \]

“It's just the chain rule of probability” t=13:04. Start in accordance with \( \rho_0 \), then for each \( t \) multiply the policy probability of the action by the kernel probability of the next state.

All RL methods solve this same problem t=15:24: return the parameters maximising expected discounted return over all trajectories. The chapter only uses gradient methods, because the policy is a neural network and we already know how to backprop through it t=16:08.

§4  The Policy Gradient Theorem p131–132, t=17:52

4.1  The obstruction

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\int p_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)d\tau =\int \underbrace{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)}_{\text{the measure}}\,R(\tau)\,d\tau \]

Look at where \( \theta \) sits. It is not in the reward — it is in the measure. You are not differentiating a function of \( \theta \); you are differentiating a distribution. And since \( \nabla_\theta R(\tau)=0 \), \( \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta R(\tau)]=0 \) identically and uselessly. The integral is inaccessible twice over: \( p_\theta \) is unknown, and the trajectory space \( |\mathcal{V}|^T \) cannot be enumerated.

t=18:15 “Note that we don't have this distribution \( p_\theta \) with us — the same problem that we are encountering all over the course.” Exactly the shape of the VAE's intractable integral. Same fix: get \( \theta \) inside a log, then Monte-Carlo it.

4.2  The log-derivative trick t=18:44

\[ \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)=p_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \qquad\Big(\text{since } p\cdot\tfrac{1}{p}\nabla p=\nabla p\Big) \] \[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\int p_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau =\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\Big] \]

Now \( \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \) is computable — and the expectation can be estimated by sample averaging over rollouts.

4.3  The three factors collapse to one

\[ \log p_\theta(\tau)=\underbrace{\log\rho_0(s_0)}_{\theta\text{-free}} +\sum_t\underbrace{\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}_{\text{only term with }\theta} +\sum_t\underbrace{\log p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)}_{\theta\text{-free}} \] \[ \Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \]

\( \rho_0 \) and the kernel differentiate to zero. They simply cancel away t=20:25.

The result p131: \[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[\Big(\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)R(\tau)\Big] \]

4.4  Why this is the “very very strong result” t=21:03

You do not need the state-transition distribution. “As long as you know how the agent behaves and you have access to the reward function, you can train the policy without knowing what the transition probabilities are.” The gradient contains no \( p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \) term at all.

That is the whole point. In an LLM the “environment” is the world, and its transition kernel is not written down anywhere — there is no model of how the world responds to a token. The theorem says you never need one.

Every method in the rest of the chapter — REINFORCE, PPO, TRPO, DPO, GRPO — is a modification of this one equation t=21:49.

§5  Variance reduction: baseline, value, advantage p132–134, t=23:49

5.1  The estimator is high-variance

\( \nabla_\theta J \) is now an expectation, so it will be approximated by a sample average — and as soon as you have an estimator you have bias and variance t=23:25. Empirically the plain estimator has a lot of variance t=24:39. The fix: subtract a baseline \( b(s_t) \) from the return.

\[ R_t=\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r(s_k,a_k) \qquad\text{(reward-to-go, p132)} \] \[ R_t \;\longmapsto\; R_t-b(s_t) \]

The notes shift the time index from \( t \) to \( k \) on p132 and never say why t=24:02: it is so that \( R_0 \) is the full return and \( R_t \) is the return from time \( t \) onward.

5.2  Why a baseline is free

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,b(s_t)\big] =b(s_t)\sum_a\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t) =b(s_t)\,\nabla_\theta\underbrace{\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)}_{=\,1} =b(s_t)\cdot 0=0 \]

So subtracting any \( b(s_t) \) that does not depend on the action leaves the gradient exactly unbiased. It changes the estimator's variance only.

ERRATUM — lecture t=24:57 “Suppose you take an estimator that has high variance. You subtract a constant — the variance of the estimator would simply change.” This is false for a constant. \[ \mathrm{Var}(X-c)=\mathrm{Var}(X)\quad\text{for any constant }c \qquad\text{but}\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X-b)=\mathrm{Var}(X)-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,b)+\mathrm{Var}(b) \]

Subtracting a constant does nothing. Variance falls only because \( b(s) \) is correlated with the return and varies across states — you are removing the between-state variance. A state-dependent baseline helps; a constant one is a no-op.

5.3  The standard choice: the value function p132–133, t=25:44

\[ V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[R_t\mid s_t=s\big],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[R_t\mid s_t=s,\,a_t=a\big] \] \[ A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s) \]
Intuition, in the professor's words t=28:29: the \( Q \) function is the accumulated reward given that you started at \( s \) and took this particular action; the value function is the accumulated reward given only that you started at \( s \) — “I don't care what the action is.” The difference asks: “does taking this action lead to a better reward or not?” Hence the name advantage.

5.4  \( R_t-V(s_t) \) is an unbiased estimate of the advantage t=30:01

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[R_t-V^\pi(s_t)\mid s_t{=}s,\,a_t{=}a\big] &=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[R_t\mid s_t{=}s,\,a_t{=}a\big]}_{Q^\pi(s,a)}-V^\pi(s) &&\text{(linearity; }V^\pi(s)\text{ is constant given }s)\\[2pt] &=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)=A^\pi(s,a) &&\text{(definitions)} \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \( R_t-V(s_t) \) is a single-sample estimator of the advantage: drop the expectation from an unbiased estimator and what remains is a one-sample estimate of it t=31:02.

5.5  The key equation of every RLHF paper p134, t=31:37

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\;\approx\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;hat A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\Big] \] “This equation is the key equation that you see in all RLHF papers.”

Note the \( \approx \): it is not equal to the advantage, it is a single-sample estimate of it. The professor is careful about this t=31:37.

§6  An autoregressive LM as an RL policy p134–135, t=34:29

\[ \begin{aligned} s_0 &= \text{prompt}=x_{<1} &&\leftarrow \rho_0 \text{ is the prompt distribution}\\ a_0 &= x_1 &&\text{first output token}\\ s_1 &= [\,s_0,a_0\,],\quad a_1=x_2 &&\text{concatenate, then predict}\\ &\ \ \vdots\\ \mathcal{A} &= \mathcal{V},\qquad \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)=\mathrm{softmax}(z_t)\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|} \end{aligned} \]
Three consequences, all stated in the lecture:
  1. The LM is the policy t=37:07 — “the transformer that gives you a distribution over the tokens of the vocabulary is taken as a policy.” No separate head, no wrapper.
  2. The action space is the vocabulary, so \( |\mathcal{A}|\approx 5\times10^4\!-\!2\times10^5 \) — enormous by RL standards. And \( a_t \) is a token id, a one-hot over \( \mathcal{V} \), not the token's embedding.
  3. Updating the policy = changing the transformer's weights t=38:45. “Training a language model using RL is equivalent to changing the parameters of the language model itself.”
ADDED Where is \( r \) nonzero? Neither source says it plainly, and it matters. \[ r(s_t,a_t)=0\ \ \forall\,tThe reward arrives once, at the end of the episode, because a preference is a judgement about a complete response. This is why the advantage estimate in LLM-PPO is dominated by “how did the episode end” and why the value head exists at all (§8). Episodes terminate at EOS or max length, so in practice \( \gamma=1 \) — a quiet mismatch with the infinite-horizon discounted MDP of §2.

§7  PPO, part 1: importance sampling and clipping p136–140, t=39:10–52:42

7.1  The on-policy problem p136, t=41:18

\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\alpha\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta_t),\qquad \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\hat A_t\Big] \]

The expectation is over trajectories from the very policy being optimised. That is what on-policy means: every update needs fresh rollouts, because the moment \( \theta \) moves, the sample you just collected is from the wrong distribution.

For an LLM a rollout means running inference on the model you are training — generating full responses, several per prompt. Expensive. You want to reuse them.

7.2  Importance sampling p137, t=42:34

You want an expectation under \( p \) but only have samples from \( q \):

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[f(x)\big]=\int p(x)f(x)\,dx =\int q(x)\,\underbrace{\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}}_{\text{importance weight}}\,f(x)\,dx =\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)\Big] \]

Price paid: you must be able to evaluate the density ratio at every point. The professor's framing t=44:39: the advantage is you can estimate an expectation without samples from the target; the cost is the ratio.

7.3  Why a transformer is the perfect place to pay that price t=45:48

\[ r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)} \]

You can compute the log-likelihood a token got under the old checkpoint (it was written to the rollout buffer) and under the current one (one forward pass). The ratio is two numbers and a subtraction. That is only possible because the policy is an explicit likelihood model — you could never do this with a \( Q \)-learning agent.

7.4  The surrogate objective p138, t=46:48

Two forms circulate. They are not the same function — the lecture is explicit about this t=47:28:

\[ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}\big[r_t(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat A_t\big]}_{\text{policy gradient}} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}\big[r_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t\big]}_{\text{surrogate }L(\theta)} \]

They differ by ratio times grad-log versus grad-ratio. The bridge:

\[ \nabla_\theta r_t(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}} =\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta =r_t(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta \]
\[ \nabla_\theta L(\theta)\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}} =\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}} \] “Take this as homework and do it” t=48:00. Note the qualifier: the surrogacy holds at \( \theta_{\text{old}} \), i.e. it is a first-order approximation to \( J \) around the current policy — not an identity everywhere.
ADDED In an LLM implementation the ratio is per-token, \( r_t \) for the single token being scored, not the product over the whole trajectory. The trajectory-level product telescopes to \( \pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x) \) and is dominated by the first tokens, which destroys credit assignment. Every real PPO-for-LLM clips per token.

7.5  Off-policy, and why it is better here t=46:16

With the ratio, rollouts come from \( \pi_{\text{old}} \), not \( \pi_\theta \). This is the off-policy version. The lecture's claim that off-policy is preferred is about stability: taking a gradient from a rollout of the current policy at that exact instant is unstable; taking several gradient steps against a slightly stale policy, reweighted, is smoother and cheaper per sample.

7.6  The failure mode p139, t=49:22

\[ \pi_\theta \text{ far from } \pi_{\text{old}} \;\Longrightarrow\; r_t(\theta) \text{ explodes} \;\Longrightarrow\; L(\theta) \text{ explodes} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{high-variance, unstable updates} \]

7.7  TRPO: a trust region p140, t=50:04

\[ \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}\big[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t\big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\big)\Big]\le\delta \]

\( \delta \) is a hyperparameter and the region it defines is the trust region — the set of policies close enough to the old one that the surrogate is still trustworthy.

Problem t=51:53: solving this constrained problem is non-trivial — the KL is expensive at scale and constrained optimisation wants a second-order solver. So PPO replaces the constraint with a cheap surrogate for it.

7.8  PPO: clip instead of constrain p140, t=52:05

\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\Big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\hat A_t\Big)\Big], \qquad \epsilon\approx 0.1\!-\!0.2 \]

Read the \( \min \) as a pessimistic bound. Two regimes:

\( \hat A_t \)unclipped term wantswhat clip doesresult
\( >0 \)\( r \) as large as possible (raise the good action's prob) caps the gain at \( r=1+\epsilon \)no incentive past \( 1+\epsilon \)
\( <0 \)\( r \) as small as possible (lower the bad action's prob) caps the gain at \( r=1-\epsilon \)no incentive below \( 1-\epsilon \)

\( \min \) picks the worse of the two, so the objective can never be gamed by a runaway ratio. A ratio stuck inside \( [1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon] \) is, approximately, a bound on the KL t=52:17 — a cheap surrogate for the trust region.

Lineage t=50:43, worth memorising as one chain: 
REINFORCE  -- no importance sampling, on-policy, plain grad J
   -> PPO  -- importance sampling, off-policy, surrogate L = r * A
   -> TRPO -- PPO + explicit KL constraint (trust region)
   -> PPO  -- TRPO's constraint replaced by clipping
   -> GRPO -- a TRPO-family variant whose advantage needs no reward model

ERRATUM — notes Two problems with p141 as written:
  1. Signs are garbled in the ink. Since the notes write \( \theta^\star=\arg\max \), the value term must be subtracted (you minimise the squared error) and the entropy added (you maximise entropy). Written as a loss to minimise, all signs flip: \( -L^{\text{CLIP}}+c_1\,\text{MSE}-c_2\,\mathcal{H} \). Pick one convention and stay in it.
  2. A term is missing that makes RLHF-PPO what it is. The notes never include the KL penalty to a frozen reference policy — yet p144 relies on exactly that term when motivating DPO. In RLHF the per-token reward is shaped as \[ r_t=-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)} \] plus the terminal \( r_\phi(x,y) \). Without it, PPO will happily drift into degenerate text that games the reward model (§9.4).
ADDED Advantage estimation in practice. Neither source says how \( \hat A \) is actually computed. The standard is GAE (generalised advantage estimation), a geometric interpolation between one-step TD error and the full Monte-Carlo return: \[ \delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_\theta(s_{t+1})-V_\theta(s_t),\qquad \hat A_t=\sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l},\qquad \lambda\approx 0.95 \] \( \lambda\to 0 \): low variance, high bias (trusts \( V \)). \( \lambda\to 1 \): high variance, unbiased (trusts the observed return).

§9  Reward modelling p141–143, t=53:07

9.1  The naive route, and why it fails p142

In principle one could regress \( r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R} \) on \( \big[(s,a),\,r\big] \) tuples by ERM. “However, obtaining such data is not easy.” The lecture's phrasing t=53:32: absolute reward labels are expensive, noisy, and inconsistent across annotators — two people give the same response 6/10 and 9/10.

So switch the data format: ask for comparisons instead of scores. Humans are far more reliable at “which of these two is better” than at “rate this 0–10”.

\[ \mathcal{D}=\Big\{\big(x,\,y_w,\,y_l\big)\Big\},\qquad x=\text{prompt},\quad y_w=\text{preferred},\quad y_l=\text{non-preferred} \]

The notes end p142 with the live question that §9.2 answers: “Given preferential data, how would one build a reward model?”

9.2  The Bradley–Terry model p143

\[ P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\frac{\exp\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)\big)}{\exp\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)\big)+\exp\big(r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)} =\sigma\Big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\Big) \] \[ \sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}} \]

Fit by maximum likelihood on the preference labels:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\Big)\Big], \qquad \phi^\star=\arg\max_\phi \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) \]

The two lines on p143 are the same object: dividing numerator and denominator by \( e^{r_\phi(x,y_w)} \) turns the softmax form into the sigmoid-of-difference form. Both appear in the notes; do not be confused by the duplication.

9.3  What \( \sigma \) is doing, mechanically

\[ r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\to\begin{cases} +\infty & P(y_w\succ y_l)\to 1\quad\text{confidently right}\\ 0 & P(y_w\succ y_l)\to \tfrac12\quad\text{cannot tell them apart}\\ -\infty & P(y_w\succ y_l)\to 0\quad\text{confidently \textit{wrong}} \end{cases} \]

So the loss is a logistic loss on the reward difference. The gradient pushes \( r_\phi(x,y_w) \) up and \( r_\phi(x,y_l) \) down with weight \( \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_l)-r_\phi(x,y_w)\big) \) — how wrong the model currently is. A pair the model already gets right contributes almost nothing.

ERRATUM — both sources Three properties of Bradley–Terry that neither the notes nor the lecture state, and that a practitioner needs:
  1. Only differences are identifiable. \[ r_\phi \longmapsto r_\phi+c \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} \text{ unchanged}\quad\forall c\in\mathbb{R} \] So the absolute scale and offset of the reward are meaningless — only gaps matter. This is not a technicality: it is exactly the freedom DPO exploits in §10.3 to cancel \( Z(x) \).
  2. Ties are unrepresentable. BT demands a strict preference. Real annotation has ties and near-ties; you either drop them or model them (Rao–Kupper). Dropping them biases the reward toward whichever side won the coin flip.
  3. BT assumes the preference is stochastic with a logistic link on a scalar difference. It is a modelling assumption, not a theorem. If human preference depends on features that no scalar \( r \) captures, the reward model is misspecified and PPO optimises the misspecification.

9.4  Reward hacking — the failure mode that shapes RLHF

ADDED \( r_\phi \) is a proxy trained on finite data. PPO is an optimiser with no other objective. Give it enough steps and it will find inputs where \( r_\phi \) is high and human preference is not — sycophancy, excessive length, confident-sounding nonsense. This is Goodhart's law with a gradient. The mitigation is the KL leash: \[ \max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] -\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]

Which is the term missing from p141 (§8). \( \beta \) trades reward against faithfulness. This exact objective is what DPO will solve in closed form — the next section is one algebraic step away.

§10  Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) p144–146

10.1  The goal p144

\[ \text{optimise } \pi_\theta \text{ on preference data } \mathcal{D} \quad\text{without a reward model} \]

Start from the constrained objective of §9.4 — the notes' line \( L_{\text{policy}}=\mathbb{E}[r]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}} \):

\[ \max_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)\big] -\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]

10.2  It has a closed-form solution p144

\[ \pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big), \qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big) \]
ADDED Why this is the solution. The objective separates over \( x \), so fix \( x \) and maximise over the distribution \( \pi(\cdot\mid x) \) pointwise, subject to \( \sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1 \): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}&=\sum_y \pi(y)\Big[r(y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big]+\mu\Big(\sum_y\pi(y)-1\Big)\\[2pt] \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi(y)}&=r(y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}-\beta+\mu=0\\[2pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \pi(y)&\;\propto\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp\!\big(r(y)/\beta\big) \underbrace{\exp\!\big((\mu-\beta)/\beta\big)}_{\text{constant in }y\;\Rightarrow\;\text{absorbed into }Z(x)} \end{aligned} \]

The KL term is precisely what makes this tractable — without it the optimum is the degenerate “put all mass on \( \arg\max r \)”.

But \( Z(x) \) is intractable — it is a sum over every possible response. That is the obstruction the notes name on p144, and why you cannot just sample from \( \pi_r \) and be done.

10.3  Invert the closed form p145

Take logs and rearrange — \( Z(x) \) does not depend on \( y \), so it is a constant you can isolate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\pi_r(y\mid x)&=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)-\log Z(x)+\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\\[2pt] \Longrightarrow\quad r(x,y)&=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) \end{aligned} \]
This line is the pivot of the whole algorithm. It says the optimal reward is the log-ratio of the optimal policy to the reference policy, plus a per-prompt constant. So the reward is not something you need to learn separately — it is implicitly present in the policy itself.

10.4  Substitute into Bradley–Terry — \( Z(x) \) cancels p145–146

\[ \begin{aligned} P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)&=\sigma\Big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\Big)\\[2pt] &=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \underbrace{+\beta\log Z(x)}_{} -\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} \underbrace{-\beta\log Z(x)}_{\text{cancels}}\Big)\\[2pt] &=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} -\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Why the cancellation works: \( Z(x) \) is a function of \( x \) only — and \( y_w \), \( y_l \) share the same prompt \( x \). So it appears twice with the same value and subtracts out. That is the entire trick of DPO. If the two responses had come from different prompts, DPO would not exist.

Now replace \( \pi_r \) with the policy you are actually training, \( \pi_\theta \), take the negative log-likelihood, done:

\[ \boxed{\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big( \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} -\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big]\;} \] p146 — the notes' “objective for DPO”.

The notes close p146 with: “The above expression is independent of the reward \( r_\phi \).” Correct — and that is the headline. No reward model, no PPO, no rollouts, no value head. Just preference pairs and a log-ratio.

10.5  Reading the loss

\[ \hat r_\theta(x,y)\;\triangleq\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \qquad\text{(the \textit{implicit reward})} \] \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\Big)\Big] \]

It is formally identical to the Bradley–Terry reward-model loss of §9.2 — the same \( -\log\sigma(\text{gap}) \) — with \( r_\phi \) replaced by \( \hat r_\theta \). That is the entire content of DPO:

DPO is Bradley–Terry reward modelling with the policy as its own reward function. The reward is not absent. It is implicit, and it is \( \beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}) \).
\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big) \Big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big] \]

Two readings worth keeping:

  • Weight = surprise. \( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) \) is large when the model currently prefers the loser. Pairs the model already ranks correctly get almost no gradient. Same structure as §9.3.
  • Push and pull. Raise the log-likelihood of \( y_w \), lower that of \( y_l \) — but both are relative to \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \), so the model is only ever asked to change its mind about which of two responses is better, not to relearn language from scratch.

§15  Worked example — one PPO term, one DPO step

15.1  The PPO clip, numerically

\[ \text{vocab}=\{A,B,C\},\quad \epsilon=0.1,\quad \pi_{\text{old}}(A)=0.50,\quad \pi_\theta(A)=0.60 \] \[ r=\frac{\pi_\theta(A)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(A)}=\frac{0.60}{0.50}=1.20 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \mathrm{clip}(r,\,0.9,\,1.1)=1.10 \quad(r \text{ is above the band}) \]
\( \hat A \)unclipped \( r\hat A \)clipped \( 1.1\hat A \)\( \min \)reading
\( +0.8 \)\( 1.20(0.8)=0.96 \)\( 1.10(0.8)=0.88 \)0.88 the clipped one wins → constant → no gradient. We already raised a good action's probability by more than \( \epsilon \); stop rewarding it.
\( -0.8 \)\( 1.20(-0.8)=-0.96 \)\( 1.10(-0.8)=-0.88 \)\( -0.96 \) the unclipped one wins → gradient flows. We wrongly raised a bad action's probability; the objective keeps pushing \( r \) down.
The asymmetry is the algorithm. \( \min \) makes the objective flat when you have moved too far in the beneficial direction, but keeps it sloped when you have moved too far in the harmful direction. Clipping never protects a bad update — it only refuses to reward a good one further. A \( \max \) would invert this and let the policy run away.

\( \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \)\( \pi_\theta(y\mid x) \)\( \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \)\( \hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \)

And after the model has learned:

\( \pi_{\text{ref}} \)\( \pi_\theta \)\( \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \)\( \hat r_\theta \)

Two checks you can run on any DPO implementation.
  1. At initialisation the loss is exactly \( \ln 2\approx 0.6931 \). When \( \pi_\theta=\pi_{\text{ref}} \) every log-ratio is 0, so the gap is 0, so \( \sigma(0)=\tfrac12 \). If your first step is not \( \approx 0.693 \), your reference model is not a frozen copy of the policy — a classic bug.
  2. The gradient weight is \( \sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)=0.5173 \) in the first case. That is the “surprise” factor from §10.5: the model currently ranks the loser above the winner, so it gets a large correction.

§16  Common confusions, one line each

the wrong statementthe correction
“PPO is on-policy.”REINFORCE is on-policy. PPO is off-policy via importance sampling — that is the entire reason the ratio exists.
“The advantage is the reward.”\( A=Q-V \). It is the reward minus a baseline. A high-reward action in a high-value state can have zero advantage.
“A constant baseline reduces variance.”\( \mathrm{Var}(X-c)=\mathrm{Var}(X) \). Only a state-dependent baseline correlated with the return helps (§5.2).
“Clipping keeps each update small.”Clipping flattens the objective outside the band; it does not bound the gradient. It is a cheap proxy for a KL constraint, not a step-size limit.
“The reward model gets better during PPO.”\( r_\phi \) is frozen. Only \( \pi_\theta \) and the value head move. Otherwise the reward would chase the policy.
“Higher reward means a better model.”It means a better proxy score. Past a point, reward up → quality down. That is reward hacking (§9.4).
“DPO is reward-free.”The reward is implicit: \( \hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}) \). What DPO removes is the separate reward network and the rollouts.
“Larger \( \beta \) means stronger alignment.”Larger \( \beta \) means a tighter leash to \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) — less optimisation. Too large and the model never changes.
“\( \delta \) and \( \epsilon \) are the same knob.”\( \delta \) is a KL radius (TRPO). \( \epsilon \) is a probability-ratio band (PPO). Related, not equivalent.
“The value head is part of the final model.”It is a training-only artefact for estimating the advantage. Discarded at inference.
“Reward scale matters.”\( r\mapsto r+c \) is a symmetry of Bradley–Terry. Only differences are identified.
“DPO needs no preference data.”It needs exactly preference pairs and nothing else — no rollouts, no reward model. That is the trade.

§18  Appendix — the five derivations, stripped to exam length

Reproduce these from blank paper. Each line is annotated with the reason for the step, because that is what gets marked.

1.  Policy gradient

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J&=\int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau &&\text{gradient moves inside the integral}\\ &=\int p_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau &&\text{log-derivative trick}\\ &=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\big] &&\text{back to an expectation}\\ \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)&=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) &&\rho_0,\,p \text{ are } \theta\text{-free} \end{aligned} \]

2.  A baseline is free (needs \( b \) independent of the action)

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)b(s)\big] =b(s)\sum_a\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s) =b(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla_\theta 1=0 \]

3.  \( R_t-V(s_t) \) estimates the advantage

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[R_t-V^\pi(s_t)\mid s_t{=}s,a_t{=}a\big] &=\mathbb{E}\big[R_t\mid s_t{=}s,a_t{=}a\big]-V^\pi(s) &&\text{linearity; }V^\pi(s)\text{ fixed}\\ &=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)=A^\pi(s,a) &&\text{definitions} \end{aligned} \]

4.  The surrogate matches the gradient at \( \theta_{\text{old}} \)

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta r_t(\theta)&=\nabla_\theta\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}} =\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta=r_t(\theta)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta &&\text{chain rule on a log}\\ \Longrightarrow\ \nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}\big[r_t\hat A_t\big]&=\mathbb{E}\big[r_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\hat A_t\big]=\nabla_\theta J &&\text{at } r_t=1,\ \text{i.e. } \theta=\theta_{\text{old}} \end{aligned} \]

5.  DPO

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{(a) closed form:}\quad &\max_{\pi}\ \sum_y\pi(y)\Big[r(y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_y\pi(y)=1\\[3pt] &\frac{\partial}{\partial\pi(y)}=0\;\Longrightarrow\; \pi(y)\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp\big(r(y)/\beta\big) &&\text{the Lagrange multiplier is constant in }y\Rightarrow Z(x)\\[3pt] \text{(b) invert:}\quad &\log\pi=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log Z+r/\beta \;\Longrightarrow\; r=\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}+\beta\log Z(x)\\[3pt] \text{(c) substitute:}\quad &r_w-r_l=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)} \underbrace{+\beta\log Z(x)-\beta\log Z(x)}_{=0} &&Z(x)\text{ depends only on }x;\ y_w,y_l\text{ share }x \end{aligned} \]
The four sentences that unlock all five.
1. Differentiate the measure, not the integrand — then convert with \( \nabla p=p\,\nabla\log p \).
2. Any action-independent term has zero expected gradient, because \( \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=1 \).
3. \( A=Q-V \) removes the state's own value, leaving only the action's contribution.
4. Two constants that depend only on \( x \) cannot change the ranking of two responses to that same \( x \) — so any such constant is invisible to Bradley–Terry.

§19  GRPO — the advantage without a reward model t=50:25, t=21:49

ADDED The lecture mentions GRPO twice — “GRPO is a version of TRPO where the advantage function is computed without rewards” t=50:25 — but p128–146 never introduces it. It is the one algorithm that closes the loop between §1.4 (verifiable rewards) and §7 (PPO), so it belongs here.

Recall the problem (§8): \( \hat A_t \) needs a reward and a value function, and \( V_\theta \) is an entire extra network. But if the reward is verifiable (§1.4) there is no reward model at all — the verifier is the reward function. And the baseline does not have to be a learned \( V \); it only has to be something action-independent and correlated with the return (§5.2).

GRPO's move: sample a group of \( G \) completions for the same prompt, score each with the verifier, and use the group's own statistics as the baseline.

\[ \mathcal{A}_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}\big(r_1,\dots,r_G\big)}{\operatorname{std}\big(r_1,\dots,r_G\big)}, \qquad i=1,\dots,G \]

Why it matters. GRPO is the algorithm behind modern “reasoning” training on math and code: drop the reward model (the verifier is exact), drop the value head (the group is the baseline), and spend the compute on more samples per prompt instead. It is exactly the payoff of the observation in §1.4 — RLHF is strongest where the reward can be checked.
Not free. The normalisation by \( \operatorname{std} \) makes the advantage scale-free, which is convenient but means a group where every sample scores identically (all pass, or all fail) has \( \mathcal{A}_i=0 \) — the prompt contributes no gradient. Hard-but-solvable prompts carry the whole signal.
