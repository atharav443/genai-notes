Bounds, the law
of large numbers,
and the bell curve

How to say something true when you cannot compute anything, what averaging really buys you, why the error shrinks as the square root of effort, and what to do when you cannot even draw a sample

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the last of the seven essays on probability. It needs the fourth and fifth, on expectation and on densities, and it closes the loop: Jensen's inequality from the fifth section is the step that turns a likelihood into the bound every variational model optimises, and the square-root law of the eighth section is the reason batch size is a tuning knob rather than an afterthought.

Part I — Bounding what you cannot compute

1 · Why an inequality is a tool

A machine produces a positive quantity with mean 2. What is the chance it exceeds 10?

You cannot answer. You have one number — a mean — and the tail is determined by the shape, which you do not know. Two different distributions with mean 2 give wildly different answers: one bounded by 2 answers zero, and a distribution that is 10 with probability 0.2 and 0 otherwise answers 0.2.

What you can say is a bound that holds for all of them. That is what the inequalities of this part are: statements that are true of every distribution satisfying a stated hypothesis, and therefore weaker than the truth but free of assumptions.

The trade is worth being explicit about. An inequality is loose — it may be off by a large factor — but it is never wrong, and it requires almost nothing. That combination is what makes it usable when the distribution is unavailable, which is to say, almost always.

Question  1.1 What does a probability inequality give you that an exact computation would not?

Answer A statement that holds for every distribution satisfying a few weak hypotheses, when the distribution itself is unknown.

Question  1.2 What do you pay for that?

Answer Tightness — the bound can be far from the truth, sometimes by orders of magnitude.

2 · Markov: a bound from one number

The weakest useful inequality, and the ancestor of the rest. For a non-negative variable,

\[ P(X \ge a) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}, \qquad a > 0 \]

The proof is a one-line geometric observation. The expectation is the integral of \(x\) against the density; the part of that integral sitting above \(a\) is at least \(a\) times the mass above \(a\), because every point there contributes at least \(a\) and the mass is \(P(X \ge a)\).

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx \;\ge\; \int_a^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx \;\ge\; a\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = a\,P(X \ge a) \]

With mean 2 and threshold 10, the bound is \(0.2\). That is the answer the two-point distribution gives exactly, so Markov is tight in the worst case — and hopeless in the typical case. If the quantity is a coin flip scaled to mean 2 with a maximum of 2, the true probability of exceeding 10 is zero and Markov still says \(0.2\).

Tightness in the worst case is the right way to read these results. They are guarantees, not estimates, and the distribution that makes them tight is always a deliberately awkward one.

Question  2.1 State Markov's inequality, including its hypothesis.

Answer For \(X \ge 0\) and \(a > 0\), \(P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a\).

Question  2.2 A non-negative variable has mean 2. Bound \(P(X \ge 10)\).

Answer \(2/10 = 0.2\).

Question  2.3 When is Markov exactly tight?

Answer When the variable takes only the values \(a\) and 0, so all its mass sits at the threshold.

3 · Chebyshev: a bound from two numbers

Add a variance and the bound improves sharply, because you can now talk about distance from the mean in both directions.

\[ P\big(|X - \mu| \ge a\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{a^2} \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P\big(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\big) \le \frac{1}{k^2} \]

The derivation is Markov applied to the non-negative variable \((X-\mu)^2\), whose mean is \(\sigma^2\):

\[ P\big((X-\mu)^2 \ge a^2\big) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{a^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{a^2} \]

With \(\sigma = 2\) and \(a = 6\), the bound is \(4/36 \approx 0.111\) — the same as \(1/3^2\), because six is three standard deviations. Compare the truth for a normal distribution: \(0.0027\). So Chebyshev is loose by a factor of forty, and it is still the best you can do without assuming a shape.

The version worth memorising is the second one, \(1/k^2\), because it is dimensionless: no more than \(1/k^2\) of any distribution's mass lies more than \(k\) standard deviations from the mean, whatever the distribution is. That is a genuinely surprising statement the first time you meet it, and it is the reason a six-sigma event is remarkable in any well-behaved process.

Question  3.1 State Chebyshev's inequality in both forms.

Answer \(P(|X-\mu| \ge a) \le \sigma^2/a^2\), equivalently \(P(|X-\mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2\).

Question  3.2 \(\sigma = 2\), \(a = 6\). What does Chebyshev give, and how does it compare with a normal?

Answer \(4/36 \approx 0.111\), against the normal's \(0.0027\) — loose, but valid for every distribution.

Question  3.3 Which earlier inequality does Chebyshev follow from, and applied to what?

Answer Markov, applied to the non-negative variable \((X-\mu)^2\).

4 · Cauchy–Schwarz, and the ceiling on correlation

Correlation cannot exceed 1. Why not?

\[ \big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2] \]

This is Cauchy–Schwarz, and it is the statement that the inner product of two random variables is at most the product of their lengths — the same fact as the triangle inequality for vectors, in a space whose elements are random variables. Apply it to the centred variables \(X - \mu_X\) and \(Y - \mu_Y\) and it reads \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)^2 \le \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2\), which after taking square roots is exactly \(|\rho| \le 1\).

The bound is attained only when one variable is an affine function of the other, which is why \(\rho = \pm 1\) means a perfect straight-line relationship and nothing else. And it gives the honest reading of the previous essay's counterexample: \(Y = X^2\) has a curved relationship, so its correlation must be under 1 in magnitude — it happens to be 0, which is the extremum in the other direction.

In estimation the inequality appears as a variance sanity check. The importance-sampling estimator's variance involves \(\mathbb{E}[f^2]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[g^2]\) terms whose ratio blows up when the weights have heavy tails, and Cauchy–Schwarz is the tool that tells you so before you run the experiment rather than after.

Question  4.1 State Cauchy–Schwarz for random variables.

Answer \((\mathbb{E}[XY])^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2]\).

Question  4.2 How does it give \(|\rho| \le 1\)?

Answer Applied to the centred variables it reads \(\mathrm{Cov}^2 \le \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2\); take square roots and divide.

Question  4.3 When is the bound attained, and what does that mean for the scatter plot?

Answer Only when one variable is an affine function of the other, so \(|\rho| = 1\) means a perfect straight line.

5 · Jensen, and the direction of the bending

You have met this twice already, in the fourth essay as “expectation does not commute with a curve” and in the sixth as the missing ingredient for a bound. Here it is properly, because it is the single most consequential inequality in this series.

A function is convex if the chord between any two of its points lies above the curve. For such a function, and any random variable,

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[g(X)\big] \;\ge\; g\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big) \qquad (g \text{ convex}) \]

and the inequality reverses for a concave \(g\), which is to say for \(-g\). The intuition is the same as the seesaw: the average of the heights of the curve at the two ends of a chord is above the height of the curve at the midpoint, because the curve sags below its chords.

Average of the curve versus curve of the average. The solid dot is g(E[X]); the open dot on the dashed chord is E[g(X)]. Convex puts the chord above; concave reverses the inequality.

In numbers

Let \(X\) be 1 or 4 with probability \(1/2\) each, and take \(g = \log\), which is concave.

\[ \log\mathbb{E}[X] = \log 2.5 = 0.9163 \qquad\text{while}\qquad \mathbb{E}[\log X] = \tfrac12(0 + 1.3863) = 0.6931 \]

The gap is \(0.2231\), and it is exactly the price of moving the logarithm outside the average. Note what the gap measures: it is zero when \(X\) is constant, and it grows as the spread of \(X\) grows. Jensen's gap is a measure of variability.

Why the KL divergence cannot be negative

The application that matters most. For two distributions \(p\) and \(q\),

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\log\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right] \;\ge\; \log \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right] = \log 1 = 0 \]

The inequality is Jensen on the concave log, and the final equality is the definition of an expectation. So the divergence is non-negative, and it is zero only when the ratio is constant, which for two distributions means \(p = q\) almost everywhere.

That two-line proof is load-bearing for everything in the generative-AI series that says “this is a bound”. A bound is only a bound because the thing you subtracted is non-negative, and the thing you subtracted is non-negative because of Jensen.

Question  5.1 State Jensen's inequality for a convex function, and say what happens for a concave one.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)] \ge g(\mathbb{E}[X])\) for convex \(g\); the inequality reverses for concave \(g\).

Question  5.2 \(X = 1\) or \(4\) with probability \(1/2\), \(g = \log\). Compute both sides.

Answer \(\log 2.5 = 0.9163\) and \(\tfrac12(0 + 1.3863) = 0.6931\); the gap is \(0.2231\).

Question  5.3 Prove \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0\) using Jensen.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_p[\log(p/q)] \ge \log\mathbb{E}_p[p/q] = \log 1 = 0\), since log is concave.

Question  5.4 What does the size of Jensen's gap measure?

Answer Variability: zero when the variable is constant, growing as its spread grows.

Part II — What happens as \(n\) grows

6 · The law of large numbers

A casino runs a game with expected loss 2% per bet. Over ten bets the operator might lose money. Over ten million, the result is not in doubt. Something is making the average concentrate, and the law of large numbers names it.

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \;\xrightarrow{\;P\;}\; \mu \qquad\text{weak law} \]

“Converges in probability” means: for every \(\epsilon > 0\), the probability that the average differs from \(\mu\) by more than \(\epsilon\) tends to zero. The strong law says the same thing with almost-sure convergence, which is stronger and which matters for statements about a single infinite sequence rather than about each fixed \(n\).

The weak law is a two-line consequence of Chebyshev, and the derivation is worth seeing because it shows exactly where the \(\sqrt{n}\) comes from. The average has mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2/n\), so for any \(\epsilon\):

\[ P\big(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \]

That is the whole proof. The variance of the average shrinks like \(1/n\), Chebyshev converts a variance into a tail probability, and the tail goes to zero. Nothing was assumed about the shape of the distribution — only that \(\sigma^2\) is finite, and that finiteness is doing real work.

Where this breaks Without a finite mean, nothing concentrates. Draw from a Cauchy distribution — the ratio of two standard normals — and the average of \(n\) draws has exactly the same distribution as one draw, for every \(n\). Average a million of them and you have learned nothing. The law of large numbers is not a statement about arithmetic; it is a statement about a hypothesis, and the hypothesis can fail.

Question  6.1 State the weak law of large numbers.

Answer The sample average of i.i.d. variables with finite mean \(\mu\) converges to \(\mu\) in probability: \(P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \epsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\).

Question  6.2 Sketch the proof from Chebyshev.

Answer \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n\), so Chebyshev gives \(P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2) \to 0\).

Question  6.3 How does the strong law differ from the weak one?

Answer It asserts almost-sure convergence — the average converges along the whole sequence with probability one, not merely in probability at each fixed \(n\).

Question  6.4 Give a distribution for which averaging gains nothing.

Answer The Cauchy: the average of \(n\) draws has the same distribution as one draw, for every \(n\).

7 · The central limit theorem

The law of large numbers says the average lands on \(\mu\). The central limit theorem says something far more precise about how it lands: the fluctuations around \(\mu\), scaled up by \(\sqrt{n}\), are normal.

\[ \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1) \]

Convergence in distribution. The shape of the error does not depend on the shape you started with — only on its mean and variance. This is why the bell curve is everywhere, and it is the deep answer to the question the fifth essay deferred: sums of many small independent contributions are normal whatever the contributions were.

In numbers

Take \(n = 100\) draws with \(\mu = 2\) and \(\sigma = 2\). The average has standard deviation \(2/\sqrt{100} = 0.2\). Then

\[ P(\bar{X}_{100} > 2.4) = P\!\left(Z > \frac{2.4 - 2}{0.2}\right) = P(Z > 2) \approx 0.023 \]

Two and a half percent, and it came from a table for the standard normal because the CLT says the standardized average is approximately standard normal. Nothing else about the original distribution was used.

The \(n \ge 30\) folklore

Somewhere in every statistics course there is a claim that thirty observations is enough. It is not a theorem and it is not reliable. The approximation's accuracy depends on how close the original distribution already is to normal: for a uniform starting point a dozen draws is ample, and for a distribution with a long right tail a thousand may not be. Ask what the convergence rate is, not what the threshold is.

Question  7.1 State the central limit theorem.

Answer \((\bar{X}_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\) converges in distribution to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) for i.i.d. variables with finite variance.

Question  7.2 \(\mu = 2\), \(\sigma = 2\), \(n = 100\). Compute \(P(\bar{X}_{100} > 2.4)\) approximately.

Answer Standardise to \((2.4-2)/0.2 = 2\), so \(P(Z > 2) \approx 0.023\).

Question  7.3 Why is the “\(n \ge 30\)” rule unreliable?

Answer Because the rate of convergence depends on how non-normal the starting distribution is; a skewed or heavy-tailed one may need far more than thirty.

Question  7.4 Which hypothesis does the central limit theorem need that the law of large numbers does not emphasise?

Answer A finite variance; without it there is no limiting normal, as the Cauchy case shows.

8 · Error bars, and the difference between two sigmas

You have estimated an average from \(n\) samples. How wrong is it?

The standard deviation of the estimator is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), called the standard error, and it is a different object from \(\sigma\). The confusion between them is the most common statistical error in applied work, so state it plainly: \(\sigma\) describes how spread out one observation is; \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) describes how spread out the average is. Data is noisy; an estimate is less noisy than the data, by the square root of how much of it you have.

\[ \mathrm{se} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad 95\% \text{ interval} \approx \hat\mu \pm 1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

With \(\sigma = 2\) and \(n = 400\): the standard error is \(0.1\) and the interval is about \(\pm 0.196\). Halving that interval requires 1,600 samples, not 800 — the fourfold tax from the fourth essay, unchanged.

The \(1.96\) is not magic and is worth demystifying: it is the point beyond which a standard normal puts 2.5% of its mass on each side. It is inherited from the CLT, so it is approximately right for averages from any distribution with finite variance, and exactly right for a normal.

Question  8.1 What is the standard error of the mean, and how does it differ from \(\sigma\)?

Answer \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\): the spread of the average, whereas \(\sigma\) is the spread of a single observation.

Question  8.2 \(\sigma = 2\), \(n = 400\). Give the standard error and the 95% interval half-width.

Answer \(\mathrm{se} = 0.1\), half-width \(\approx 1.96 \times 0.1 = 0.196\).

Question  8.3 Where does the number 1.96 come from?

Answer It is the standard-normal quantile leaving 2.5% in each tail, inherited through the central limit theorem.

Question  8.4 You want to halve the width of a confidence interval. How many more samples?

Answer Four times as many, since the width scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Part III — When you cannot even draw

9 · Sampling without a sampler

Every argument so far has assumed you can draw from the distribution. Often you cannot: you know the posterior \(p(\theta \mid \text{data})\) only up to a constant, because the denominator is the same intractable integral from the second essay.

The Monte Carlo methods of the fourth essay need independent samples from \(p\). When those are unavailable, there is a different idea: build a Markov chain whose long-run distribution is \(p\), and walk it. After a while the states it visits are samples from \(p\), approximately and asymptotically.

The Metropolis–Hastings rule is the standard construction. From the current state \(x\), propose \(x'\) from some proposal distribution, and accept it with probability

\[ \min\left(1, \;\frac{\tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)}\right) \]

where \(\tilde p\) is the unnormalised density. Look at what happened: the normalising constant appears in both the numerator and the denominator and cancels. That cancellation is the entire point — it is why MCMC works precisely in the case where the direct approach fails.

Gibbs sampling is the special case where you cycle through the coordinates and sample each one from its conditional given the others, which needs no accept/reject step at all. Both methods are correct in the limit and neither is fast, and both come with the same honest caveats: the early iterations are not from \(p\) (burn-in), successive samples are correlated (autocorrelation), and the guarantees are asymptotic rather than finite-sample.

Where this breaks MCMC gives you samples, not a number. There is no error bar in the usual sense, because the samples are dependent; the effective sample size is smaller than the number of iterations by roughly the autocorrelation time, and estimating that factor is itself a research problem. A chain that has not converged produces confident nonsense, and the diagnostic that catches it — running several chains from different starts and comparing — is a heuristic, not a guarantee.

Question  9.1 Why is direct sampling impossible in a Bayesian posterior, and what replaces it?

Answer The normalising constant \(p(\text{data})\) is an intractable integral; Markov chain Monte Carlo replaces independent draws with a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior.

Question  9.2 What cancels in the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio, and why does that matter?

Answer The unnormalised density's normalising constant, which is exactly the quantity that made direct sampling impossible.

Question  9.3 Name the two honest caveats of any MCMC run.

Answer Burn-in (early states are not from the target) and autocorrelation (samples are dependent, so the effective sample size is smaller than the iteration count).

Part IV — Where this lands in generative AI

10 · Jensen is the reason any of it is a bound

Almost every objective in the series has the same skeleton: take a log-likelihood you cannot compute, apply Jensen to get something you can, and prove the gap is non-negative. Both halves of that — the direction and the non-negativity — come from the fifth section.

\[ \log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]}_{\text{ELBO, computable}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge\, 0 \text{ by Jensen}} \]

The equality is exact; the usefulness comes from the second term being non-negative, which is Jensen on the logarithm, which is the concavity of \(\log\). Remove Jensen and the ELBO is just an algebraic rearrangement with no reason to be a lower bound.

MCMCsampling an unnormalised posterior in VAE-Curious's discussion of what the encoder is approximating — but note that diffusion's reverse process is not MCMC, it is a learned transport with a fixed number of steps

One correction worth making explicitly, because it is a common conflation. Diffusion sampling is often described as “MCMC-like”, and the analogy is tempting: both walk a chain from noise toward data. But the diffusion reverse process is a fixed, learned, finite-length sequence of Gaussian steps with no accept/reject test, so it has no stationary distribution and no asymptotic guarantee. Its correctness comes from having learned the reverse of a known forward process, not from any chain converging. The vocabulary is borrowed; the mathematics is not.

The habit to take away from this essay is a single question, asked of any objective you meet: which inequality makes this a bound, and in which direction does it point? If the answer is Jensen on a concave function, the objective is a lower bound on a log-likelihood and you should expect a gap you can name. If there is no inequality anywhere in the derivation, the quantity is an identity and calling it a bound is a mistake.

Question  10.1 Which inequality makes the ELBO a lower bound rather than a rearrangement?

Answer Jensen's inequality applied to the concave logarithm, which is what forces the KL gap to be non-negative.

Question  10.2 Why does a minibatch gradient estimate the full-data gradient?

Answer By the law of large numbers: the average over a random minibatch converges to the expectation over the data distribution.

Question  10.3 Why is diffusion's reverse process not a Markov chain Monte Carlo method?

Answer It is a fixed, learned, finite sequence of Gaussian steps with no accept/reject test and no stationary distribution; its correctness comes from inverting a known forward process.

Question  10.4 A derivation produces an objective with no inequality anywhere. What should you conclude?

Answer That it is an identity, not a bound — and calling it a bound is an error.
