How to squeeze a photograph through a narrow pipe and get it back — and why the pipe needs a little noise to be trainable at all

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. Each box is numbered, the questions are collected at the end, and the same list ships as a plain-text file. On screen, each Part ends with a deck that deals its cards to you one at a time.

Everything here follows pages 43–71 of an IIT Madras generative-AI course. A few short passages are marked beyond the notes: those go further than the pages do. The argument runs: we write down a model with a hidden variable, discover the likelihood is intractable, bound it, find that the bound has a gradient we cannot take, and fix that with one line of algebra.

Part I — A model with something hidden in it

1 · Latent variables

Suppose someone hands you a photograph of a cake and asks you to write down the recipe. The photograph is what you observed; the recipe is what you did not. Many recipes would produce a cake that photographs identically, so the recipe is not recoverable in principle — and that ambiguity is not an inconvenience to be tidied away later. It is the object of study. A latent variable model is the attempt to have a recipe, and to be honest about the fact that the photograph does not determine it.

A latent variable model says that what you observe is a shadow of something you do not observe. Write \(z\) for the hidden thing and \(x\) for the observation. The model's density is a marginal:

\[ P_\theta(x) = \int_z P_\theta(x, z) \, dz \]

with \(z\) continuous, or a sum \(\sum_z P_\theta(x,z)\) if it is discrete. The integral is the whole difficulty of this essay: it is why the likelihood has no closed form, and every technique below is a way of living without it.

What \(z\) means depends on its type. Make it discrete, taking one of \(M\) values, and asking for \(z_i\) given \(x_i\) is a clustering question — which of the \(M\) groups does this point belong to. That is where Gaussian mixture models and k-means live. Make it continuous in a much smaller space than \(x\), say \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k \ll d\), and asking for \(z_i\) given \(x_i\) is a compression question: find a short description that still explains the long observation.

Question  1.1 Write down the defining equation of a latent variable model, and say what makes it hard.

Answer \(P_\theta(x) = \int_z P_\theta(x,z)\,dz\), a marginal over the joint. The difficulty is that the integral over the latent space is generally intractable — there is no closed form, so \(\log P_\theta(x)\) cannot be evaluated directly.

Question  1.2 The same model gives clustering in one case and feature extraction in another. What changes?

Answer The type and size of \(z\). Discrete \(z \in \{1,\dots,M\}\) assigns each \(x_i\) to one of \(M\) groups — that is clustering. Continuous \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k \ll d\) gives each \(x_i\) a real-valued code — that is an embedding, and the machinery is an auto-encoder.

Question  1.3 In a latent variable model, which of \(x\) and \(z\) is observed?

Answer \(x\) is observed; \(z\) is hidden. The model explains each observation as the shadow of an unobserved cause.

Hierarchical priors

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

One latent layer forces the encoder to commit to a single description at a single scale. Stack the layers instead — a coarse code, then a finer code conditioned on the coarse one, and so on — and the model can separate “what is this” from “what exactly”. Each layer’s prior depends on the layer above it, which is the structural idea that diffusion later takes to its limit: a thousand layers, each making one small refinement to the last.

Part II — From "match the distribution" to "maximise likelihood"

2 · Why maximum likelihood, of all things

Maximum likelihood is usually handed to you as a starting assumption, and then defended with a shrug about statistical efficiency. Watch it arrive here instead as a consequence. You asked to be close to the data under one particular ruler, and that ruler turned out to be a difference of two terms, one of which knows nothing about your model. Cross the term out and what is left is “make the observed data likely”. Nobody chose it. It fell out of the ruler.

The stated goal is to make the model's distribution match the data's. Measure the mismatch with forward KL and minimise:

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \int_x P_x(x) \log\frac{P_x(x)}{P_\theta(x)} \, dx \]

Split the logarithm. The first piece is \(\int_x P_x \log P_x\), which contains no \(\theta\) at all — it is a property of the data, a constant as far as the optimisation is concerned. What is left is

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \left[-\int_x P_x(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx\right] = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x) \]

which is maximum likelihood estimation. This is worth pausing on, because maximum likelihood is usually taught as a starting assumption. It is not. It is what "minimise the KL divergence to the data" reduces to once the constants fall out. The whole rest of the essay is a fight with one term: \(\log P_\theta(x)\), which contains an integral nobody can do.

Question  2.1 Show why minimising \(D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta)\) is the same as maximum likelihood.

Answer Expand the KL: \(\int P_x \log P_x - \int P_x \log P_\theta\). The first integral involves only \(P_x\), so it is constant with respect to \(\theta\) and cannot affect the argmin. Dropping it leaves minimising \(-\mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x)\), i.e. maximising \(\mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x)\).

Question  2.2 Which term of the KL expansion vanishes, and what is the general lesson?

Answer The entropy term \(\int_x P_x(x)\log P_x(x)\,dx\) — it does not depend on the parameters. The lesson: when a divergence to a fixed distribution is minimised, only the cross term matters, which is why MLE keeps appearing in generative modelling.

Part III — A bound you can actually compute

3 · Jensen, and the appearance of \(q\)

Let \(L(\theta) = \log P_\theta(x)\). Multiply inside the integral by \(q(z\mid x)/q(z\mid x)\) for any density \(q\) over the latent space — free, since it equals one:

\[ L(\theta) = \log \int_z q(z\mid x)\left[\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right] dz = \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\left[\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right] \]

Now apply Jensen's inequality, \(\log \mathbb{E}[\cdot] \ge \mathbb{E}\log[\cdot]\), which holds because \(\log\) is concave. The inequality points the useful way:

\[ L(\theta) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\log\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)} \;=:\; J_\theta(q) \]

\(J_\theta(q)\) is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO, and it is a function of two independent things: the model parameters \(\theta\), and the density \(q\) you chose. That second slot is the free variable, and the entire method lives in the choice you put there. The bound becomes exact precisely when \(q\) equals the true posterior \(P_\theta(z\mid x)\) — which we cannot compute, which is why it is still a bound.

Jensen, in numbers, and exactly how loose it is

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

Let \(X\) be 1 or 4 with equal probability. Then \(\log\mathbb{E}X = \log 2.5 = 0.916\), while \(\mathbb{E}\log X = \tfrac12(0 + 1.386) = 0.693\). The bound holds with a gap of 0.223. Now let \(X\) be 2.5 with certainty: both sides are \(\log 2.5\) and the gap is zero. Jensen is tight exactly when the thing inside the expectation does not vary.

Apply that to the ELBO. The thing inside is the ratio \(P_\theta(x,z)/q(z\mid x)\). It is constant in \(z\) precisely when \(q(z\mid x) \propto P_\theta(x,z)\), that is, when \(q\) is the true posterior. And you can measure the gap exactly rather than wave at it. Write \(P_\theta(x,z) = P_\theta(z\mid x)P_\theta(x)\) inside the ELBO:

\[ J_\theta(q) = \mathbb{E}_q\log\frac{P_\theta(z\mid x)\,P_\theta(x)}{q(z\mid x)} = \log P_\theta(x) - D_{KL}\!\left(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,P_\theta(z\mid x)\right) \]

The shortfall of the bound is the KL divergence from your guess to the truth. Raising the ELBO over \(q\) is the same act as dragging \(q\) toward the posterior, and it is why the encoder of a VAE ends up being an approximate posterior without anyone telling it to be one.

Question  3.1 \(X\) is 1 or 4 with equal probability. Compute \(\log\mathbb{E}X\) and \(\mathbb{E}\log X\).

Answer \(\log 2.5 \approx 0.916\) and \(\tfrac12\log 4 \approx 0.693\). The second is smaller, as Jensen promises for the concave \(\log\).

Question  3.2 When is Jensen’s inequality \(\log\mathbb{E}X \ge \mathbb{E}\log X\) an equality?

Answer When \(X\) is constant (almost surely). For the ELBO, that means the ratio \(P_\theta(x,z)/q(z\mid x)\) does not depend on \(z\).

Question  3.3 Write \(\log P_\theta(x)\) exactly in terms of the ELBO.

Answer \(\log P_\theta(x) = J_\theta(q) + D_{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,P_\theta(z\mid x))\). The gap is the KL from \(q\) to the true posterior.

Question  3.4 For fixed \(\theta\), maximising the ELBO over \(q\) is equivalent to what?

Answer Minimising \(D_{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,P_\theta(z\mid x))\): making \(q\) the best approximation to the posterior, because \(\log P_\theta(x)\) does not depend on \(q\).

Question  3.5 Why does Jensen give a lower bound rather than an upper one?

Answer Because \(\log\) is concave, so \(\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}\log X\). The quantity we can compute, \(\mathbb{E}\log\) of the ratio, sits below the quantity we want, \(\log\) of the evidence. Concavity fixes the direction.

Question  3.6 What is \(q(z\mid x)\), and when does the bound become tight?

Answer It is any density over the latent variable given the observation — the variational latent posterior. It is a free choice we introduce; it is not the true posterior. The ELBO is exact exactly when \(q(z\mid x) = P_\theta(z\mid x)\), which is the quantity we were trying to avoid needing.

Flows as posteriors

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

The Gaussian posterior is a guess of convenience, chosen because it makes the divergence computable. A normalising flow refuses that bargain: start with a Gaussian and push it through a chain of invertible maps, and the result can have any shape at all while its density stays exactly computable. The bound tightens as the posterior approaches the truth, so a more expressive posterior is a strictly better bound. The price is that every map must be invertible with a tractable Jacobian, which is why that literature is mostly a long sequence of clever ways to be invertible.

The importance-weighted bound

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

The ELBO is one sample’s worth of a lower bound. Draw several samples of the latent variable and average their likelihood ratios before taking the logarithm — the average of the ratios, not the average of the logs — and you get a bound that is provably tighter, approaching the true likelihood as the number of samples grows. The catch is instructive: a tighter bound is a different objective, and optimising the tighter one well can leave you with a worse model by the measure you actually care about. Every improvement in this family trades statistical tightness against where the gradient pressure lands.

Part IV — The classical answer, and why it stops working

4 · Expectation maximisation

You are standing on a foggy hillside and you can only walk due east or due north. Neither direction climbs all the way to the summit, but alternating them — east until it stops rising, then north until it stops rising, and again — will get you to one. The E step walks east, the M step walks north, and the guarantee that the likelihood never decreases is exactly the guarantee that each leg refuses to lose height. You may not reach the highest summit on the range. You will always reach the top of the valley you started in.

We now want

\[ \theta^*, q^* = \arg\max_{\theta, q} \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\log\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)} \]

Two blocks of unknowns, so alternate. Hold \(\theta\) fixed and find the best \(q\); that is the E step. Hold \(q\) fixed and find the best \(\theta\); that is the M step. Repeating this is the EM algorithm, and it can be shown that \(L(\theta_{t+1}) \ge L(\theta_t)\): every full cycle improves the actual likelihood, not just the bound.

For a Gaussian mixture the E step has a closed form and a satisfying one: the best \(q\) is the true posterior,

\[ q^*(z\mid x) = P_\theta(z\mid x) = \frac{P_\theta(x\mid z)\,P_\theta(z)}{P_\theta(x)} \]

which is just Bayes' rule with the mixture weights in the numerator. Then the M step differentiates with respect to \(\alpha\), \(\mu\), \(\Sigma\) and solves. Beautiful — and it works only because \(P_\theta(z\mid x)\) is available in closed form. Put a neural network where the GMM's components were, and Bayes' rule asks you for a normalising constant that is an integral over the latent space. EM dies at exactly the place the problem began.

One E step by hand shows what "closed form" buys. Two equally weighted one-dimensional Gaussians, unit variance, means 0 and 2; one data point, \(x = 1.5\). Bayes' rule says the posterior over the component is proportional to weight times likelihood:

\[ q^*(z{=}2\mid x) = \frac{e^{-(1.5-2)^2/2}}{e^{-(1.5-0)^2/2} + e^{-(1.5-2)^2/2}} = \frac{e^{-0.125}}{e^{-1.125} + e^{-0.125}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-1}} \approx 0.73 \]

The point belongs 73% to the right-hand component and 27% to the left. That "responsibility" is the soft assignment the M step then uses as a weight when it re-estimates each mean. The calculation needed a sum over two components in the denominator. Replace the two components by a neural network and the denominator becomes an integral over every possible \(z\).

Question  4.1 GMM with weights \(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\), unit variances, means 0 and 2. What is the responsibility of the mean-2 component for \(x = 1.5\)?

Answer \(e^{-0.125}/(e^{-1.125} + e^{-0.125}) = 1/(1 + e^{-1}) \approx 0.73\).

Question  4.2 In EM for a GMM, what does the M step do with the responsibilities?

Answer Uses them as soft weights: each component’s weight, mean and covariance are re-estimated as responsibility-weighted averages over the data.

Question  4.3 State the two steps of EM in terms of the ELBO.

Answer E step: \(q_{t+1} = \arg\max_q J_{\theta_t}(q)\) with \(\theta\) fixed. M step: \(\theta_{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta J_\theta(q_{t+1})\) with \(q\) fixed. Each step cannot decrease the bound, and EM guarantees \(L(\theta_{t+1}) \ge L(\theta_t)\).

Question  4.4 Why does EM fail for a neural latent-variable model?

Answer The E step requires evaluating \(P_\theta(z\mid x) = P_\theta(x,z)/P_\theta(x)\), and \(P_\theta(x)\) is the intractable integral over \(z\). For a GMM that normaliser is a finite sum; with neural components it is an integral nobody can do, so the E step has no closed form.

Part V — The trick that makes it trainable

5 · A gradient you cannot take

Here is the trouble in one sentence: you cannot backpropagate through a dice roll. The randomness sits inside the computation, and an operation that samples has no derivative — there is no meaningful answer to “how does this output change if I nudge the seed”. So the obvious way of estimating the gradient breaks, and the whole method is stuck at the first step.

So we keep the ELBO and give up on exact EM: let the encoder network output the parameters of \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) rather than the density itself. Now differentiate with respect to the encoder's weights \(\varphi\). One term is harmless (the KL has a closed form). The other is

\[ \nabla_\varphi \mathbb{E}_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\log P_\theta(x\mid z) \]

and it is a trap. Write the expectation as an integral and push the gradient inside:

\[ \nabla_\psi \mathbb{E}_{P_\psi(v)} f_\psi(v) = \int_v (\nabla_\psi f_\psi(v)) P_\psi(v)\,dv + \int_v (\nabla_\psi P_\psi(v)) f_\psi(v)\,dv \]

The first integral is an expectation and can be estimated by sampling. The second is not an expectation of anything — it contains the gradient of a density with respect to its own parameters, so a plain sample average cannot stand in for it. The notes conclude that the gradient "can't be computed".

Beyond the notes — a correction to page 55.

That conclusion is too strong, and the reason is worth owning, because the same move is the engine of the RLHF essay. Every density satisfies \(\nabla_\psi P_\psi = P_\psi\,\nabla_\psi\log P_\psi\). Substitute it into the second integral and it becomes an expectation:

\[ \int_v (\nabla_\psi P_\psi(v))\,f_\psi(v)\,dv = \mathbb{E}_{P_\psi(v)}\left[f_\psi(v)\,\nabla_\psi\log P_\psi(v)\right] \]

That is the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator: unbiased, and computable from samples. Its problem is not existence but variance. It multiplies a whole log-likelihood by a noisy direction and learns which way is better only by comparing many rolls of the dice. The trick below is preferred because it is far less noisy, not because it is the only option.

6 · Move the randomness outside

The fix is not to remove the randomness but to deport it. Stop asking the network for a random number. Ask it for the dial settings, and turn a fixed, external random number into the sample with arithmetic: multiply by the scale, add the shift. The randomness now happens at the edge of the graph where nothing needs differentiating, and every operation the gradient has to cross is addition and multiplication. The distribution of the output is unchanged. Only its parentage has moved.

The repair is one of the neatest moves in the subject. Do not sample \(z\) from a distribution that depends on \(\varphi\). Sample from something that does not, and push the parameter dependence into a function. Let \(\epsilon \sim P_\epsilon\) be a fixed source of noise, independent of \(\varphi\), and write \(z = g(\epsilon)\). Then by the law of the unconscious statistician,

\[ \mathbb{E}_{P_\varphi(z\mid x)} f(z) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon} f\!\left(g(\epsilon)\right) \approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} f\!\left(g(\epsilon_j)\right) \]

and the gradient now flows through \(g\), which is an ordinary differentiable function of \(\varphi\). Concretely, if \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(z; \mu_\varphi(x), \Sigma_\varphi(x))\), take \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and set

(The notes write \(\Sigma\epsilon\). That sample would have covariance \(\Sigma^2\), not \(\Sigma\); the scale that multiplies \(\epsilon\) is a square root of the covariance, such as its Cholesky factor.)

\[ z = \mu_\varphi(x) + \Sigma_\varphi(x)^{1/2}\,\epsilon \;\overset{\text{diagonal}}{=}\; \mu_\varphi(x) + \sigma_\varphi(x)\odot\epsilon \]

which has exactly the intended distribution. The noise is drawn outside the network; the network only supplies the shift and the scale. That is the reparameterization trick, and without it a VAE is not trainable by ordinary backpropagation.

Turn the handle once. The encoder says \(\mu = 2\), \(\sigma = 0.5\); the noise source rolls \(\epsilon = -1.2\). Then \(z = 2 + 0.5 \times (-1.2) = 1.4\), and the two derivatives the backward pass needs are just \(\partial z/\partial\mu = 1\) and \(\partial z/\partial\sigma = \epsilon = -1.2\). No density was differentiated; a multiplication and an addition were.

The Gaussian is not special. For any scalar variable with an invertible CDF \(F\), feed uniform noise through the inverse: \(z = F^{-1}(\epsilon)\) with \(\epsilon \sim U[0,1]\). An exponential with rate \(\lambda\), for instance, is \(z = -\log(1-\epsilon)/\lambda\). The pattern is always the same: fixed noise in, differentiable map out.

Question  5.1 Differentiating \(\mathbb{E}_{P_\psi(v)}f_\psi(v)\) under the integral gives two terms. Which one is not directly a sample average?

Answer \(\int_v (\nabla_\psi P_\psi(v))\,f_\psi(v)\,dv\): it weights \(f\) by the gradient of the density, not by the density itself.

Question  5.2 What identity turns \(\int (\nabla_\psi P_\psi) f\,dv\) into an expectation?

Answer The log-derivative trick, \(\nabla_\psi P_\psi = P_\psi\nabla_\psi\log P_\psi\), giving \(\mathbb{E}_{P_\psi}[f\,\nabla_\psi\log P_\psi]\) — the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator.

Question  5.3 If the score-function estimator exists, why do VAEs use reparameterization instead?

Answer Variance. The score-function estimator is unbiased but very noisy; the reparameterized gradient passes through the decoder’s own slope and is far less noisy.

Question  5.4 State the reparameterization identity that fixes this.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_{P_\varphi(v)}f_\varphi(v) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon}f_\varphi(g(\epsilon))\) when \(v = g(\epsilon)\) and \(\epsilon\) is independent of \(\varphi\). The parameter dependence moves out of the sampling distribution and into the deterministic map \(g\), so the expectation is taken over a fixed distribution.

Question  5.5 Write the reparameterized sample for a diagonal Gaussian posterior.

Answer \(z = \mu_\varphi(x) + \sigma_\varphi(x)\odot\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) drawn outside the network.

Question  5.6 For a full-covariance Gaussian, why is the reparameterization \(z = \mu + \Sigma^{1/2}\epsilon\) and not \(z = \mu + \Sigma\epsilon\)?

Answer \(\mathrm{Cov}(A\epsilon) = AA^\top\). With \(A = \Sigma\) the covariance would be \(\Sigma^2\); you need \(AA^\top = \Sigma\), e.g. the Cholesky factor.

Question  5.7 \(\mu = 2\), \(\sigma = 0.5\), \(\epsilon = -1.2\). What are \(z\), \(\partial z/\partial\mu\) and \(\partial z/\partial\sigma\)?

Answer \(z = 1.4\), \(\partial z/\partial\mu = 1\), \(\partial z/\partial\sigma = \epsilon = -1.2\).

Question  5.8 How do you reparameterize a scalar variable with invertible CDF \(F\)?

Answer \(z = F^{-1}(\epsilon)\) with \(\epsilon \sim U[0,1]\) — the inverse-CDF method. E.g. an exponential: \(z = -\log(1-\epsilon)/\lambda\).

Part VI — Assembling the thing people actually train

7 · Splitting the ELBO into two losses

A lower bound sounds like a compromise, so it is worth restating what it is. The quantity you wanted was the evidence — how likely the data is under your model — and you cannot compute it. What you get instead is an invoice with a discount: something computable that is always at most the evidence, where the shortfall is precisely the distance between your guess about the hidden variable and the truth. Improve the guess and the discount shrinks. The bound is not an apology; it is a measurement of your own ignorance.

Factor the joint as \(P_\theta(x,z) = P_\theta(x\mid z)P_\theta(z)\) and the ELBO splits into two interpretable pieces:

\[ J_\theta(q_\varphi) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\log P_\theta(x\mid z)}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{KL}\!\left(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,P_\theta(z)\right)}_{\text{regularisation}} \]

To make the first term concrete, choose the decoder's likelihood. Put \(P_\theta(x\mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \hat{x}_\theta(z), I)\), so the decoder outputs a mean. Then

\[ \log P_\theta(x\mid z) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2 - \tfrac{d}{2}\log 2\pi \]

and the reconstruction term becomes squared error. Meanwhile the KL is available in closed form, because both sides are Gaussian and the prior is standard normal. If \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\varphi(x), \Sigma_\varphi(x))\) and \(P_\theta(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), then

\[ D_{KL} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\text{tr}\,\Sigma_\varphi(x) + \|\mu_\varphi(x)\|_2^2 - k - \log|\Sigma_\varphi(x)|\right] \;\overset{\text{diagonal}}{=}\; \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\sigma_j^2 + \mu_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2\right) \]

Two terms, two jobs. The decoder only ever sees the reconstruction error, because the KL contains no \(\theta\). The encoder sees both, and has to satisfy two demands at once: encode informatively, but keep the encoding near the prior. Almost everything that goes wrong with VAEs is a consequence of that tension.

(The notes drop the overall \(\tfrac12\) on every term but the log-determinant. It changes no minimiser, but it changes the number you report, and it silently doubles the KL’s weight against the reconstruction.)

One dimension, one number. An encoder that outputs \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.5\) pays \(\tfrac12(0.25 + 1 - 1 - \log 0.25) = \tfrac12(0.25 + 1.386) \approx 0.82\) nats for being that far from a standard normal: a little for the shifted mean, most of it for being too narrow.

Question  6.1 Why does the reconstruction term turn into mean squared error?

Answer Because we choose a Gaussian decoder likelihood \(P_\theta(x\mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \hat{x}_\theta(z), I)\). The log of that density contains \(-\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2\) plus constants, so maximising it is minimising squared error. The choice of likelihood is what picks the loss; MSE is not an independent decision.

Question  6.2 Why does the decoder's gradient not include the KL term?

Answer The KL is between \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) and the prior \(P_\theta(z)\), and in the standard setup the prior is a fixed \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) with no learned parameters. So \(\nabla_\theta\) of the KL is zero and the decoder trains purely on reconstruction.

Question  6.3 What does the forward pass of a VAE actually do, step by step?

Answer Pass \(x_i\) through the encoder to get \(\mu_\varphi(x_i)\) and \(\Sigma_\varphi(x_i)\); draw \(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_M\) from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) outside the networks; form \(z_j = \mu_\varphi(x_i) + \sigma_\varphi(x_i)\odot\epsilon_j\); push each \(z_j\) through the decoder to get \(\hat{x}_\theta(z_j)\); average \(\|x_i - \hat{x}_\theta(z_j)\|_2^2\) over \(j\); add the KL.

The KL between two Gaussians, derived once

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

The notes hand you the formula. It is worth seeing why it has exactly the shape it has. Compare a Gaussian with mean and variance to a standard normal, and ask what makes two bell curves different: only their position and their width. So the divergence has one term per difference. The mean term is the squared distance of the mean from zero. The variance terms are the variance, minus one, minus the logarithm of the variance — and that trio is doing real work. A variance above one is punished, a variance squeezed toward zero is punished harder, and the minimum sits quietly at one. Completing the square is how you get there; the interpretation is that the bound is charging you for every way your guess fails to be an ordinary bell curve.

Question  6.4 Write \(D_{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\) in one dimension.

Answer \(\tfrac12(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)\).

Question  6.5 In the 1-D Gaussian KL to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), at what \(\sigma^2\) is the variance part \(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\) minimised, and what happens as \(\sigma^2 \to 0\)?

Answer Minimised at \(\sigma^2 = 1\) (derivative \(1 - 1/\sigma^2 = 0\)). As \(\sigma^2 \to 0\), \(-\log\sigma^2 \to \infty\): an over-confident encoder is punished without limit.

Question  6.6 An encoder outputs \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.5\). What is its KL to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)?

Answer \(\tfrac12(0.25 + 1 - 1 - \log 0.25) \approx 0.82\) nats.

Question  6.7 With decoder \(\mathcal{N}(x;\hat{x}_\theta(z), I)\), what exactly is \(\log P_\theta(x\mid z)\)?

Answer \(-\tfrac12\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2 - \tfrac{d}{2}\log 2\pi\): squared error, halved, plus a constant.

Part VII — Using it, and the way it fails

8 · Sampling and embedding

A trained VAE is used by walking around its middle. One road runs from the world into the code, the other from the code back into the world, and the network’s two halves are those two roads. To generate, start in the code space and walk outward. To embed, walk inward and keep the address instead of the destination. Everything downstream — retrieval, editing, interpolation between two faces — is a variation on keeping that address.

A trained VAE is used in two directions. To generate, skip the encoder: draw \(z_{new} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and push it through the decoder. This works precisely because the KL term has been pulling every \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) towards \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) — if training succeeded, the prior is a valid description of the latent space, so sampling from it samples from the model.

To embed, skip the decoder: push \(x_{test}\) through the encoder and take \(\mu_\varphi(x_{test})\) as a \(k\)-dimensional feature vector for a \(d\)-dimensional input. The bottleneck is doing the compression you asked for.

Question  7.1 Why is “sample \(z\) from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and decode” a valid way to generate?

Answer Because the KL term has driven every \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) towards the prior \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). If training succeeded, the marginal latent distribution is the prior, so drawing from the prior is drawing from the model's latent distribution, and decoding maps it back to data space.

Question  7.2 What are the two ways to read an embedding out of a trained VAE?

Answer Either take the mean \(z = \mu_\varphi(x_{test})\) — the deterministic choice — or sample \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\varphi(x_{test}), \Sigma_\varphi(x_{test}))\). The first is what people usually mean by "the embedding".

9 · Posterior collapse, and two ways out

Imagine a filing clerk given two instructions: the files must be findable, and every file must be entered on the standard form. If compliance is enforced hard enough, the clerk eventually stops reading the files and enters the same standard form for all of them. The archive is now immaculate and useless. That is posterior collapse. The encoder has obeyed the prior so thoroughly that the code says nothing about the input, and the decoder — handed the identical description every time — can only learn the average of the dataset.

Now the failure. The KL term is a regulariser, and a regulariser with no weight is a demand, not a preference. If it dominates, then for every \(x\) the encoder is pushed to output exactly the prior: \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) for all inputs. The latent code then carries no information about \(x\) at all, and the decoder, receiving the same distribution whatever it is shown, can only learn the average of the dataset. This is posterior collapse.

Two standard repairs follow.

Question  7.3 What is posterior collapse?

Answer The encoder outputs the prior for every input, \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) for all \(x\), so the latent code carries no information about \(x\).

Question  7.4 Under posterior collapse, what can the decoder learn, and why?

Answer Only the average of the dataset: it receives the same distribution of \(z\) whatever the input, so it cannot tell inputs apart.

Turning the dial: the \(\beta\)-VAE

Every regularised loss in machine learning has the shape "cost plus a constant times a penalty". The VAE is the odd one out: its constant is fixed at one by the derivation. Put the dial back:

\[ \hat{J} = \|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2 + \beta\,D_{KL}\!\left(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,P(z)\right) \]

(a loss to minimise, so the signs have flipped). The notes keep \(0 \le \beta \le 1\): lower \(\beta\) gives sharper reconstructions but a latent space the prior no longer describes, so generation suffers; higher \(\beta\) gives cleaner sampling and edges toward collapse.

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

The paper that named the \(\beta\)-VAE used \(\beta > 1\) on purpose. Squeezing harder towards an isotropic prior pressures the encoder to give each latent dimension one independent factor of variation (pose, lighting, size), which is called disentanglement. The dial is useful in both directions.

Question  7.5 What does a VAE look like once you stop calling the KL a bound and call it a penalty?

Answer A regularised auto-encoder: reconstruction cost plus a weighted penalty on the latent code. The ELBO's two terms are “loss plus regularisation”, with the implicit weight fixed at 1. Writing that weight explicitly as \(\beta\) gives the \(\beta\)-VAE and makes the trade-off tunable.

Making the code discrete: the VQ-VAE

The other repair abandons the Gaussian. Keep a learned dictionary of \(M\) vectors, \(L = \{z_1, \dots, z_M\}\) with \(z_j \in \mathbb{R}^k\). The encoder outputs a grid of \(p\) vectors, \(\hat{z}_e(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times p}\), and each column is snapped to its nearest dictionary entry:

\[ z_q(x_i) = z_{j^*}, \qquad j^* = \arg\min_{j} \|z_e(x_i) - z_j\|_2^2 \]

The decoder sees only the snapped vectors, and the notes train everything on reconstruction plus a term pulling code and encoder output together:

\[ J = \|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z_q)\|_2^2 + \|z_e(x) - z_q(x)\|_2^2 \]

Beyond the notes — not in pages 43–71.

Two details make this actually train. First, \(\arg\min\) has no gradient, so nothing would reach the encoder. The fix is the straight-through estimator: in the backward pass, pretend quantisation was the identity and copy the decoder's gradient with respect to \(z_q\) straight onto \(z_e\). Second, the single \(\|z_e - z_q\|^2\) is split with a stop-gradient \(\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]\) so that each side moves for its own reason:

\[ \|\mathrm{sg}[z_e] - z_q\|_2^2 \;+\; \beta\,\|z_e - \mathrm{sg}[z_q]\|_2^2 \]

The first term moves the dictionary toward the encoder outputs; the second, the commitment loss with \(\beta\) around 0.25, stops the encoder's outputs from wandering off faster than the dictionary can follow.

Why does this dodge collapse? With a uniform prior over \(M\) codes and a deterministic snap, the KL term is the constant \(\log M\): there is no gradient dragging every code toward the prior, because there is no gradient from the prior at all. The cost appears at generation time. You cannot draw from "a uniform choice of code per position" and get an image, because neighbouring positions are correlated. So a second generative model is fitted to the codes the encoder actually produces — the notes suggest a GMM; the original paper used an autoregressive PixelCNN — and its samples are decoded.

Question  7.6 Write the \(\beta\)-VAE loss.

Answer \(\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2 + \beta\,D_{KL}(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,P(z))\), with \(\beta\) a hyperparameter (the plain VAE is \(\beta = 1\)).

Question  7.7 In a \(\beta\)-VAE, what does lowering \(\beta\) trade for what?

Answer Sharper reconstructions in exchange for worse generation, because \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) drifts away from the prior you sample from.

Question  7.8 Why did the original \(\beta\)-VAE paper use \(\beta > 1\)?

Answer For disentanglement: stronger pressure toward an isotropic prior pushes each latent dimension to capture one independent factor of variation.

Question  7.9 In a VQ-VAE, how is an encoder output \(z_e\) turned into a code?

Answer Snapped to its nearest dictionary vector: \(z_q = z_{j^*}\), \(j^* = \arg\min_j\|z_e - z_j\|_2^2\).

Question  7.10 Quantisation is an \(\arg\min\), which has no gradient. How does the encoder of a VQ-VAE get trained?

Answer The straight-through estimator: the backward pass treats quantisation as the identity and copies \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_q\) onto \(z_e\).

Question  7.11 In the VQ-VAE loss \(\|\mathrm{sg}[z_e] - z_q\|^2 + \beta\|z_e - \mathrm{sg}[z_q]\|^2\), what does each term update?

Answer The first moves the dictionary vectors toward the encoder outputs; the second (commitment) keeps the encoder outputs close to their chosen codes.

Question  7.12 How do you generate new samples from a trained VQ-VAE?

Answer Fit a separate generative model to the discrete codes the encoder produces (a GMM in the notes, an autoregressive PixelCNN in the original), sample codes from it, and decode them.

Question  7.13 Why does a VQ-VAE with a uniform code prior not suffer posterior collapse in the usual way?

Answer Its KL term is the constant \(\log M\), so no gradient pulls the codes toward the prior. The price is that a separate prior over codes must be learned for generation.

Part VIII — Footnotes to the notes

10 · Five places the notes need correcting

Lecture notes compress, and a few compressions here would cost you marks or a working model. Each appears where it bites, above; collected:

1. "Can't be computed" (page 55). The second gradient term is computable by the log-derivative trick. It is noisy, which is the real reason for the reparameterization trick.

2. \(z = \mu + \Sigma\epsilon\) (pages 57–60). Should be \(\mu + \Sigma^{1/2}\epsilon\). For the usual diagonal posterior that is \(\mu + \sigma\odot\epsilon\), with the encoder outputting standard deviations, not variances.

3. The Gaussian KL (page 61). The whole bracket carries the \(\tfrac12\): \(\tfrac12[\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma + \|\mu\|^2 - k - \log|\Sigma|]\).

4. "\(\propto -\|x - \hat{x}\|^2\)" (page 59). The log-likelihood is \(-\tfrac12\|x-\hat{x}\|^2\) plus a constant, which is affine rather than proportional. Harmless for the argmax, but the \(\tfrac12\) sets the balance between reconstruction and KL, so it matters for what a "\(\beta = 1\)" VAE actually is.

5. The VQ-VAE objective (page 70). As written it cannot train the encoder, because the gradient stops at the \(\arg\min\). It needs the straight-through estimator and the stop-gradient split into codebook and commitment terms.

Question  8.1 In practice, what does a VAE encoder usually output for a diagonal Gaussian posterior, and why?

Answer \(\mu\) and \(\log\sigma^2\) (log-variance): it is unconstrained, so any real network output is valid, and \(\sigma = e^{\frac12\log\sigma^2}\) is positive automatically.
