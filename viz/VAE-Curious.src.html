Variational
autoencoders,
for the very curious

How to squeeze a photograph through a narrow pipe and get it back — and why the pipe needs a little noise to be trainable at all

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. Each box is numbered, the questions are collected at the end, and the same list ships as a plain-text file. On screen, each Part ends with a deck that deals its cards to you one at a time.

Everything here follows pages 43–71 of an IIT Madras generative-AI course. The argument runs: we write down a model with a hidden variable, discover the likelihood is intractable, bound it, find that the bound has a gradient we cannot take, and fix that with one line of algebra.

Part I — A model with something hidden in it

1 · Latent variables

Suppose someone hands you a photograph of a cake and asks you to write down the recipe. The photograph is what you observed; the recipe is what you did not. Many recipes would produce a cake that photographs identically, so the recipe is not recoverable in principle — and that ambiguity is not an inconvenience to be tidied away later. It is the object of study. A latent variable model is the attempt to have a recipe, and to be honest about the fact that the photograph does not determine it.

A latent variable model says that what you observe is a shadow of something you do not observe. Write \(z\) for the hidden thing and \(x\) for the observation. The model's density is a marginal:

\[ P_\theta(x) = \int_z P_\theta(x, z) \, dz \]

with \(z\) continuous, or a sum \(\sum_z P_\theta(x,z)\) if it is discrete. The integral is the whole difficulty of this essay: it is why the likelihood has no closed form, and every technique below is a way of living without it.

What \(z\) means depends on its type. Make it discrete, taking one of \(M\) values, and asking for \(z_i\) given \(x_i\) is a clustering question — which of the \(M\) groups does this point belong to. That is where Gaussian mixture models and k-means live. Make it continuous in a much smaller space than \(x\), say \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k \ll d\), and asking for \(z_i\) given \(x_i\) is a compression question: find a short description that still explains the long observation.

Question  1.1 Write down the defining equation of a latent variable model, and say what makes it hard.

Answer \(P_\theta(x) = \int_z P_\theta(x,z)\,dz\), a marginal over the joint. The difficulty is that the integral over the latent space is generally intractable — there is no closed form, so \(\log P_\theta(x)\) cannot be evaluated directly.

Question  1.2 The same model gives clustering in one case and feature extraction in another. What changes?

Answer The type and size of \(z\). Discrete \(z \in \{1,\dots,M\}\) assigns each \(x_i\) to one of \(M\) groups — that is clustering. Continuous \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k \ll d\) gives each \(x_i\) a real-valued code — that is an embedding, and the machinery is an auto-encoder.

Part II — From "match the distribution" to "maximise likelihood"

2 · Why maximum likelihood, of all things

Maximum likelihood is usually handed to you as a starting assumption, and then defended with a shrug about statistical efficiency. Watch it arrive here instead as a consequence. You asked to be close to the data under one particular ruler, and that ruler turned out to be a difference of two terms, one of which knows nothing about your model. Cross the term out and what is left is “make the observed data likely”. Nobody chose it. It fell out of the ruler.

The stated goal is to make the model's distribution match the data's. Measure the mismatch with forward KL and minimise:

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \int_x P_x(x) \log\frac{P_x(x)}{P_\theta(x)} \, dx \]

Split the logarithm. The first piece is \(\int_x P_x \log P_x\), which contains no \(\theta\) at all — it is a property of the data, a constant as far as the optimisation is concerned. What is left is

\[ \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \left[-\int_x P_x(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx\right] = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x) \]

which is maximum likelihood estimation. This is worth pausing on, because maximum likelihood is usually taught as a starting assumption. It is not. It is what "minimise the KL divergence to the data" reduces to once the constants fall out. The whole rest of the essay is a fight with one term: \(\log P_\theta(x)\), which contains an integral nobody can do.

Question  2.1 Show why minimising \(D_{KL}(P_x\|P_\theta)\) is the same as maximum likelihood.

Answer Expand the KL: \(\int P_x \log P_x - \int P_x \log P_\theta\). The first integral involves only \(P_x\), so it is constant with respect to \(\theta\) and cannot affect the argmin. Dropping it leaves minimising \(-\mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x)\), i.e. maximising \(\mathbb{E}_{P_x}\log P_\theta(x)\).

Question  2.2 Which term of the KL expansion vanishes, and what is the general lesson?

Answer The entropy term \(\int_x P_x(x)\log P_x(x)\,dx\) — it does not depend on the parameters. The lesson: when a divergence to a fixed distribution is minimised, only the cross term matters, which is why MLE keeps appearing in generative modelling.

Part III — A bound you can actually compute

3 · Jensen, and the appearance of \(q\)

Let \(L(\theta) = \log P_\theta(x)\). Multiply inside the integral by \(q(z\mid x)/q(z\mid x)\) for any density \(q\) over the latent space — free, since it equals one:

\[ L(\theta) = \log \int_z q(z\mid x)\left[\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right] dz = \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\left[\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right] \]

Now apply Jensen's inequality, \(\log \mathbb{E}[\cdot] \ge \mathbb{E}\log[\cdot]\), which holds because \(\log\) is concave. The inequality points the useful way:

\[ L(\theta) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\log\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)} \;=:\; J_\theta(q) \]

\(J_\theta(q)\) is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO, and it is a function of two independent things: the model parameters \(\theta\), and the density \(q\) you chose. That second slot is the free variable, and the entire method lives in the choice you put there. The bound becomes exact precisely when \(q\) equals the true posterior \(P_\theta(z\mid x)\) — which we cannot compute, which is why it is still a bound.

Question  3.1 Why does Jensen give a lower bound rather than an upper one?

Answer Because \(\log\) is concave, so \(\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}\log X\). The quantity we can compute, \(\mathbb{E}\log\) of the ratio, sits below the quantity we want, \(\log\) of the evidence. Concavity fixes the direction.

Question  3.2 What is \(q(z\mid x)\), and when does the bound become tight?

Answer It is any density over the latent variable given the observation — the variational latent posterior. It is a free choice we introduce; it is not the true posterior. The ELBO is exact exactly when \(q(z\mid x) = P_\theta(z\mid x)\), which is the quantity we were trying to avoid needing.

Part IV — The classical answer, and why it stops working

4 · Expectation maximisation

You are standing on a foggy hillside and you can only walk due east or due north. Neither direction climbs all the way to the summit, but alternating them — east until it stops rising, then north until it stops rising, and again — will get you to one. The E step walks east, the M step walks north, and the guarantee that the likelihood never decreases is exactly the guarantee that each leg refuses to lose height. You may not reach the highest summit on the range. You will always reach the top of the valley you started in.

We now want

\[ \theta^*, q^* = \arg\max_{\theta, q} \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\log\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)} \]

Two blocks of unknowns, so alternate. Hold \(\theta\) fixed and find the best \(q\); that is the E step. Hold \(q\) fixed and find the best \(\theta\); that is the M step. Repeating this is the EM algorithm, and it can be shown that \(L(\theta_{t+1}) \ge L(\theta_t)\): every full cycle improves the actual likelihood, not just the bound.

For a Gaussian mixture the E step has a closed form and a satisfying one: the best \(q\) is the true posterior,

\[ q^*(z\mid x) = P_\theta(z\mid x) = \frac{P_\theta(x\mid z)\,P_\theta(z)}{P_\theta(x)} \]

which is just Bayes' rule with the mixture weights in the numerator. Then the M step differentiates with respect to \(\alpha\), \(\mu\), \(\Sigma\) and solves. Beautiful — and it works only because \(P_\theta(z\mid x)\) is available in closed form. Put a neural network where the GMM's components were, and Bayes' rule asks you for a normalising constant that is an integral over the latent space. EM dies at exactly the place the problem began.

Question  4.1 State the two steps of EM in terms of the ELBO.

Answer E step: \(q_{t+1} = \arg\max_q J_{\theta_t}(q)\) with \(\theta\) fixed. M step: \(\theta_{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta J_\theta(q_{t+1})\) with \(q\) fixed. Each step cannot decrease the bound, and EM guarantees \(L(\theta_{t+1}) \ge L(\theta_t)\).

Question  4.2 Why does EM fail for a neural latent-variable model?

Answer The E step requires evaluating \(P_\theta(z\mid x) = P_\theta(x,z)/P_\theta(x)\), and \(P_\theta(x)\) is the intractable integral over \(z\). For a GMM that normaliser is a finite sum; with neural components it is an integral nobody can do, so the E step has no closed form.

Part V — The trick that makes it trainable

5 · A gradient you cannot take

Here is the trouble in one sentence: you cannot backpropagate through a dice roll. The randomness sits inside the computation, and an operation that samples has no derivative — there is no meaningful answer to “how does this output change if I nudge the seed”. So the gradient we need does not exist as something a computer can estimate, and the whole method is stuck at the first step.

So we keep the ELBO and give up on exact EM: let the encoder network output the parameters of \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) rather than the density itself. Now differentiate with respect to the encoder's weights \(\varphi\). One term is harmless (the KL has a closed form). The other is

\[ \nabla_\varphi \mathbb{E}_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\log P_\theta(x\mid z) \]

and it is a trap. Write the expectation as an integral and push the gradient inside:

\[ \nabla_\psi \mathbb{E}_{P_\psi(v)} f_\psi(v) = \int_v (\nabla_\psi f_\psi(v)) P_\psi(v)\,dv + \int_v (\nabla_\psi P_\psi(v)) f_\psi(v)\,dv \]

The first integral is an expectation and can be estimated by sampling. The second is not an expectation of anything — it contains the gradient of a density with respect to its own parameters, and there is no way to sample it. So the gradient we need does not exist as an estimable quantity.

6 · Move the randomness outside

The fix is not to remove the randomness but to deport it. Stop asking the network for a random number. Ask it for the dial settings, and turn a fixed, external random number into the sample with arithmetic: multiply by the scale, add the shift. The randomness now happens at the edge of the graph where nothing needs differentiating, and every operation the gradient has to cross is addition and multiplication. The distribution of the output is unchanged. Only its parentage has moved.

The repair is one of the neatest moves in the subject. Do not sample \(z\) from a distribution that depends on \(\varphi\). Sample from something that does not, and push the parameter dependence into a function. Let \(\epsilon \sim P_\epsilon\) be a fixed source of noise, independent of \(\varphi\), and write \(z = g(\epsilon)\). Then by the law of the unconscious statistician,

\[ \mathbb{E}_{P_\varphi(z\mid x)} f(z) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon} f\!\left(g(\epsilon)\right) \approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} f\!\left(g(\epsilon_j)\right) \]

and the gradient now flows through \(g\), which is an ordinary differentiable function of \(\varphi\). Concretely, if \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(z; \mu_\varphi(x), \Sigma_\varphi(x))\), take \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and set

\[ z = \mu_\varphi(x) + \Sigma_\varphi(x)\,\epsilon \]

which has exactly the intended distribution. The noise is drawn outside the network; the network only supplies the shift and the scale. That is the reparameterization trick, and without it a VAE is not trainable by ordinary backpropagation.

Question  5.1 Precisely why can \(\nabla_\varphi\mathbb{E}_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\log P_\theta(x\mid z)\) not be estimated by sampling?

Answer Differentiating the integral produces two terms. One is \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\varphi\log P_\theta(x\mid z)]\), which is estimable. The other is \(\int (\nabla_\varphi q_\varphi(z\mid x))\log P_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz\), which is not an expectation — it weights a log-likelihood by the gradient of a density — so no sample average can stand in for it.

Question  5.2 State the reparameterization identity that fixes this.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_{P_\varphi(v)}f_\varphi(v) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon}f_\varphi(g(\epsilon))\) when \(v = g(\epsilon)\) and \(\epsilon\) is independent of \(\varphi\). The parameter dependence moves out of the sampling distribution and into the deterministic map \(g\), so the expectation is taken over a fixed distribution.

Question  5.3 For a Gaussian posterior, what does the reparameterized sample look like, and where is the randomness?

Answer \(z = \mu_\varphi(x) + \Sigma_\varphi(x)\,\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). The randomness lives entirely in \(\epsilon\), drawn outside the network; the encoder only outputs \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\), which is what lets gradients reach \(\varphi\).

Part VI — Assembling the thing people actually train

7 · Splitting the ELBO into two losses

A lower bound sounds like a compromise, so it is worth restating what it is. The quantity you wanted was the evidence — how likely the data is under your model — and you cannot compute it. What you get instead is an invoice with a discount: something computable that is always at most the evidence, where the shortfall is precisely the distance between your guess about the hidden variable and the truth. Improve the guess and the discount shrinks. The bound is not an apology; it is a measurement of your own ignorance.

Factor the joint as \(P_\theta(x,z) = P_\theta(x\mid z)P_\theta(z)\) and the ELBO splits into two interpretable pieces:

\[ J_\theta(q_\varphi) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\log P_\theta(x\mid z)}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{KL}\!\left(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\,\|\,P_\theta(z)\right)}_{\text{regularisation}} \]

To make the first term concrete, choose the decoder's likelihood. Put \(P_\theta(x\mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \hat{x}_\theta(z), I)\), so the decoder outputs a mean. Then

\[ \log P_\theta(x\mid z) \propto -\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2 \]

and the reconstruction term becomes squared error. Meanwhile the KL is available in closed form, because both sides are Gaussian and the prior is standard normal. If \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\varphi(x), \Sigma_\varphi(x))\) and \(P_\theta(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), then

\[ D_{KL} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_\varphi(x)| - k + \text{tr}\!\left[\Sigma_\varphi(x)\right] + \|\mu_\varphi(x)\|_2^2 \]

Two terms, two jobs. The decoder only ever sees the reconstruction error, because the KL contains no \(\theta\). The encoder sees both, and has to satisfy two demands at once: encode informatively, but keep the encoding near the prior. Almost everything that goes wrong with VAEs is a consequence of that tension.

Question  6.1 Why does the reconstruction term turn into mean squared error?

Answer Because we choose a Gaussian decoder likelihood \(P_\theta(x\mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \hat{x}_\theta(z), I)\). The log of that density contains \(-\|x - \hat{x}_\theta(z)\|_2^2\) plus constants, so maximising it is minimising squared error. The choice of likelihood is what picks the loss; MSE is not an independent decision.

Question  6.2 Why does the decoder's gradient not include the KL term?

Answer The KL is between \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) and the prior \(P_\theta(z)\), and in the standard setup the prior is a fixed \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) with no learned parameters. So \(\nabla_\theta\) of the KL is zero and the decoder trains purely on reconstruction.

Question  6.3 What does the forward pass of a VAE actually do, step by step?

Answer Pass \(x_i\) through the encoder to get \(\mu_\varphi(x_i)\) and \(\Sigma_\varphi(x_i)\); draw \(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_M\) from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) outside the networks; form \(z_j = \mu_\varphi(x_i) + \Sigma_\varphi(x_i)\epsilon_j\); push each \(z_j\) through the decoder to get \(\hat{x}_\theta(z_j)\); average \(\|x_i - \hat{x}_\theta(z_j)\|_2^2\) over \(j\); add the KL.

Part VII — Using it, and the way it fails

8 · Sampling and embedding

A trained VAE is used by walking around its middle. One road runs from the world into the code, the other from the code back into the world, and the network’s two halves are those two roads. To generate, start in the code space and walk outward. To embed, walk inward and keep the address instead of the destination. Everything downstream — retrieval, editing, interpolation between two faces — is a variation on keeping that address.

A trained VAE is used in two directions. To generate, skip the encoder: draw \(z_{new} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and push it through the decoder. This works precisely because the KL term has been pulling every \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) towards \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) — if training succeeded, the prior is a valid description of the latent space, so sampling from it samples from the model.

To embed, skip the decoder: push \(x_{test}\) through the encoder and take \(\mu_\varphi(x_{test})\) as a \(k\)-dimensional feature vector for a \(d\)-dimensional input. The bottleneck is doing the compression you asked for.

9 · Posterior collapse, and two ways out

Imagine a filing clerk given two instructions: the files must be findable, and every file must be entered on the standard form. If compliance is enforced hard enough, the clerk eventually stops reading the files and enters the same standard form for all of them. The archive is now immaculate and useless. That is posterior collapse. The encoder has obeyed the prior so thoroughly that the code says nothing about the input, and the decoder — handed the identical description every time — can only learn the average of the dataset.

Now the failure. The KL term is a regulariser, and a regulariser with no weight is a demand, not a preference. If it dominates, then for every \(x\) the encoder is pushed to output exactly the prior: \(q_\varphi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) for all inputs. The latent code then carries no information about \(x\) at all, and the decoder, receiving the same distribution whatever it is shown, can only learn the average of the dataset. This is posterior collapse.

Two standard repairs. Weight the KL by a coefficient \(\beta\) and tune the trade-off explicitly — the \(\beta\)-VAE. Small \(\beta\) favours reconstruction at the cost of generation; large \(\beta\) favours a clean prior at the cost of blurry reconstructions. Or sidestep the continuous prior entirely: the VQ-VAE replaces the Gaussian posterior with vector quantisation, snapping each encoder column to its nearest entry in a learned dictionary (\(z_q(x_i) = z_{j^*}\) with \(j^* = \arg\min_j \|z_e(x_i) - z_j\|_2^2\)), and trains encoder, decoder and the dictionary together. A discrete latent cannot drift towards a Gaussian prior, so the collapse mode does not arise in the same way — but sampling then requires a separate model over the discrete codes, because the prior is no longer something you can just draw from.

Question  7.1 Why is \"sample \(z\) from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and decode"@ a valid way to generate?

Answer Because the KL term has driven every \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) towards the prior \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). If training succeeded, the marginal latent distribution is the prior, so drawing from the prior is drawing from the model's latent distribution, and decoding maps it back to data space.

Question  7.2 What are the two ways to read an embedding out of a trained VAE?

Answer Either take the mean \(z = \mu_\varphi(x_{test})\) — the deterministic choice — or sample \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\varphi(x_{test}), \Sigma_\varphi(x_{test}))\). The first is what people usually mean by "the embedding".

Question  7.3 Describe posterior collapse mechanically.

Answer The KL term forces \(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\) to equal the prior for every \(x\), so \(q_\varphi(z\mid x_i) = q_\varphi(z\mid x_j) = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) for any two inputs. The decoder receives samples from the same distribution regardless of the input and becomes unable to distinguish inputs — the latent variable has been switched off.

Question  7.4 What does a VAE look like once you stop calling the KL a bound and call it a penalty?

Answer A regularised auto-encoder: reconstruction cost plus a weighted penalty on the latent code. The ELBO's two terms are \"loss plus regularisation"@, with the implicit weight fixed at 1. Writing that weight explicitly as \(\beta\) gives the \(\beta\)-VAE and makes the trade-off tunable.

What this essay does not tell you

The notes stop at VQ-VAE and do not follow the thread into hierarchical priors, normalising flows as posteriors, or the importance-weighted bound — all of which are attempts to tighten the same inequality. They also present the KL between two Gaussians as a formula to be used, without deriving it once by completing the square. That derivation is a few lines and worth doing yourself if you want the formula to stay in your head rather than on a page.
