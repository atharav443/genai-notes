The mathematics
under generative AI,
for the very curious

Eight tools — averages, surprise, curvature, bell curves, Bayes, and two ways to differentiate a dice roll — that every other essay in this series assumes you already own

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each. A question that feels too easy today is doing its job if you still answer it in six months.

Read this one first if the GAN, VAE, diffusion or RLHF essays ever made you stop at a line and think "where did that come from?". Nothing here is specific to any one model. Everything here is used by all of them, and the last section maps each tool to the places it reappears. It goes beyond the lecture notes throughout; it is the background the notes take for granted.

Part I — Averages you can actually compute

1 · An expectation is a weighted average

A fairground game pays ₹10 with probability 0.2 and nothing otherwise. What is it worth to play? Not ₹10, and not zero: on average, ₹2 a go. That is all an expectation is — every outcome, weighted by how often it happens, added up.

\[ \mathbb{E}_{P}[h(x)] = \sum_x P(x)\,h(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \int P(x)\,h(x)\,dx \]

Two things sit inside: a distribution \(P\) that says how often, and a function \(h\) that says how much. The subscript on \(\mathbb{E}\) names the distribution, and it is the single most important piece of notation in this subject, because nearly every derivation in the series is a fight over which distribution an expectation is taken under. When you see \(\mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\) in the VAE or \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\) in RLHF, read it aloud: "averaged over samples from \(q\)", "averaged over what the policy does".

Question  1.1 A game pays 10 with probability 0.2 and 0 otherwise. What is its expected payout?

Answer \(0.2 \times 10 + 0.8 \times 0 = 2\).

Question  1.2 In \(\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(x)]\), what do \(P\) and \(h\) each contribute?

Answer \(P\) supplies the weights (how often each \(x\) occurs); \(h\) supplies the values being averaged.

2 · Replacing an integral with a sample average

The integral is usually impossible. The samples are usually free. The law of large numbers is the licence to swap one for the other: draw \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) independently from \(P\), and

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} h(x_i) \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}_P[h(x)] \quad\text{as } n \to \infty \]

This is Monte Carlo estimation, and every training loss you will ever compute is one. A minibatch loss is a sample average standing in for an expectation over the data.

How good is the stand-in? The average of \(n\) independent draws has standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), where \(\sigma\) is the spread of a single \(h(x)\). Read the square root as a tax: to halve the error you need four times as many samples, and to cut it by ten, a hundred times as many. That is why, later, so much effort goes into estimators with a smaller \(\sigma\) rather than into more samples.

Question  1.3 What does the law of large numbers let you replace \(\mathbb{E}_P[h(x)]\) with?

Answer The sample average \(\frac1n\sum_i h(x_i)\) over independent draws \(x_i \sim P\), which converges to it as \(n\) grows.

Question  1.4 How does the standard error of a Monte Carlo average scale with the number of samples \(n\)?

Answer As \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of a single term.

Question  1.5 To halve the error of a Monte Carlo estimate, by how much must you increase the sample count?

Answer Fourfold, because the error scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Question  1.6 Why is a minibatch loss a Monte Carlo estimate?

Answer It averages the per-example loss over a random sample of the data, standing in for the expectation of the loss over the whole data distribution.

Part II — Changing what you average over

3 · The law of the unconscious statistician

Suppose \(z = g(\epsilon)\) for some random \(\epsilon\). You want the average of \(f(z)\). One way is to work out the distribution of \(z\) and average over it. The other way is to not bother: average \(f(g(\epsilon))\) over \(\epsilon\) directly. Both give the same number:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{P_z}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon}\big[f(g(\epsilon))\big] \]

The rule earned its name because people use it without noticing. It is the whole legal basis of the reparameterization trick in the VAE essay: you never need the density of the code \(z\), only a recipe that turns standard noise into it.

When you do want that density, there is a price, and it is a slope. For an invertible map \(y = g(x)\) in one dimension,

\[ p_y(y) = p_x(x)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| \]

Stretching space thins the probability out. If \(x\) is uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(y = 2x\), then \(y\) is uniform on \([0,2]\) with density \(\tfrac12\): the same total mass spread twice as wide. In many dimensions the slope becomes the absolute determinant of the Jacobian, and models that keep that determinant cheap (normalising flows) get exact likelihoods in exchange.

Question  2.1 State the law of the unconscious statistician for \(z = g(\epsilon)\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_{P_z}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{P_\epsilon}[f(g(\epsilon))]\): you can average over the noise without knowing the distribution of \(z\).

Question  2.2 For invertible \(y = g(x)\) in 1-D, how is the density of \(y\) related to the density of \(x\)?

Answer \(p_y(y) = p_x(x)\,|dx/dy|\). In \(d\) dimensions, multiply by \(|\det \partial x/\partial y|\).

Question  2.3 \(x\) is uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(y = 2x\). What is the density of \(y\)?

Answer \(\tfrac12\) on \([0,2]\): the mass is spread over twice the length.

4 · Multiplying by one: importance sampling

Sometimes you can only sample from the wrong distribution. You want \(\mathbb{E}_P[f]\) but your samples come from \(Q\). Multiply inside by \(Q/Q\), which is one, and regroup:

\[ \mathbb{E}_P[f(x)] = \int Q(x)\,\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,f(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_Q\!\left[\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}f(x)\right] \]

Each sample from \(Q\) is reweighted by how much more (or less) likely it was under \(P\). This one move appears in three disguises in the series. In the VAE it introduces the free distribution \(q(z\mid x)\) that becomes the encoder. In PPO it is the ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) that lets you reuse stale samples. In f-divergences it is the density ratio \(P_x/P_\theta\) at the heart of the definition. The catch is always the same: when \(Q\) puts little mass where \(P\) puts a lot, a few samples get enormous weights and the variance explodes — which is exactly what PPO's clip is for.

Question  2.4 Write \(\mathbb{E}_P[f(x)]\) as an expectation under a different distribution \(Q\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_Q[(P(x)/Q(x))\,f(x)]\), by multiplying inside the integral by \(Q/Q\).

Question  2.5 When does importance sampling become unreliable?

Answer When \(Q\) puts little mass where \(P\) puts a lot: the ratio \(P/Q\) gets huge on a few samples and the variance explodes.

Question  2.6 Where does the importance ratio appear in RLHF?

Answer As the PPO ratio \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)\), which reweights samples drawn from the old policy.

Part III — How surprised should you be?

5 · Surprise and entropy

"The sun rose" is not news. "It snowed in Chennai" is. Information theory turns that intuition into a number: an outcome of probability \(p\) carries a surprise of \(-\log p\). Certain events carry none; rare ones carry a lot; and two independent events together surprise you by the sum of their separate surprises, which is why the logarithm is the right function.

The entropy of a distribution is its average surprise:

\[ H(P) = \mathbb{E}_P[-\log P(x)] \]

A fair coin has \(H = \log 2 \approx 0.693\) nats (one bit, if you use \(\log_2\)). A coin that lands heads 90% of the time has \(-(0.9\log 0.9 + 0.1\log 0.1) \approx 0.325\) nats: it is more predictable, so it surprises you less on average. Throughout this series, \(\log\) is the natural log and the unit is the nat.

Question  3.1 What is the surprise (information content) of an outcome with probability \(p\)?

Answer \(-\log p\).

Question  3.2 Why is \(-\log p\), rather than say \(1/p\), the right measure of surprise?

Answer Probabilities of independent events multiply; logarithms turn that product into a sum, so independent surprises add.

Question  3.3 Define the entropy \(H(P)\).

Answer The expected surprise: \(H(P) = \mathbb{E}_P[-\log P(x)]\).

Question  3.4 What is the entropy of a fair coin, in nats and in bits?

Answer \(\log 2 \approx 0.693\) nats, which is exactly 1 bit.

6 · Cross-entropy and the KL divergence

Now suppose the world runs on \(P\) but you believe \(Q\). You will be surprised by \(-\log Q(x)\) each time, on outcomes that actually arrive from \(P\). Your average surprise is the cross-entropy:

\[ H(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}_P[-\log Q(x)] \]

You can never do better than knowing the truth, so \(H(P,Q) \ge H(P)\). The excess — the extra surprise you pay for believing the wrong thing — is the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

\[ D_{KL}(P\|Q) = H(P,Q) - H(P) = \mathbb{E}_P\!\left[\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] \]

Check it with coins. World: fair, \(P = (0.5, 0.5)\). Belief: \(Q = (0.9, 0.1)\). Cross-entropy \(= -0.5\log 0.9 - 0.5\log 0.1 \approx 1.204\); entropy \(\approx 0.693\); so \(D_{KL}(P\|Q) \approx 0.511\) nats — the same number the GAN essay computes from the definition directly.

Two facts about KL matter more than any formula. It is never negative, and it is zero only when \(P = Q\). And it is not symmetric: \(D_{KL}(Q\|P)\) for the same coins is about 0.368. KL is a divergence, not a distance, and which way round you write it changes what an optimiser does.

Question  3.5 Define the cross-entropy \(H(P,Q)\) in words and symbols.

Answer The average surprise of someone who believes \(Q\) while outcomes come from \(P\): \(\mathbb{E}_P[-\log Q(x)]\).

Question  3.6 Write \(D_{KL}(P\|Q)\) in terms of cross-entropy and entropy.

Answer \(D_{KL}(P\|Q) = H(P,Q) - H(P)\): the extra surprise caused by believing \(Q\) instead of \(P\).

Question  3.7 Is \(D_{KL}(P\|Q) = D_{KL}(Q\|P)\) in general?

Answer No. For \(P = (0.5, 0.5)\), \(Q = (0.9, 0.1)\): 0.511 one way, 0.368 the other.

Question  3.8 When is \(D_{KL}(P\|Q) = 0\)?

Answer Exactly when \(P = Q\) (almost everywhere). Otherwise it is strictly positive.

7 · Why training on data is minimising KL

Put the data distribution in the first slot and your model in the second. Then \(D_{KL}(P_{\text{data}}\|P_\theta) = H(P_{\text{data}}, P_\theta) - H(P_{\text{data}})\), and the second term does not contain \(\theta\). So

\[ \arg\min_\theta D_{KL}(P_{\text{data}}\|P_\theta) = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{data}}}[-\log P_\theta(x)] = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{data}}}[\log P_\theta(x)] \]

Minimising the cross-entropy loss, maximising likelihood, and minimising forward KL to the data are one and the same act. The language model at the start of the RLHF essay, the VAE's ELBO, and the diffusion bound are all attempts to climb this one hill.

Which direction of KL you minimise shapes the result. Forward KL, \(D_{KL}(P_{\text{data}}\|P_\theta)\), averages over the data, so it heavily punishes a model that assigns near-zero probability anywhere the data lives: it is mode-covering, preferring to smear mass over every mode, even at the cost of putting some where no data is. Reverse KL, \(D_{KL}(P_\theta\|P_{\text{data}})\), averages over the model, so it punishes the model for putting mass where the data is absent but does not mind missing whole modes: it is mode-seeking. Variational inference in the VAE uses the reverse direction for its approximate posterior, and inherits that tendency to under-cover.

Question  3.9 Why does minimising \(D_{KL}(P_{\text{data}}\|P_\theta)\) equal maximum likelihood?

Answer It equals \(H(P_{\text{data}}, P_\theta) - H(P_{\text{data}})\); the entropy term has no \(\theta\), so minimising KL is minimising \(\mathbb{E}_{\text{data}}[-\log P_\theta]\).

Question  3.10 Forward KL \(D_{KL}(P\|Q_\theta)\) is called mode-covering. Why?

Answer It averages \(\log(P/Q_\theta)\) under \(P\), so any region where \(P\) has mass but \(Q_\theta\) does not costs enormously; \(Q_\theta\) spreads to cover every mode.

Question  3.11 Reverse KL \(D_{KL}(Q_\theta\|P)\) is called mode-seeking. Why?

Answer It averages under \(Q_\theta\), so it only penalises mass that \(Q_\theta\) places where \(P\) is small; ignoring a whole mode of \(P\) costs little.

Part IV — Bending lines

8 · Convexity and Jensen’s inequality

Draw a bowl. Pick any two points on its rim and join them with a straight string. The string lies above the bowl everywhere between them. That is convexity, and the most useful thing it gives you is an inequality about averages. For convex \(f\),

\[ f\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big) \;\le\; \mathbb{E}\big[f(X)\big] \]

"The function of the average is below the average of the function." For a concave function such as \(\log\), a cap rather than a bowl, the inequality flips: \(\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]\). Take \(X = 1\) or \(4\) with equal probability: \(\log 2.5 \approx 0.916\) against \(\tfrac12\log 4 \approx 0.693\). The gap closes to zero only when \(X\) does not vary.

Jensen is the hidden engine of half this series. It makes the ELBO a lower bound in the VAE and diffusion essays, it makes every f-divergence non-negative in the GAN essay, and it proves the fact the last Part asserted. Watch:

\[ -D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \mathbb{E}_P\!\left[\log\frac{Q}{P}\right] \le \log\mathbb{E}_P\!\left[\frac{Q}{P}\right] = \log\int Q(x)\,dx = \log 1 = 0 \]

So \(D_{KL} \ge 0\), with equality exactly when \(Q/P\) is constant — that is, when \(Q = P\). That is Gibbs' inequality, in one line.

Question  4.1 State Jensen’s inequality for a convex function \(f\).

Answer \(f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\).

Question  4.2 Which way does Jensen’s inequality point for \(\log\), and why?

Answer \(\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]\), because \(\log\) is concave, which flips the convex inequality.

Question  4.3 When does Jensen’s inequality hold with equality?

Answer When \(X\) is constant (or \(f\) is linear over the range \(X\) takes).

Question  4.4 Use Jensen to show \(D_{KL}(P\|Q) \ge 0\).

Answer \(-D_{KL} = \mathbb{E}_P\log(Q/P) \le \log\mathbb{E}_P(Q/P) = \log\int Q = \log 1 = 0\).

Question  4.5 Name the place in each of the VAE and GAN essays where Jensen does the work.

Answer VAE: it turns \(\log\mathbb{E}_q[P_\theta(x,z)/q]\) into the ELBO lower bound. GAN: it proves every f-divergence is \(\ge f(1) = 0\).

Part V — The algebra of bell curves

9 · The Gaussian, and what linear maps do to it

The Gaussian is everywhere in this series for a reason that is almost embarrassingly practical: it is the one distribution whose algebra closes. In one dimension,

\[ \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \log\mathcal{N} = -\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \]

The log is a quadratic. That single fact is why "Gaussian likelihood" and "squared error loss" are the same sentence in every essay here.

Push a Gaussian through a linear map and it stays Gaussian. If \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\) then

\[ Ax + b \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(A\mu + b,\; A\Sigma A^\top\right) \]

Read it backwards and you have the reparameterization trick: start from standard noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and build any Gaussian as \(\mu + L\epsilon\), where \(LL^\top = \Sigma\). And add two independent Gaussians and you get a Gaussian whose means add and whose variances add. That is the entire reason diffusion can jump from a clean image to step 700 in one line instead of seven hundred.

Question  5.1 What is \(\log\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)\)?

Answer \(-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) - \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\): a quadratic in \(x\) plus a constant.

Question  5.2 Why does a Gaussian likelihood with fixed variance give a squared-error loss?

Answer Its negative log is \((x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)\) plus a constant, so maximising likelihood minimises squared error.

Question  5.3 If \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\), what is the distribution of \(Ax + b\)?

Answer \(\mathcal{N}(A\mu + b,\ A\Sigma A^\top)\).

Question  5.4 \(x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) and \(x_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\) are independent. What is the distribution of \(x_1 + x_2\)?

Answer \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)\): means add and variances add (standard deviations do not).

10 · Multiplying two Gaussians: completing the square

Multiply two Gaussian densities in the same variable and the exponents add. A sum of two quadratics is a quadratic, so the product is another Gaussian shape. Completing the square tells you which one, and the answer is beautifully simple when you think in precisions (inverse variances):

\[ \mathcal{N}(x;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2) \;\propto\; \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2), \qquad \frac{1}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}, \qquad \mu = \sigma^2\left(\frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\right) \]

Precisions add, and the mean is a precision-weighted average. Combine a belief "\(x\) is about 0, give or take 1" with a measurement "\(x\) looks like 2, give or take 1" and you get "\(x\) is about 1, give or take \(1/\sqrt{2}\)": halfway, and more confident than either. This is exactly the computation that produces the true reverse step \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\) in the diffusion essay — one Gaussian from the forward step, one from the jump-to-any-time formula, multiplied and completed.

Question  5.5 When you multiply two Gaussian densities in \(x\), how do their precisions combine?

Answer They add: \(1/\sigma^2 = 1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2\).

Question  5.6 What is the mean of the product of two Gaussian densities?

Answer The precision-weighted average \(\mu = \sigma^2(\mu_1/\sigma_1^2 + \mu_2/\sigma_2^2)\).

Question  5.7 Combine \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) with \(\mathcal{N}(2, 1)\) by multiplication. What Gaussian results?

Answer \(\mathcal{N}(1, \tfrac12)\): mean halfway, variance halved.

Question  5.8 In which essay does completing the square produce a key closed form, and which?

Answer Diffusion: the true reverse step \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\), whose mean \(\mu_q\) and variance \(\Sigma_q\) come from multiplying Gaussians.

11 · The KL between two Gaussians

Because the log of a Gaussian is a quadratic, the expected log-ratio of two Gaussians is an expectation of quadratics, and expectations of quadratics only need means and variances. So KL between Gaussians has a closed form. In one dimension,

\[ D_{KL}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\right) = \log\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12 \]

Two special cases do most of the work in the series. Against a standard normal it becomes \(\tfrac12(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)\), the VAE's regulariser. And with equal variances everything but the means cancels, leaving \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2/(2\sigma^2)\): a squared error between means, which is how a thousand-step diffusion bound turns into a regression.

Question  5.9 Why does the KL between two Gaussians have a closed form?

Answer The log-ratio is a quadratic in \(x\), and the expectation of a quadratic depends only on the mean and variance.

Question  5.10 What is \(D_{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\)?

Answer \(\tfrac12(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)\).

Question  5.11 What does the KL between two Gaussians reduce to when their variances are equal?

Answer \((\mu_1-\mu_2)^2/(2\sigma^2)\) — a scaled squared error between the means.

Part VI — Turning evidence around

12 · Bayes’ rule, and the integral that ruins it

A model usually tells you how causes produce effects: given a hidden code \(z\), here is the distribution of the image \(x\). What you want is the reverse: given this image, which codes could have made it? Bayes' rule turns one into the other:

\[ P(z\mid x) = \frac{P(x\mid z)\,P(z)}{P(x)}, \qquad P(x) = \int P(x\mid z)\,P(z)\,dz \]

The names are worth owning: \(P(z)\) is the prior, \(P(x\mid z)\) the likelihood, \(P(z\mid x)\) the posterior, and \(P(x)\) the evidence or marginal likelihood. The numerator is usually easy: a prior you chose and a network you can run. The denominator is the problem. It sums the numerator over every possible cause, and when \(z\) is a continuous vector feeding a neural network, that integral has no closed form and too many dimensions to grid.

Almost every model in this series is a different answer to that one denominator. The VAE bounds it from below with an encoder. Diffusion fixes the encoder so that the posterior steps are Gaussian and tractable. GANs refuse to evaluate any density at all. And DPO, at the end of the RLHF essay, wins by making a similar normaliser cancel.

Question  6.1 Write Bayes’ rule for a posterior over a latent \(z\) given data \(x\).

Answer \(P(z\mid x) = P(x\mid z)P(z)/P(x)\).

Question  6.2 Name the four pieces of Bayes’ rule \(P(z\mid x) = P(x\mid z)P(z)/P(x)\).

Answer Posterior \(P(z\mid x)\), likelihood \(P(x\mid z)\), prior \(P(z)\), evidence (marginal likelihood) \(P(x)\).

Question  6.3 Which part of Bayes’ rule is usually intractable for a neural latent-variable model, and why?

Answer The evidence \(P(x) = \int P(x\mid z)P(z)\,dz\): an integral over a high-dimensional \(z\) through a neural network, with no closed form.

Question  6.4 In the RLHF essay, how does DPO deal with an intractable normaliser?

Answer It never computes it: the partition function \(Z(x)\) appears in both responses of a preference pair and cancels in their difference.

Part VII — Differentiating a dice roll

13 · Two ways to take the gradient of an expectation

Training means differentiating. But many losses here are expectations under a distribution that itself depends on the parameters: \(\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z)}[f(z)]\). You cannot simply sample and differentiate, because the sampling step has no derivative. There are exactly two standard escapes, and the series uses both.

The score-function trick (also called the log-derivative trick, or REINFORCE) works for any distribution whose log-density you can differentiate. Use \(\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta\):

\[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\!\left[f(z)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z)\right] \]

It never differentiates \(f\), so \(f\) can be a reward model, a human, or a black box. That is why policy gradients in RLHF use it: a token is discrete and there is nothing to differentiate through.

The reparameterization trick works when you can write the sample as a smooth function of parameter-free noise, \(z = g_\theta(\epsilon)\). Then, by the law of the unconscious statistician,

\[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\!\left[\nabla_\theta f(g_\theta(\epsilon))\right] \]

It does differentiate \(f\), so \(f\) must be smooth in \(z\) — a decoder, say — and it is what makes the VAE trainable.

Question  7.1 State the log-derivative identity.

Answer \(\nabla_\theta p_\theta(z) = p_\theta(z)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z)\), because \(\nabla\log p = \nabla p / p\).

Question  7.2 Write the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator of \(\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(z)]\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(z)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z)]\), estimated by averaging over samples of \(z\).

Question  7.3 Write the reparameterization estimator of \(\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(z)]\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}_\epsilon[\nabla_\theta f(g_\theta(\epsilon))]\), where \(z = g_\theta(\epsilon)\) and \(\epsilon\) has a fixed distribution.

Question  7.4 Why does RLHF use the score-function estimator rather than reparameterization?

Answer Tokens are discrete and the reward is a black box: there is no smooth \(z = g_\theta(\epsilon)\) to differentiate through, but \(\log\pi_\theta\) is differentiable.

Question  7.5 What does the reparameterization estimator require of \(f\) that the score-function estimator does not?

Answer That \(f\) be differentiable in \(z\). The score-function estimator only needs to evaluate \(f\).

14 · Why the difference matters, in numbers

Both estimators are unbiased. They are not equally noisy. Take the simplest possible case: \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)\), \(f(z) = z^2\), at \(\mu = 1\). The true gradient is \(\frac{d}{d\mu}\mathbb{E}[z^2] = \frac{d}{d\mu}(\mu^2 + 1) = 2\mu = 2\).

The reparameterized estimator writes \(z = \mu + \epsilon\) and differentiates: each sample contributes \(2z\). Its variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(2z) = 4\).

The score-function estimator uses \(\nabla_\mu\log\mathcal{N}(z;\mu,1) = z - \mu\): each sample contributes \(z^2(z - \mu)\). Its mean is also 2, but working out the fourth and sixth moments of a Gaussian gives a variance of 30.

Same answer on average, seven and a half times the variance — and by the square-root law from Part I, roughly seven times as many samples to reach the same accuracy. In high dimensions the gap is usually far worse. That is the whole case for the reparameterization trick, and it is why RLHF spends so many sections on baselines and advantages: subtracting a baseline \(b\), giving \((f(z) - b)\nabla\log p\), leaves the mean untouched because \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p] = 0\), and can cut the variance dramatically.

Question  7.6 \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)\), \(f(z) = z^2\). What single-sample term does each estimator of \(d\mathbb{E}[f]/d\mu\) use?

Answer Reparameterization: \(2z\). Score function: \(z^2(z - \mu)\). Both have mean \(2\mu\).

Question  7.7 In that example at \(\mu = 1\), what are the two estimators’ variances?

Answer 4 for reparameterization, 30 for the score function — about 7.5 times larger.

Question  7.8 What is \(\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z)]\)?

Answer Zero: it equals \(\int\nabla_\theta p_\theta = \nabla_\theta\int p_\theta = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0\).

Question  7.9 Why can you subtract a constant baseline \(b\) from \(f\) in the score-function estimator without biasing it?

Answer Because \(\mathbb{E}[b\,\nabla\log p_\theta] = b\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p_\theta] = 0\). The mean is unchanged; the variance can fall a lot.

Part VIII — Two things called the score, and a map

15 · The word “score” means two different gradients

This trips up almost everyone who reads both the RLHF and diffusion literatures, so it gets its own section. Both fields talk about "the score", and both mean a gradient of a log-density — but with respect to different things.

namegradient with respect toused in
score function (statistics)parameters: \(\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\)REINFORCE, policy gradients, RLHF
score (score matching)data: \(\nabla_x\log p(x)\)diffusion, score-based models

The second kind is a vector field over the data space that points uphill, toward regions of higher probability. For a Gaussian it is simply \(\nabla_x\log\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = -(x-\mu)/\sigma^2\): an arrow from wherever you stand back toward the mean, longer the further out you are and the narrower the bell. Follow such arrows from noise, adding a little jitter as you go, and you climb into the places where data lives. That is diffusion sampling, described in one sentence.

Question  8.1 What is the "score" in diffusion / score matching?

Answer The gradient of the log-density with respect to the data: \(\nabla_x\log p(x)\).

Question  8.2 What is the "score function" in REINFORCE / policy gradients?

Answer The gradient of the log-density with respect to the parameters: \(\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\).

Question  8.3 What is \(\nabla_x\log\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)\), and which way does it point?

Answer \(-(x-\mu)/\sigma^2\): toward the mean, with length growing with distance from it.

Question  8.4 Which single tool from this essay turns the diffusion bound into a regression, and how?
