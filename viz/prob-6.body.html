Joint distributions,
the Jacobian,
and conditioning on a variable

Two variables at once, what one of them tells you about the other, what a curve does to a density, and the tower property that turns an inequality into the bound every variational model is trained on

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the sixth of seven essays on probability. It needs the third, fourth and fifth, and it is the one that makes the variational autoencoder legible: a latent variable model is a joint distribution, the encoder is a conditional, the decoder is a conditional, and the bound they are trained with is the tower property of conditional expectation with Jensen's inequality bolted on.

Part I — Two variables at once

1 · A table of two variables

Somebody records the weather and whether you carry an umbrella, on a hundred mornings. Two variables, and neither alone tells the story: the interesting quantity is how often each combination happens.

That is the joint distribution. For two discrete variables it is a table, and the table is the distribution.

umbrellano umbrellarow total
rain0.280.020.30
sun0.120.580.70
column total0.400.601.00

The row and column totals are the marginals — the distributions of each variable on its own, obtained by summing out the other. The joint contains strictly more information: knowing both marginals — 30% rain, 40% umbrellas — does not tell you whether umbrellas and rain are related. You could build a table with those same margins in which rain and umbrellas are independent (0.12 each cell for the rain row), or one in which nobody carries an umbrella in the sun. Same edges, different interior.

That asymmetry is the theme of this essay. The joint determines the marginals, always, by summing. The marginals almost never determine the joint.

Question  1.1 What does a joint distribution record that the two marginals do not?

Answer How the two variables co-occur — the weight of each combination, and hence any dependence between them.

Question  1.2 Given a joint PMF table, how do you obtain the marginal of one variable?

Answer Sum over the other variable: add up the row or column entries.

Question  1.3 Do the two marginals determine the joint distribution?

Answer No. Many different joints share the same margins; only if the variables are independent is the joint the product of the marginals.

2 · Marginalising: integrating a variable away

A point is chosen uniformly from the triangle with corners \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((1,1)\). What is the distribution of its \(x\)-coordinate alone?

The triangle has area \(1/2\), so the joint density is \(2\) on the triangle and zero elsewhere — constant, because the point is uniform. The marginal is now an integral rather than a sum, but the instruction is identical: average out the variable you do not care about.

\[ f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy \]

Here the triangle is described by \(0 \le y \le x \le 1\), so for a fixed \(x\) the inner variable runs from 0 to \(x\):

\[ f_X(x) = \int_0^{x} 2\,dy = 2x \qquad (0 < x < 1) \]

which is the density from the previous essay, arrived at from a completely different direction. Do the same for \(y\) and the limits swap: \(x\) runs from \(y\) to 1,

\[ f_Y(y) = \int_y^{1} 2\,dx = 2(1-y) \qquad (0 < y < 1) \]

So \(X\) crowds toward 1 and \(Y\) crowds toward 0. Both facts are visible in the triangle if you look at it — most of its width is at large \(x\), most of its height at small \(y\) — and the integrals are just the picture done carefully. This is worth internalising: the marginal is not an abstraction, it is the shape of the shadow the joint casts on one axis.

Question  2.1 Write the formula for a marginal density from a joint density.

Answer \(f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy\) — integrate the other variable out.

Question  2.2 A uniform point on the triangle \(0 \le y \le x \le 1\). What is \(f_X(x)\)?

Answer \(\int_0^x 2\,dy = 2x\) for \(0 < x < 1\).

Question  2.3 Same triangle: what is \(f_Y(y)\), and why are the limits different?

Answer \(\int_y^1 2\,dx = 2(1-y)\); for a fixed \(y\) the triangle extends in \(x\) from \(y\) up to 1.

3 · Conditional densities, and the slice

You are told the point's \(y\)-coordinate is \(0.5\). What is the distribution of \(x\) now?

The joint told you where the point can be. Learning \(y = 0.5\) cuts a horizontal line through the triangle, and the point must lie on the segment of that line inside the triangle. That is the same “delete the impossible outcomes and renormalise” move as the second essay, with an integral where the sum used to be.

\[ f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}, \qquad f_Y(y) > 0 \]

At \(y = 0.5\): \(f_Y(0.5) = 2(1 - 0.5) = 1\), and \(f_{X,Y}(x, 0.5) = 2\) for \(x \in (0.5, 1)\). So

\[ f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid 0.5) = \frac{2}{1} = 2 \quad\text{on } (0.5, 1) \]

which is uniform on a segment of length \(1/2\), and \(2 \times 0.5 = 1\). The slice kept the same height and got shorter, so the renormalisation had to raise it. In general, a conditional density is the joint restricted to a slice, rescaled to integrate to one — and the rescaling factor is exactly \(1/f_Y(y)\).

The denominator can be zero, and that is not a technicality to skip. Conditioning on an event of probability zero is undefined by this formula, and the whole of the next section is about what you can say instead.

Question  3.1 Write the conditional density formula and say which quantity must be positive.

Answer \(f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_Y(y)\), requiring \(f_Y(y) > 0\).

Question  3.2 For the uniform triangle, what is the conditional density of \(X\) given \(Y = 0.5\)?

Answer Uniform on \((0.5, 1)\), i.e. \(f = 2\) there and 0 elsewhere.

Question  3.3 Conditioning on a slice keeps the joint's height but shortens the support. What must the rescaling therefore do?

Answer Raise the height, by dividing by the marginal weight of the slice, so the result integrates to one.

Part II — Dependence, and what it does not tell you

4 · Covariance, correlation, and a number you can compute

Two variables can be related in every possible way, and the simplest relation to measure is whether they move together. Covariance asks exactly that: when \(X\) is above its mean, is \(Y\) above its mean too?

\[ \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y] \]

The second form is the one you compute with, and its sign is the answer to the question: positive means they rise together, negative means one rises as the other falls, zero means no linear tendency.

For the uniform triangle, all four pieces are integrals. The two means are \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 x \cdot 2x\,dx = 2/3\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^1 y \cdot 2(1-y)\,dy = 1/3\). The cross term is

\[ \mathbb{E}[XY] = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x} 2xy\;dy\,dx = \int_0^1 x^3\,dx = \frac14 \]

so \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac14 - \tfrac29 = \tfrac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278\).

Covariance has units — square centimetres if the variables are in centimetres — and a magnitude that depends on how spread out each variable is. Dividing by the two standard deviations fixes both problems at once:

\[ \rho = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \in [-1, 1] \]

Here both variances turn out to be \(1/18\), so \(\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = 1/\sqrt{18}\), and \(\rho = (1/36) / (1/18) = 1/2\). A moderate positive association, which matches the picture: the triangle leans.

Question  4.1 Give both forms of the covariance and say which you compute with.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)]\) for the definition, \(\mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Y\) for computation.

Question  4.2 Why is covariance divided by the two standard deviations to get correlation?

Answer Because covariance carries units and scales with each variable's spread; dividing normalises it to a dimensionless number in \([-1,1]\).

Question  4.3 On the uniform triangle, \(\mathrm{Cov} = 1/36\) and both variances are \(1/18\). What is \(\rho\)?

Answer \(\rho = (1/36)/(1/18) = 1/2\).

5 · The multivariate normal, and why it is closed under everything

Assemble \(d\) correlated normals into one vector and you have the multivariate normal: a mean vector \(\mu\) and a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), and nothing else.

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = (2\pi)^{-d/2}\,|\Sigma|^{-1/2} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac12 (\mathbf{x}-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu)\right) \]

The single most useful fact about it is that it is closed under the three operations this essay is about. Its marginals are normal. Its conditionals are normal. Affine maps of it are normal. Nowhere else do all three hold simultaneously, and that is why Gaussian models are the ones that can be solved on paper.

In two dimensions with unit variances and correlation \(\rho\), conditioning gives the regression line and the shrinkage:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2] = \mu_1 + \rho\,\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}\,(x_2 - \mu_2), \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2) = \sigma_1^2\,(1 - \rho^2) \]

With \(\mu = (0,0)\), \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1\) and \(\rho = 0.8\), observing \(X_2 = 1\) moves your guess for \(X_1\) to \(0.8\), and the uncertainty shrinks from 1 to \(\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 0.6\). Observe something surprising and the conditional mean moves toward it, but only by \(\rho\) of the way; the residual variance is what remains after that.

Read the two formulas as the whole idea of conditioning in continuous settings. The mean becomes a function of what you observed, and the variance drops by a factor you can name. Every Gaussian filter, every Kalman step, and every closed-form posterior in a Gaussian model is these two lines.

Question  5.1 Which three operations leave the multivariate normal closed?

Answer Taking marginals, taking conditionals, and applying affine maps.

Question  5.2 Write the conditional mean for a bivariate normal with correlation \(\rho\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2] = \mu_1 + \rho(\sigma_1/\sigma_2)(x_2 - \mu_2)\).

Question  5.3 \(\rho = 0.8\), unit variances, and you observe \(X_2 = 1\). What is your guess for \(X_1\), and what is the conditional variance?

Answer Guess \(0.8\); conditional variance \(1 - 0.64 = 0.36\) (standard deviation \(0.6\)).

6 · Uncorrelated, and still dependent

Let \(X\) be \(-1\), \(0\) or \(1\) with equal probability, and let \(Y = X^2\). Knowing \(X\) tells you \(Y\) exactly, so these two are as dependent as two variables can be.

Now compute the covariance. \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0\) by symmetry, so \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0 - 0 = 0\). Uncorrelated, and utterly dependent.

\[ \text{independent} \Rightarrow \text{uncorrelated} \qquad\text{but}\qquad \text{uncorrelated} \not\Rightarrow \text{independent} \]

Only one of those arrows runs. The reason is that covariance measures a specific thing — the tendency of deviations to have the same sign, i.e. linear association — and \(Y = X^2\) has a perfectly symmetric relationship with no linear part at all. A scatter plot makes it obvious; a single number cannot.

So correlation is a first look and never a verdict. When someone says two quantities are uncorrelated and leaves it there, ask what they would expect to see in the scatter plot. And when a latent-variable model asserts that its coordinates are independent, note that it is asserting something far stronger than zero covariance — which is exactly why a diagonal covariance matrix is a modelling shortcut rather than a proof of independence.

Where the shortcut bites A diagonal covariance is a claim of independence, not a consequence of one. If you model \(q(z) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}(z_i; \mu_i, \sigma_i^2)\), you have declared the latent coordinates independent. If the true posterior has correlated coordinates — and it usually does — the approximation cannot represent it, no matter how well you train. That gap is a modelling error, distinct from the sampling error of a Monte Carlo estimate, and no amount of data removes it.

Question  6.1 With \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1,0,1\}\) and \(Y = X^2\), compute the covariance.

Answer Zero: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0\) by symmetry, yet the two are perfectly dependent.

Question  6.2 Which implication holds between independence and zero correlation?

Answer Independence implies zero correlation; zero correlation does not imply independence.

Question  6.3 What kind of association does correlation actually measure?

Answer Linear association — the tendency of the two deviations to share a sign — and nothing about curved or otherwise symmetric relationships.

Question  6.4 A model uses a factorised latent \(q(z) = \prod_i q_i(z_i)\). What has it asserted, and what can it not represent?

Answer That the latent coordinates are independent; it cannot represent a posterior whose coordinates are correlated, however much data it sees.

Part III — Pushing a variable through a function

7 · Change of variables, and the Jacobian

You know the distribution of \(X\). What is the distribution of \(2X\)? Of \(\ln X\)? Of the area of a square whose side is \(X\)?

A density is a rate per unit of its own variable, so when you change the variable you must also change the units. Stretch the axis by a factor of two and the same probability is spread over twice the width, so the density halves. That factor is the whole content of the change-of-variables formula.

\[ Y = g(X), \; g \text{ monotone} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f_Y(y) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\frac{d}{dy}\,g^{-1}(y)\right| \]

Take \(X \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) and \(Y = 2X\). Then \(g^{-1}(y) = y/2\), whose derivative is \(1/2\), so \(f_Y(y) = 1 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac12\) on \((0,2)\). Density halved, support doubled, total still one.

In two dimensions the derivative becomes a determinant, because a nonlinear map stretches area by a factor that varies from point to point:

\[ f_{U,V}(u,v) = f_{X,Y}\big(x(u,v), y(u,v)\big)\,\big|\det J\big|, \qquad J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \]

The Jacobian determinant is the local area-scaling factor — how much a tiny square is inflated or deflated by the map. It appears in the denominator when you push forward and in the numerator when you pull back, which is the only thing to remember about which way up it goes: density times area is conserved.

Convolutions, and the sum of two uniforms

The sum of two independent variables is a change of variables in disguise, and it has its own formula:

\[ f_{X+Y}(z) = \int f_X(x)\,f_Y(z - x)\,dx \]

Two independent uniforms on \((0,1)\): the integrand is 1 wherever both arguments are in \((0,1)\), and the length of that overlap depends on \(z\). For \(0 \le z \le 1\) the overlap is \((0,z)\), length \(z\). For \(1 \le z \le 2\) it is \((z-1, 1)\), length \(2-z\). So

\[ f_{X+Y}(z) = \begin{cases} z & 0 \le z \le 1 \\ 2 - z & 1 \le z \le 2 \end{cases} \]

a triangle: zero at both ends, one in the middle. Check the area: \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1\). The corners are rounded off compared with the flat density you started from, and that rounding is the first hint of the central limit theorem — add more uniforms and the shape relaxes toward a bell.

Beyond the book — not in §8.1–§8.2.

The same Jacobian formula is the density of a normalizing flow. Let \(f\) be an invertible, differentiable map from a simple \(z\) to a complicated \(x\). Then \(\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z(f^{-1}(x)) + \log|\det \partial f^{-1}/\partial x|\). Everything a flow does is choosing \(f\) so that the determinant is cheap: triangular Jacobians make it the sum of the diagonal's logs, which is why coupling layers are built to be triangular by construction.

Question  7.1 \(X \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) and \(Y = 2X\). What is the density of \(Y\)?

Answer \(f_Y(y) = \tfrac12\) on \((0,2)\): the support doubled and the density halved.

Question  7.2 What does the Jacobian determinant represent, geometrically?

Answer The local scaling of area (or volume) at a point under the map, so that density times area is conserved.

Question  7.3 Sum of two independent \(\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) variables: what is the density, and how do you check it?

Answer \(z\) on \([0,1]\) and \(2-z\) on \([1,2]\) — a triangle with area \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\).

Question  7.4 Write the convolution formula for the density of a sum of independent variables.

Answer \(f_{X+Y}(z) = \int f_X(x)f_Y(z-x)\,dx\), the overlap integral of the two densities.

8 · Conditional expectation, given a number and given a variable

Two questions that sound identical and are not.

Given that it rained, how many umbrellas on average? The answer is a number. You conditioned on an event, and the conditional expectation is a constant obtained by averaging over the shrunken space.

Given the weather, how many umbrellas on average? The answer is not a number. It depends on the weather, which is itself random, so the answer is a random variable — a function of the thing you conditioned on.

\[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid A] \text{ is a number} \qquad\qquad \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] \text{ is a function of } Y \]

For the uniform triangle, compute the second kind. The conditional density of \(X\) given \(Y = y\) is \(1/(1-y)\) on \((y,1)\) — uniform on that slice — so its mean is the midpoint of the slice:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] = \frac{1 + y}{2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = \frac{1 + Y}{2} \]

At \(y = 0.5\) this gives \(0.75\), which is the midpoint of \((0.5, 1)\), as it must be. Note the object on the right: a formula in \(Y\), so a random variable. Draw \(Y\), plug it in, get a number. Average over the draws and you get \(2/3\), which is \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) — the subject of the next section.

The distinction matters because only the second kind can be composed. You cannot ask about the variance of a number, you cannot nest an average of a number, and you cannot make a bound out of one. Everything interesting is the version conditioned on a random variable.

Question  8.1 What is the difference between \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid A]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\)?

Answer The first is a number (conditioning on an event); the second is a random variable, a function of \(Y\).

Question  8.2 For the uniform triangle, what is \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\)?

Answer \((1+y)/2\), the midpoint of the slice \((y,1)\).

Question  8.3 The conditional mean above is \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = (1+Y)/2\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = 1/3\). What is the average of the conditional mean?

Answer \((1 + 1/3)/2 = 2/3\), which equals \(\mathbb{E}[X]\).

9 · The tower property

Averages of averages should be averages, and they are. This is the property that carries most of the weight in variational inference, so it is worth stating precisely and seeing why it is forced.

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X] \]

The outer average is over \(Y\). You first average \(X\) inside each group, then average the group averages — and because each group is weighted by its size, that is the same as averaging everything at once. The previous section checked it numerically: \(2/3 = 2/3\).

Two companions are used constantly. Taking out what is known: anything that is a function of \(Y\) can be pulled outside a conditional expectation given \(Y\), because within a group it is a constant.

\[ \mathbb{E}[g(Y)X \mid Y] = g(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] \]

And the law of total variance, which splits uncertainty into two parts you can name:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y)\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big) \]

Read it as: total uncertainty is the average uncertainty inside groups, plus the uncertainty of the group means themselves. The second term vanishes exactly when knowing \(Y\) determines the answer, and the first vanishes when \(Y\) tells you nothing about the spread. That decomposition is the reason conditional expectations make good predictors — they capture precisely the variation that \(Y\) explains.

Where this is easy to misuse The tower property is an equality; anything you derive from it needs a second ingredient. Averaging a logarithm is not averaging, so the moment you write \(\mathbb{E}[\log(\cdot)]\), the tower property alone will not carry you — you need Jensen's inequality to relate it to the log of the average. That combination, tower then Jensen, is the ELBO in the next section, and it is worth noticing that the inequality enters from exactly one place.

Question  9.1 State the tower property and describe what it computes in words.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]] = \mathbb{E}[X]\): average the within-group averages, weighted by group size, and you recover the overall average.

Question  9.2 State the “taking out what is known” rule and explain why it holds.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[g(Y)X \mid Y] = g(Y)\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\); given \(Y\), \(g(Y)\) is a constant and constants factor out.

Question  9.3 Write the law of total variance and name its two terms.

Answer \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y)] + \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y])\): average within-group variance plus the variance of the group means.

Question  9.4 The tower property is an equality. What extra ingredient is needed to get a bound?

Answer Jensen's inequality, to relate the expectation of a log to the log of an expectation.

Part IV — Where this lands in generative AI

10 · The bound is conditional expectation plus Jensen

A latent-variable model says \(p(x, z) = p(z)\,p(x \mid z)\). You want \(\log p(x)\), and the honest route is

\[ \log p(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \]

Two moves, both from this essay. The left equality is total probability — marginalising \(z\) out, which is the integral from section 2. The inequality is Jensen applied to the concave logarithm. The right-hand side is the ELBO, and it is a conditional expectation under \(q(z \mid x)\).

Everything the variational essay does follows from the gap. The gap is exactly \(\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big)\), so the bound is tight precisely when the approximate posterior equals the true one — and the true one is unavailable because its denominator is the very quantity you were trying to compute.

The reparameterization gradient is the other place a change of variables earns its keep. The gradient of an expectation under a parameter-dependent distribution is not the expectation of the gradient, because the region being integrated over moves. Writing \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\) moves the dependence out of the distribution and into the integrand, and once the distribution being averaged over no longer depends on the parameters, linearity is enough and the gradient goes inside. That is a change of variables used to make a derivative legal — the Jacobian is the constant \(\sigma\), and it drops out because the ratio of densities is a constant.

What to carry forward is a single diagnostic. When a generative model's bound looks arbitrary, ask two questions: what is the joint, and what did you integrate out to get the marginal? Every bound in the series is an answer to those two.

Question  10.1 Which two results from this essay combine to produce the ELBO?

Answer Marginalising out \(z\) to write \(\log p(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_q[p(x,z)/q]\), then Jensen's inequality on the concave logarithm.

Question  10.2 What is the gap between \(\log p(x)\) and the ELBO, exactly?

Answer \(\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big)\), which is zero only when the approximate posterior equals the true one.

Question  10.3 Why does the reparameterization change of variables make a gradient legal?

Answer It moves the parameter dependence out of the sampling distribution and into the integrand, so the distribution no longer moves with the parameters and linearity lets the gradient go inside.

Question  10.4 Why does \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\) have a closed form after \(t\) diffusion steps?

Answer Because the steps add independent Gaussian noise, and the convolution of Gaussians is Gaussian — the sum is normal with means and variances that accumulate.
