Expectation, variance,
and the bridge
between them

One weighted average that works whether or not anything is independent, one second moment that does not, and the reason four times the data buys you exactly half the error

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the fourth of seven essays on probability. It needs the third — random variables and their distributions — and it is the last one you can read without calculus. Everything in the generative-AI series is an expectation of something, so this essay is the one that makes the rest legible: the loss, the gradient, the bound and the baseline are all averages, and their differences are all variances.

Part I — Averages you can compute

1 · A weighted average, read twice

A fairground game pays ₹10 with probability 0.2 and nothing otherwise. What is it worth to play?

Not ₹10, and not zero. Play it a hundred times and you collect about twenty times, so the fair price per go is ₹2. That number is the expectation, and there is nothing more to it than that: every outcome, weighted by how often it happens, added up.

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p_X(x) \]

Now read the same formula as physics. Put a unit of mass at each value \(x\), with the amount of mass proportional to \(p_X(x)\). The expectation is where the whole thing balances on a finger. That reading is not a decoration — it predicts things. Just as a balance point can sit between two masses with nothing there, an expectation can sit at a value the variable never takes.

A fair die is the example to keep. \(\mathbb{E}[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5\). A die never shows 3.5. Nobody finds this paradoxical once they have seen a seesaw.

The two readings disagree about nothing The average reading and the centre-of-mass reading are the same equation. The average reading is what you would see if you ran the experiment many times; the centre-of-mass reading is what you get from the distribution alone, in one shot. They agree because of the law of large numbers, which is the seventh essay in this series. Until then, treat the centre of mass as the definition and the long-run average as the reason you care.

Question  1.1 Define \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) for a discrete random variable.

Answer The sum of each value weighted by its probability: \(\sum_x x\,p_X(x)\).

Question  1.2 What is the expectation of a fair die, and is it a value the die can show?

Answer \(3.5\), which the die never shows — an expectation is a balance point, not necessarily an outcome.

Question  1.3 Which physical picture corresponds to the expectation, and what does it predict about values the variable cannot take?

Answer The centre of mass of weights \(p_X(x)\) placed at positions \(x\); a balance point can lie between the masses, so the expectation need not be attainable.

2 · Linearity, and the independence you do not need

The single most useful fact in this essay, and it is worth saying three times in three different ways so that it survives being forgotten.

\[ \mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y] \qquad\text{always, with no conditions} \]

First way: expectations add. Second way: they add whether or not \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent — whether they are dependent, negatively dependent, or deterministic functions of each other. Third way: for any number of terms, \(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_i X_i\right] = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[X_i]\), with no assumption anywhere.

Compare that with the corresponding fact for variance, which has a condition attached (essay section 5). The asymmetry is the whole reason expectations are easy and variances are hard, and it is why so much of applied probability is an attempt to express a quantity as a sum of indicators.

Two dependent variables, computed both ways

Draw one card from a deck. Let \(X = 1\) if it is an ace, and \(Y = 1\) if it is a spade. These are dependent: an ace is slightly more likely to be a spade than a random card is, because there are four aces and one of them is the ace of spades.

By linearity, \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 4/52\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = 13/52\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = 17/52\). Directly: the number of cards that are an ace or a spade is \(4 + 13 - 1 = 16\), so the probability of the indicator sum being 1 is \(16/52\).

Now count directly, and mind the difference. The number of cards that are an ace or a spade is \(4 + 13 - 1 = 16\), so the probability that \(X + Y \ge 1\) is \(16/52 \approx 0.3077\). But that is not the average of \(X + Y\), and the reason is one card. \(X + Y\) is an indicator only when the two conditions are exclusive; for the ace of spades both hold, so \(X + Y = 2\), while every other ace-or-spade card contributes 1. Hence

\[ \mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \frac{15}{52}(1) + \frac{1}{52}(2) = \frac{17}{52} \]

and linearity had it right all along. The direct count was not wrong; it was taken on the wrong variable. Here \(X + Y\) can take the value 2, and an indicator cannot.

Constant shifts and scalings behave as you would want: \(\mathbb{E}[aX + b] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\). Averages are linear, and that is the end of it.

Question  2.1 Does \(\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]\) require independence?

Answer No. It holds for every pair of random variables, dependent or not.

Question  2.2 One card is drawn. \(X = 1\) for an ace, \(Y = 1\) for a spade. Compute \(\mathbb{E}[X+Y]\) by linearity.

Answer \(4/52 + 13/52 = 17/52\). The ace of spades contributes 2 to \(X+Y\), which is why this exceeds the 16/52 probability of “ace or spade”.

Question  2.3 What is \(\mathbb{E}[aX + b]\) in terms of \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)?

Answer \(a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\).

Question  2.4 Why does the absence of a condition on linearity make expectations easier to work with than variances?

Answer Because any quantity expressible as a sum of indicators can be averaged term by term with no assumptions, while variances of sums need a covariance term unless independence holds.

3 · Indicators, and the fundamental bridge

Ten letters are stuffed into ten addressed envelopes at random. On average, how many letters reach the right envelope?

The direct route is grim. You would need the probability of exactly \(k\) correct, which is the derangement count from the previous essay, and then you would sum \(k\) times it over all \(k\) — a page of algebra that tests your patience rather than your understanding. The indicator route takes two lines and works for any \(n\).

Write \(I_j = 1\) if letter \(j\) lands in envelope \(j\). Then the number of correct letters is \(X = \sum_{j=1}^{n} I_j\), and by linearity

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[I_j] = \sum_{j=1}^{n} P(\text{letter } j \text{ is correct}) = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1 \]

One. Always one, for every \(n\). The indicators are heavily dependent — if nine letters are correct the tenth must be — and linearity does not care.

Check it for \(n = 3\) by hand. The six permutations of \(123\) give fixed-point counts \(3, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1\), which sum to 6, and \(6/6 = 1\). The distribution of \(X\) is genuinely awkward: \(P(X = 0) = 2/6\), \(P(X=1) = 3/6\), \(P(X=3) = 1/6\), and \(P(X=2) = 0\) because two correct letters force the third.

The move that made this easy has a name, the fundamental bridge:

\[ P(A) = \mathbb{E}[1_A] \]

It is obvious once stated — the average of a 0/1 variable is the fraction of ones — and it converts probability questions into addition questions. That conversion is the whole trick. A union bound, an expected count, a loss: all of them become sums of indicators, and sums can be averaged term by term without knowing anything about how the terms interact.

Question  3.1 State the fundamental bridge and say what it converts.

Answer \(P(A) = \mathbb{E}[1_A]\) — it converts a probability question into a question about the average of a 0/1 variable.

Question  3.2 On average, how many of \(n\) randomly stuffed letters land in the correct envelope?

Answer Exactly 1, for every \(n\).

Question  3.3 Verify the answer for \(n = 3\) from the six permutations.

Answer Fixed-point counts are \(3,1,1,0,0,1\), summing to 6, so the average is \(6/6 = 1\).

Question  3.4 Why does the indicator method avoid needing the PMF of \(X\)?

Answer Because linearity averages the indicators term by term, and each term needs only its own marginal probability, never the joint distribution.

Part II — The second moment

4 · LOTUS: you do not need the distribution of \(g(X)\)

You know the distribution of \(X\). You want the average of \(X^2\). Do you have to work out the distribution of \(X^2\) first?

No, and the fact that you do not is what makes applied probability computable at all. The result is called the law of the unconscious statistician, because it is used so habitually that people forget it is a theorem.

\[ \mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\, p_X(x) \]

You push \(g\) inside the sum and weight by the distribution of \(X\) itself. Nothing is rearranged; you simply never needed the induced distribution of \(g(X)\), only the probability of each original value.

Take \(X\) uniform on \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) and \(g(X) = X^2\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16)/4 = 30/4 = 7.5\). For comparison \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 2.5\), so \((\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = 6.25\), and the gap between the two will turn out to be the variance.

The reason LOTUS is indispensable in practice: the functions you care about are usually not invertible. A loss is not one-to-one in its input, so the distribution of the loss is a mess; but you never need it, because you average the loss over the data distribution instead. Every training loop is LOTUS executed on a sample.

Question  4.1 State LOTUS for a discrete random variable.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)p_X(x)\) — weight \(g\)'s values by \(X\)'s probabilities.

Question  4.2 \(X\) is uniform on \(\{1,2,3,4\}\). What is \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\)?

Answer \((1+4+9+16)/4 = 7.5\).

Question  4.3 What does LOTUS save you from having to compute?

Answer The probability distribution of \(g(X)\) itself, which is often unobtainable when \(g\) is not invertible.

5 · Variance, and why the standard deviation is the one with units

Two archers both average the bullseye. One clusters; one sprays. The mean cannot tell them apart, so we need a number for spread.

The recipe is to measure each draw's distance from the mean, square it, and average. Squaring is there to stop the two directions cancelling; averaging is the expectation.

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}X\big)^2 \]

The second form is the one you compute with, and the identity is one line of expanding the square and using linearity. For \(X\) uniform on \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) it gives \(7.5 - 6.25 = 1.25\), matching the shortcut \((n^2-1)/12 = 15/12\) for a discrete uniform on \(1..n\).

Variances are in squared units, which is why nobody quotes them. Heights measured in centimetres have a variance in square centimetres, an object with no physical meaning. The standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\) puts you back in centimetres, and that is the number to report.

What variance does and does not respect

Shifting leaves it alone and scaling squares: \(\mathrm{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)\). That one fact does a surprising amount of work — it is why standardising a variable is \((X - \mu)/\sigma\) and not \((X-\mu)/\sigma^2\).

For sums, unlike expectations, there is a price:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) \]

and the covariance term vanishes only when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent (or at least uncorrelated). This is the asymmetry from section 2 made quantitative: expectations compose for free, variances cost you a covariance. Learn the shape of that sentence; it explains why every gradient-variance discussion in machine learning is a discussion about correlations.

Question  5.1 Give both forms of the variance and say which one you compute with.

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2]\) by definition, and \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\) for computation.

Question  5.2 What is \(\mathrm{Var}(aX + b)\)?

Answer \(a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)\) — the shift does nothing and the scale is squared.

Question  5.3 Why is the standard deviation reported instead of the variance?

Answer Because the variance is in squared units, while \(\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\) is in the same units as the variable.

Question  5.4 Write \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)\) and say exactly when the extra term disappears.

Answer \(\mathrm{Var}X + \mathrm{Var}Y + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\); the covariance term vanishes when \(X\) and \(Y\) are uncorrelated, in particular when independent.

6 · Expectation does not commute with a curve

A factory makes rods whose lengths vary. The area of a rod's cross-section goes as the square of its diameter. If the average diameter is 10 mm, is the average area the area at 10 mm?

No, and the direction of the error is predictable rather than accidental. Averages pass through straight lines and stop there. Through a curve, they get bent, and which way they bend depends on the curvature.

Two examples make it concrete. Let \(X\) be 1 or 2 with probability \(1/2\) each.

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = 1.5, \quad \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]} = 0.6667 \qquad\text{but}\qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{X}\right] = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(0.5) = 0.75 \]

\(0.75 > 0.6667\), so the average of the reciprocal exceeds the reciprocal of the average. And for the square, \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 2.5\) while \((\mathbb{E}X)^2 = 2.25\) — again the curve wins.

The general statement is Jensen's inequality, and it gets a section of its own in the seventh essay because it is the engine behind the variational lower bound. For now, the working rule: E[g(X)] equals g(E[X]) when \(g\) is affine, and otherwise the two differ in a direction set by whether \(g\) curves up or down.

A special case worth separating out, because it is used constantly: since \(\mathrm{Var}(X) \ge 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\), it follows that \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] \ge (\mathbb{E}X)^2\) always, with equality only for a constant. The variance is literally the gap between the two sides of that inequality.

Question  6.1 \(X = 1\) or \(2\) with probability \(1/2\) each. Compute \(\mathbb{E}[1/X]\) and \(1/\mathbb{E}[X]\).

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 0.75\) and \(1/\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.6667\); they differ.

Question  6.2 For which functions \(g\) is \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = g(\mathbb{E}[X])\) exactly?

Answer Affine ones, \(g(x) = ax + b\) — straight lines are the only curves with no bending.

Question  6.3 Why must \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] \ge (\mathbb{E}X)^2\)?

Answer Because their difference is \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\), which is an average of squares and so cannot be negative.

7 · Moments, and the generating function that produces them

Mean and variance are the first two of an infinite family, and the family is worth naming because each member answers a different question about shape.

namedefinitionwhat it tells you
raw moment \(k\)\(\mathbb{E}[X^k]\)scale, then spread, then lean
central moment \(k\)\(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^k]\)spread with the location removed
skewness\(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\)which tail is longer; sign gives the direction
kurtosis\(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4\)how much weight sits in the tails (3 for a Gaussian)

The moment generating function is a single object that carries them all:

\[ M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad M_X'(0) = \mathbb{E}[X], \quad M_X''(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] \]

Differentiate under the expectation, and each derivative brings down a factor of \(X\). Do it once for the Bernoulli to see the machine work. With \(P(X=1) = p\):

\[ M(t) = (1-p) + p\,e^{t} \qquad M'(t) = p\,e^{t} \qquad M''(t) = p\,e^{t} \]

so \(M'(0) = p\) and \(M''(0) = p\), giving \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1-p)\) — the answer from the previous essay, re-derived without the fundamental bridge.

Two properties earn the name. First, the MGF identifies the distribution: if two variables have the same MGF in a neighbourhood of zero, they have the same distribution. Second, and this is the one used constantly, sums of independent variables become products of MGFs:

\[ M_{X+Y}(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}e^{tY}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tY}\right] = M_X(t)\,M_Y(t) \]

The middle step is exactly where independence is used — it is the only step that could fail. That is how the Binomial's MGF is obtained from the Bernoulli's, and why \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\) is a sum of \(n\) Bernoulli(\(p\)) variables.

Where this breaks An MGF need not exist. The definition involves \(e^{tX}\), which grows faster than any polynomial; for heavy-tailed variables the expectation diverges for every \(t \ne 0\), and the Cauchy and lognormal distributions are the standard casualties. The characteristic function, which replaces \(t\) with \(it\), always exists — that is why the rigorous theory uses it. For everything in this series, the MGF is fine.

Question  7.1 Define the MGF and say how to extract the first two moments from it.

Answer \(M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\), with \(M'(0) = \mathbb{E}[X]\) and \(M''(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^2]\).

Question  7.2 Write the Bernoulli(\(p\)) MGF and use it to get the variance.

Answer \(M(t) = (1-p) + pe^t\), so \(M'(0) = M''(0) = p\) and \(\mathrm{Var} = p - p^2 = p(1-p)\).

Question  7.3 Why does an MGF turn a sum of independent variables into a product, and which step needs the independence?

Answer \(M_{X+Y}(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}e^{tY}] = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]\); the factorisation of the expectation is the step that requires independence.

Question  7.4 Give a distribution for which the MGF does not exist, and name the replacement.

Answer The Cauchy distribution (and the lognormal); the characteristic function, which uses \(it\), always exists.

Part III — Variance as something you can spend

8 · The square-root tax

You estimate an average by sampling \(n\) times and dividing by \(n\). How wrong are you, typically?

Suppose a single observation has standard deviation \(\sigma\). The average of \(n\) independent observations has standard deviation

\[ \mathrm{sd}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

and the derivation is three lines of the variance rules: \(\mathrm{Var}(\frac1n\sum X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\). Square-root that, and the tax appears.

The tax is severe and it is the reason simulation is expensive. Read it as a table.

samples \(n\)error relative to one samplewith \(\sigma = 2\)
112.0
41/21.0
1001/100.2
4001/200.1
10,0001/1000.02

Halving the error costs four times the samples. Cutting it by ten costs a hundred times. There is no way around this by computing harder; the only escape is to attack \(\sigma\) itself.

That single sentence explains a great deal of modern machine learning. Why are there so many variance-reduction tricks — baselines, control variates, importance sampling, the reparameterization trick? Because every one of them reduces \(\sigma\), and reducing \(\sigma\) by a factor of ten is worth a hundredfold increase in batch size.

Question  8.1 What is the standard deviation of the average of \(n\) independent observations each with standard deviation \(\sigma\)?

Answer \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

Question  8.2 With \(\sigma = 2\), what sample count gives an error of 0.1?

Answer \(n = 400\), since \(2/\sqrt{400} = 2/20 = 0.1\).

Question  8.3 To halve a Monte Carlo error, by what factor must \(n\) grow?

Answer Four, because the error scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Question  8.4 Why is reducing \(\sigma\) by ten better than buying more samples?

Answer Because \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) makes a tenfold \(\sigma\) reduction worth a hundredfold increase in \(n\) — and the variance reduction is usually free once you find it.

9 · Subtracting something that averages to zero

An estimator can be unbiased and still useless. Unbiasedness says the average over many runs is right; it says nothing about how far any single run lands. This section is about buying precision for free by exploiting one identity.

The identity is that the score function has zero mean. For a density \(p_\theta(x)\),

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(X)\right] = \int p_\theta(x)\,\frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\,dx = \nabla_\theta \int p_\theta(x)\,dx = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0 \]

So you may subtract any constant \(b\), called a baseline, from your estimator without moving its mean:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(f(X) - b)\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(X)\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[f(X)\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(X)\big] - b \cdot 0 \]

Unchanged in expectation, and the variance can collapse. Here is the smallest case where it does. Let \(X\) be 0 or 1 with probability \(1/2\), with \(f(1) = 10\) and \(f(0) = 0\), and let the score be \(+1\) at \(X=1\) and \(-1\) at \(X=0\).

A baseline buys precision. Both estimators have mean 5. In this example, subtracting the baseline makes the two possible gradient estimates coincide, removing variance entirely.

Check the numbers. With \(b = 0\) the estimator takes values 10 and 0, so \(\mathbb{E}[\text{est}^2] = 50\) and the mean is 5, giving a variance of 25. With \(b = 5\) both draws give \(5\), so the variance is 0 — and the mean is still 5. Nothing was lost and everything was gained, because \(b\) subtracted a quantity that had no effect on the mean in the first place.

The best baseline in general is not the mean of \(f\) but the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[f \mid \text{state}]\), which is exactly what a value function in reinforcement learning estimates. That is why actor-critic methods carry a critic at all: it is a variance reduction device wearing the costume of a second network.

Question  9.1 Why may you subtract a constant baseline from a score-function estimator without introducing bias?

Answer Because \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(X)] = 0\), so the baseline's contribution to the mean is zero.

Question  9.2 In the two-point example, what are the estimator's variance with \(b=0\) and with \(b=5\)?

Answer 25 with \(b=0\) (values 10 and 0) and 0 with \(b=5\) (both values 5); the mean is 5 either way.

Question  9.3 What is the ideal baseline, and what role does it play in actor-critic methods?

Answer \(\mathbb{E}[f \mid \text{state}]\), estimated by the value function — the critic exists to supply the baseline.

Question  9.4 What does an unbiased estimator with large variance fail to guarantee?

Answer That any single run lands near the truth — unbiasedness is a statement about the average over infinitely many runs.

Part IV — Where this lands in generative AI

10 · Every loss is an expectation, and every problem is a variance

A training loop computes a number from a minibatch and takes a gradient step. That number is an estimate of an average over a distribution of data, and the whole of this essay is about what that sentence does and does not guarantee.

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ell_\theta(x)\big] \approx \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \ell_\theta(x_i) \]

Linearity is why the gradient of the estimate is an estimate of the gradient, when the sampling distribution does not depend on \(\theta\). When it does — which is exactly the case for a policy or an encoder — linearity is not enough, and you need the score function trick or a reparameterization. Recognizing which case you are in is most of the skill.

The one place this essay is most load-bearing is the VAE. Its reconstruction term, \(\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]\), is an expectation under a distribution that depends on the parameters — so the naive gradient is biased, the score-function estimator is unbiased but has variance large enough to be useless at realistic dimensions, and the reparameterization trick fixes it by rewriting the sample as a deterministic function of noise. That whole story is one sentence: same mean, smaller variance.

The habit to take away is a two-question diagnostic. What is the expectation here, and under which distribution? And what is the variance of my estimate of it? The first question finds the objective. The second finds the bug.

Question  10.1 A minibatch loss with \(B\) examples estimates an expectation. What is the standard deviation of that estimate, and in which quantity is it?

Answer \(\sigma/\sqrt{B}\), where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of the per-example loss.

Question  10.2 When is linearity enough to justify swapping a gradient and an expectation, and when is it not?

Answer Enough when the distribution being averaged over does not depend on \(\theta\); not enough when it does, which is when the score-function or reparameterization trick is needed.

Question  10.3 What is the RLHF advantage \(A = Q - V\) in the language of this essay?

Answer A baseline: a quantity subtracted from the reward whose own expected contribution is zero, so the estimator stays unbiased while its variance falls.

Question  10.4 Why does the reparameterization trick count as variance reduction rather than bias removal?

Answer Because the score-function estimator was already unbiased; the trick keeps the same mean and replaces a high-variance estimate with a low-variance one by moving the randomness outside the parameters.
