Counting,
and what
a probability is

Pebbles, sample spaces, trees, permutations, combinations, story proofs and the sieve — six counting habits that turn “how likely?” into arithmetic, and one argument for why the arithmetic is allowed to work at all

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the first of seven essays on probability, and it is the floor everything else stands on. Before you can average over a distribution you have to say what the outcomes are. Before you can say how often an outcome happens, you have to be able to count. Read this one before the essay on conditional probability, and that before the one on distributions.

Part I — A probability is a weight on pebbles

1 · Ten pebbles in a bag

A bag holds ten pebbles. Six are white and four are black. You reach in without looking and take one out. What is the chance it is white?

Six in ten. You did not compute anything — you counted. That is the whole of this essay: the entire content of “probability” for the next nine sections is counting, done carefully, in situations where the counting is not obvious.

Now say the same thing in the way the subject says it. The sample space is the set of everything that could happen. Here it is the ten pebbles, written \(S\). An event is a subset of the sample space — the set of outcomes you are asking about. Here \(A\) is the six white pebbles. A probability is a number attached to every outcome, and the probability of the event is the total weight sitting inside it.

  S: ten pebbles, each carrying weight 1/10

    o  o  o  o  o  o  .  .  .  .
    \______________/  \________/
       A: white        A^c: black
       weight 6/10     weight 4/10

  P(A) = sum of the weights on the pebbles in A = 6/10
Pebble World. Outcomes are pebbles, events are collections of pebbles, and a probability is a weight. Two rules keep it honest: no pebble has negative weight, and the weights of all pebbles together make exactly one.

Two rules and nothing else: every weight is at least zero, and the whole bag weighs one. Everything you will ever prove about probabilities follows from those two sentences plus the instruction to add. This is why the subject is easy at the bottom and hard at the top — the axioms are trivial, and the difficulty is entirely in working out which pebbles you mean.

Read that last clause again, because it is where the mistakes live. A probability is not a property of a pebble. It is a number attached to a set of pebbles, and the same pebble sits in many sets. “The chance of a white pebble” and “the chance of a pebble that is white or large” are the weights of two different collections, and one contains the other.

Question  1.1 A bag holds 6 white and 4 black pebbles, all equally likely. What is the probability that a single draw is white, and which part of that sentence did the work?

Answer \(6/10\). The work was done by “equally likely”, which let the count stand in for the weights.

Question  1.2 In Pebble World, what exactly is a probability, and what two rules constrain it?

Answer A weight assigned to each outcome, with the event's probability being the total weight of its outcomes. Weights are non-negative and sum to one over the whole sample space.

Question  1.3 If \(A\) and \(B\) share no outcomes, why is \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\)?

Answer Because the pebbles in \(A\) and in \(B\) are disjoint collections, so summing weights over their union is summing over each separately and adding the two totals.

2 · The sample space is a choice, not a fact

Roll two dice and add them. What is the probability of a seven?

The obvious sample space is the eleven possible sums, from two to twelve. It is also wrong, and it is wrong in a way worth understanding rather than memorising. If you take the eleven sums as your pebbles, each carrying the same weight, you get \(1/11\). The true answer is \(1/6\). Not a little off — off by nearly a factor of two, from a setup that felt obvious.

The eleven sums are not equally likely, and the naive counting machinery quietly assumed they were. A sum of seven can be made six ways; a sum of two in exactly one. The sample space has to be fine enough that its outcomes really are symmetric.

  second die
       1  2  3  4  5  6
   1 |  2  3  4  5  6  7
   2 |  3  4  5  6  7  8
   3 |  4  5  6  7  8  9
   4 |  5  6  7  8  9 10
   5 |  6  7  8  9 10 11
   6 |  7  8  9 10 11 12

  thirty-six cells, not eleven.  the antidiagonal sum = 7 has 6 of them.
  wrong sample space:  P(7) = 1/11      right:  P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6
The same experiment, two sample spaces. The table is the honest one because its 36 cells are symmetric — the dice cannot tell them apart. The eleven rows of the antidiagonal are not symmetric and cannot be counted.

The right sample space is the 36 ordered pairs \((1,1), (1,2), \dots, (6,6)\). They are indistinguishable to the dice, so they carry equal weight, so counting works.

A second example, because the first one gets remembered as a fact about dice when it is a fact about sets. A family has two children. What is the probability that at least one is a girl? Take the sample space \(\{GG, GB, BG, BB\}\), four outcomes, equally likely. Three of them contain a girl, so \(3/4\).

Now try the sample space \(\{\)both the same, one of each\(\}\). Two outcomes. Under it the answer would be \(1/2\), which is wrong. The pair “one of each” is twice as likely as “both the same”, for the same reason a sum of seven beats a sum of two: it can happen in more ways, and the sample space you chose had stopped being able to see the difference.

So choosing a sample space is choosing a resolution at which to look. Too coarse and outcomes you need to distinguish get merged; too fine and you count things the problem does not care about. The rule of thumb: make the outcomes the finest distinctions the experiment actually makes, and no finer. Two dice are distinguishable even when they look identical, because you can paint one red. Two electrons are not.

Where this gets slippery Whether two coins are distinguishable is a modelling decision, not a mathematical one. If you flip two identical coins and record only “how many heads”, your sample space is \(\{0,1,2\}\) — and those three outcomes are not equally likely, so you must not count them. If you record the pair as ordered, you have four symmetric outcomes and you may. Blitzstein makes this point with a spinning coin in §1.2 precisely because the temptation is to think the sample space is a fact about the coins. It is a fact about what you decided to write down.

Question  2.1 Why does treating the eleven possible sums of two dice as equally likely give the wrong answer for \(P(\text{sum} = 7)\)?

Answer Because they are not equally likely: seven arises from six of the 36 ordered pairs and two from one, so the eleven sums cannot carry equal weight.

Question  2.2 What property must a sample space have before you are allowed to compute a probability by counting outcomes?

Answer Its outcomes must be equally likely — symmetric, in the sense that the experiment gives you no way to tell them apart.

Question  2.3 For two children, \(P(\text{at least one girl}) = 3/4\) under \(\{GG,GB,BG,BB\}\). What goes wrong with the sample space \(\{\)same, different\(\}\)?

Answer “Different” covers two outcomes while “same” covers two as well but only one of each kind of family — the two cells are not equally likely, so counting them as halves is invalid.

Part II — Counting when every pebble weighs the same

3 · The naive definition, and the two conditions it hides

You are dealt five cards from a standard deck. What is the probability of a full house — three of one rank and two of another?

Here is the trouble: nobody can list the hands. There are millions of them. But if the deck is well shuffled, every hand is equally likely, so the probability is a ratio of two counts, and both counts can be computed without listing anything. That is the entire method.

\[ P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|S|} \qquad\text{provided } S \text{ is finite and its outcomes are equally likely} \]

Those two provisos are doing enormous work and are usually invisible. Finite is there so that counting makes sense at all. Equally likely is there because the formula is a dressed-up average: it says each outcome gets weight \(1/|S|\), which is only true if they all get the same weight. The justification is always a symmetry argument, and if you cannot state the symmetry you do not have the right to use the formula.

For the full house: the number of five-card hands is the number of five-element subsets of 52,

\[ \binom{52}{5} = \frac{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48}{5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 2{,}598{,}960 \]

Full houses are counted by choosing, in order: the rank of the triple (13 ways), the three suits of that rank (4 ways), the rank of the pair (12 remaining ways), and the two suits of the pair (6 ways).

\[ 13 \times \binom{4}{3} \times 12 \times \binom{4}{2} = 13 \times 4 \times 12 \times 6 = 3{,}744 \]

so \(P(\text{full house}) = 3744/2{,}598{,}960 \approx 0.00144\) — about one hand in seven hundred. Nothing was listed; two counts were divided.

Now watch the method fail. Load one face of a die so it lands six twice as often as it should. The sample space is still six outcomes, still finite, but the weights are \(2/7, 1/7, 1/7, 1/7, 1/7, 1/7\). Counting outcomes gives \(1/6\) for a six; the truth is \(2/7 \approx 0.286\). The formula did not fail — its second precondition did, and nothing warned you.

Question  3.1 What are the two preconditions of the naive definition \(P(A) = |A|/|S|\)?

Answer The sample space is finite, and its outcomes are equally likely (justified by symmetry).

Question  3.2 How many five-card hands are there from a 52-card deck, and how many of them are full houses?

Answer \(\binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960\) hands; \(13 \times 4 \times 12 \times 6 = 3{,}744\) full houses.

Question  3.3 A die is loaded so that six has weight \(2/7\) and every other face \(1/7\). What does counting outcomes report for \(P(6)\), and what is the truth?

Answer Counting gives \(1/6 \approx 0.167\); the truth is \(2/7 \approx 0.286\), because the outcomes are no longer equally likely.

4 · Trees, and the multiplication rule

You own four shirts and three pairs of trousers. How many outfits?

Twelve, and you can see why by drawing the choices as a tree: each shirt has three branches, four shirts, twelve leaves. The general statement is that sequential choices multiply, provided the number of options at each stage does not depend on what you chose earlier.

  shirt 1 ---- trouser a, b, c        leaves = 4 x 3 = 12
  shirt 2 ---- trouser a, b, c
  shirt 3 ---- trouser a, b, c
  shirt 4 ---- trouser a, b, c

  a licence plate is a tree with six levels:
  [A-Z] -> [A-Z] -> [A-Z] -> [0-9] -> [0-9] -> [0-9]
    26      26      26      10      10      10     = 17,576,000
Sequential choice multiplies. The tree is the justification: every path from root to leaf is one outcome, and a path is chosen by making one choice at each level.

Three letters then three digits gives \(26^3 \times 10^3 = 17{,}576 \times 1{,}000 = 17{,}576{,}000\) plates. A ten-element set has \(2^{10} = 1{,}024\) subsets, because you build a subset by deciding yes or no about each element in turn: two choices, ten times.

That proviso about the option count is not decoration. How many three-digit numbers have all distinct digits and no leading zero? If you start with the hundreds digit you have nine choices; then the tens digit has nine (the ten minus the one you used); then the units has eight. So \(9 \times 9 \times 8 = 648\). Choose the orders badly and the option counts change under you — the fix is to order the stages so that each stage's count is the same no matter what came before.

Question  4.1 How many licence plates use three letters followed by three digits?

Answer \(26^3 \times 10^3 = 17{,}576{,}000\).

Question  4.2 Why does a ten-element set have \(2^{10}\) subsets?

Answer Each element is independently in or out, so a subset is a sequence of ten binary choices.

Question  4.3 How many three-digit numbers have distinct digits and no leading zero?

Answer \(9 \times 9 \times 8 = 648\): nine hundreds digits, then nine tens digits, then eight units digits.

5 · Permutations: filling slots one at a time

Ten people line up for a photograph. How many orderings?

The first position takes any of ten, the second any of the remaining nine, and so on, so \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\). If only three of the ten get medals, the same argument stops after three slots: \(10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720\).

\[ n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1 \qquad\qquad n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]

The second quantity is usually written \(n^{\underline{k}}\) and called a falling factorial. It counts ordered selections of \(k\) things from \(n\), which is to say permutations of length \(k\).

Anagrams, and letters you cannot tell apart

How many distinct arrangements does the word MISSISSIPPI have? Eleven letters, so \(11! = 39{,}916{,}800\) if every letter were distinct. They are not, and here is the trouble: the four I's can be permuted among themselves in \(4! = 24\) ways without changing what you see, so \(11!\) counts each visible arrangement \(4! \times 4! \times 2! = 1{,}152\) times over.

\[ \frac{11!}{1!\,4!\,4!\,2!} = \frac{39{,}916{,}800}{1{,}152} = 34{,}650 \]

Dividing by the internal orderings is the general recipe: count as if everything were distinguishable, then divide out the orderings your eyes cannot detect. The same sentence, in different clothes, is how combinations work.

Question  5.1 How many distinct arrangements of the letters of MISSISSIPPI are there?

Answer \(11!/(4!\,4!\,2!) = 34{,}650\).

Question  5.2 Why is it legitimate to count letters as if they were distinct and then divide by \(4!\) for the repeated I's?

Answer Because each visible arrangement is produced by exactly \(4!\) permutations of the four identical I's, so the overcount is uniform and division removes it exactly.

Question  5.3 Three of ten runners get gold, silver and bronze. How many podiums?

Answer \(10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720\).

Question  5.4 What does \(\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\) count, in one phrase?

Answer Ordered selections of \(k\) items from \(n\) — permutations of length \(k\).

6 · Combinations: dividing out an order you never wanted

Ten people, and you must pick three for a committee. How many committees?

The podium answer, 720, is wrong here, and it is wrong in an instructive way: it counts each committee six times, once for each way of ordering its three members. A committee has no order. So divide by \(3! = 6\):

\[ \binom{10}{3} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = \frac{720}{6} = 120 \]

The general formula divides the falling factorial by \(k!\), because every \(k\)-element subset was counted once for each of its \(k!\) orderings:

\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \]

This is the single most used symbol in probability, and it is not a coincidence that it is a quotient of two things you already know how to count. Every time order is irrelevant, you overcount by exactly \(k!\) — and the check that the overcount is uniform is the same argument every time: whichever subset you picked, it has the same \(k!\) orderings.

The birthday problem, in numbers

Twenty-three people in a room. What is the chance two of them share a birthday?

Direct counting is hopeless: “at least one match” is a union of thousands of overlapping events. Go the other way and count the complement, no shared birthday, which is a sequence of ordered choices: the first person is free (365 options), the second must avoid one day (364), the third avoids two (363), and so on.

\[ P(\text{no match}) = \prod_{i=0}^{22}\frac{365-i}{365} \approx 0.4927 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P(\text{match}) \approx 0.5073 \]

Slightly better than a coin flip, with only 23 people. The reason is not that 23 is a large fraction of 365 — it is that the thing being counted is pairs, and 23 people make \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\) pairs. The instinct that fails is counting people when the sample space is counting pairs.

Question  6.1 How many committees of three can be chosen from ten people?

Answer \(\binom{10}{3} = 120\).

Question  6.2 Why does \(\binom{n}{k}\) divide \(n!/(n-k)!\) by \(k!\)?

Answer Because the ordered count lists each \(k\)-element subset once for each of its \(k!\) orderings, and a subset has no order.

Question  6.3 With 23 people, \(P(\text{a shared birthday}) \approx 0.507\). Which count explains why the answer is so high, and how many is it?

Answer The number of pairs, \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\), not the number of people.

Question  6.4 Why is the birthday problem easier to compute through the complement?

Answer “At least one match” is a union of many overlapping events, while “no match” is a sequence of ordered choices whose options shrink by one each time.

7 · The sampling table

Four questions sound different until you see them as one: pick \(k\) things from \(n\). Does order matter? Are repeats allowed? Answer both and the formula is read off a table.

pick \(k\) from \(n\)with replacementwithout replacement
order matters\(n^k\)\(\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\)
order irrelevant\(\dbinom{n+k-1}{k}\)\(\dbinom{n}{k}\)

Three cells should already be familiar. The fourth, unordered with replacement, is the odd one and it has a story. Picking \(k\) from \(n\) with repeats and no order is the same as putting \(k\) identical stars into \(n\) distinct boxes: lay the stars in a row and insert \(n-1\) bars to separate the boxes, giving \(k\) stars and \(n-1\) bars in a line, and choose which of the \(k+n-1\) positions hold stars.

  k = 3 scoops, n = 4 flavours, order irrelevant, repeats allowed

  scoop positions:  *  *  *  |  |  |

  choose 3 of the 7 positions for the stars:   C(7,3) = 35

  the bars say where one flavour's scoops end and the next begins
Stars and bars. Unordered sampling with replacement is just choosing positions for the stars, which is why its count is a binomial coefficient and not a power.

With \(n = 5\) and \(k = 3\) the four cells read \(125\), \(60\), \(10\) and \(35\). Four different numbers from one question, and picking the wrong cell is the second most common error in the subject — after the wrong sample space.

Question  7.1 Picking 3 things from 5 without order, with repeats allowed: how many ways?

Answer \(\binom{5+3-1}{3} = \binom{7}{3} = 35\).

Question  7.2 Which cell of the sampling table gives the number of five-card poker hands?

Answer Order irrelevant, without replacement: \(\binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960\).

Question  7.3 Why does unordered sampling with replacement give \(\binom{n+k-1}{k}\)?

Answer It is the stars-and-bars count: arrange \(k\) identical items and \(n-1\) dividers in a line and choose the \(k\) positions occupied by the items.

Part III — Counting the same thing twice

8 · Two ways to count the same committee

You want to know the number of ways to choose a committee of three from ten, with one of the three made chair. You could count it two ways, and that is the point.

Count by the committee first: choose the three, then choose the chair from them, giving \(\binom{10}{3} \times 3 = 120 \times 3 = 360\). Count by the chair first: choose the chair from all ten, then the other two from the remaining nine, giving \(10 \times \binom{9}{2} = 10 \times 36 = 360\).

Two different procedures, one set of objects, so the two answers must agree. That is a story proof: you prove an identity by exhibiting both sides as counts of the same thing, and the identity is then true whether or not you can do the algebra.

\[ k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1} \]

Story proofs are not a trick for the lazy. They are how the subject is actually built, because they explain why a formula has the shape it has, and the shape is what you remember. Blitzstein gives this habit its own section (§1.5) for exactly that reason.

Vandermonde, in numbers

Take a set of five people, three of whom wear hats. How many committees of two can you form? \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\), obviously. Now count by hat count: both hatted (\(\binom{3}{2}\binom{2}{0} = 3\)), one of each (\(\binom{3}{1}\binom{2}{1} = 6\)), both unhatted (\(\binom{3}{0}\binom{2}{2} = 1\)). Total \(3 + 6 + 1 = 10\). Same ten committees.

\[ \sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{m}{j}\binom{n}{r-j} = \binom{m+n}{r} \]

Pascal's rule, by one distinguished element

\(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}\). Pick out one person. Either they are not on the committee, which leaves \(k\) seats to fill from the other \(n-1\), or they are on it, which leaves \(k-1\) seats from the other \(n-1\). Those two cases are exhaustive and disjoint, so the counts add.

Question  8.1 What makes a “story proof” valid?

Answer Both sides must count the same set of objects, by two genuinely different procedures, so that equality follows from the object rather than from algebra.

Question  8.2 Verify \(\sum_j \binom{3}{j}\binom{2}{2-j} = \binom{5}{2}\) numerically.

Answer \(1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 = 10\), and \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\).

Question  8.3 Prove \(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}\) by cases.

Answer Fix one element: committees excluding it are counted by \(\binom{n-1}{k}\), committees including it by \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\).

9 · Inclusion–exclusion, and the matching problem

Fifty students take maths, forty take physics, twenty take both. How many take at least one?

The obvious move, adding, double-counts the twenty — and it is the same overcount as the one that made the eleven-sums sample space fail. Subtract it once: \(50 + 40 - 20 = 70\).

\[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B| \]

With three sets the correction needs its own correction, because the triple intersection got added three times and subtracted three times and has ended up counted zero times:

\[ |A \cup B \cup C| = |A| + |B| + |C| - |AB| - |AC| - |BC| + |ABC| \]

The alternating pattern continues. In general

\[ \left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right| = \sum_i |A_i| - \sum_{i

The general form looks forbidding and its special case is one of the prettiest results in elementary probability. Five letters and five addressed envelopes, stuffed at random. What is the probability that no letter reaches its own envelope?

Let \(A_i\) be “letter \(i\) is in envelope \(i\)”. Each \(P(A_i) = 1/5\), and there are five of them. Each pair \(P(A_i \cap A_j) = 1/(5 \cdot 4)\), and there are \(\binom{5}{2}\) pairs. And so on. By inclusion–exclusion, the probability that at least one letter is correct is

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(n-k)!}{n!} = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k!} \]

because \(\binom{n}{k}(n-k)!/n! = 1/k!\) — the binomial coefficient and the factorial collapse together, which is the small miracle that makes this work. So the probability of no match is

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679 \]

For \(n = 3\): \(1 - 1 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac13\), and indeed two of the six orderings of three letters are derangements. For \(n = 4\): \(9\) of \(24\), i.e. \(0.375\). Already close to \(1/e\), and it stays there as \(n\) grows. The chance that a random shuffle of any greedy pile of letters fixes at least one is stuck at about \(0.632\) forever.

Question  9.1 50 take maths, 40 physics, 20 both. How many take at least one?

Answer \(50 + 40 - 20 = 70\).

Question  9.2 In the three-set formula, why does the triple intersection term carry a plus sign?

Answer It was added three times and subtracted three times, so it is currently counted zero times and must be added back once.

Question  9.3 Three letters go into three random envelopes. What is the probability that none is correct?

Answer \(1 - 1 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac13\), matching two derangements out of six orderings.

Part IV — What a probability is, and what it is for

10 · The axioms, and why they end the argument

Everything so far has been counting. But counting assumes the bag is fair, and most bags are not: the die is loaded, the coin is bent, the email is spam. There, the honest question is not “how many” but “what number should I write down”, and counting has nothing to say.

Kolomogorov's answer, in 1933, was to stop asking where the numbers come from. Write down three rules any set of weights must obey and let everything else be a theorem.

axiomstatementwhat it forbids
non-negativity\(P(A) \ge 0\)negative chances
normalisation\(P(S) = 1\)weights that do not total one
countable additivity\(P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i)\) for disjoint \(A_i\)coarse or inconsistent merging

Every rule you have used follows. Complementary events: \(P(A) + P(A^c) = 1\), because \(A\) and \(A^c\) are disjoint and fill the space. Monotonicity: if \(A \subseteq B\) then \(P(A) \le P(B)\), because \(B\) is \(A\) plus \(B \setminus A\). Inclusion–exclusion, for the reason in the last section. None of this required a sample space in which counting works.

The third axiom is the subtle one, and its name — countable additivity — is a commitment. It says you may break a union into countably many disjoint pieces and the weights add. This is what makes probability a branch of measure theory, and it is what allows a uniform distribution on the interval \([0,1]\), where each individual point must have probability zero and yet the whole interval has probability one. Under the naive definition that distribution does not exist, because you cannot count the points.

Where this bites later A density is not a probability. The move from “count the pebbles” to “assign weights and integrate” is exactly the move made in the fifth essay of this series, and it is where \(P(X = x) = 0\) comes from for a continuous variable, and why a language model's likelihood of an exact real-valued output is a density rather than a chance. The axioms are what keep that machinery honest.

Question  10.1 State the three axioms of probability.

Answer \(P(A) \ge 0\); \(P(S) = 1\); and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events.

Question  10.2 Which axiom gives you \(P(A) + P(A^c) = 1\), and how?

Answer Additivity, applied to the disjoint pair \(A\) and \(A^c\), whose union is \(S\) with \(P(S) = 1\) by normalisation.

Question  10.3 Why can a uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) exist as a probability but not as a count?

Answer Because the axioms allow weights on uncountably many points — each point carries probability zero and additivity over intervals supplies the rest — while the naive definition needs a finite, countable sample space.

11 · Where this lands in generative AI

A language model with a 50,000-token vocabulary, writing a 20-token sentence, is choosing one point in a space of size \(50{,}000^{20} \approx 10^{94}\). For comparison, the number of atoms in the observable universe is about \(10^{80}\).

That single number decides the architecture of everything that follows. You cannot enumerate that space, so you cannot compute a normalising constant by summing over it. You cannot count the pebbles, so the naive definition is not available. What is left is the multiplication rule: cut the giant space into a tree and choose one branch at a time, conditional on the branch already taken.

\[ p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}) \]

That factorisation is the multiplication rule of section 4, applied to a tree with one level per token. Every autoregressive model is a multiplication rule with learned branch probabilities, and the training objective is a story about averaging over that tree rather than walking it.

complement countingthe birthday-style argument behind deduplication and contamination checks: the space of pairs, not of items

One habit is worth carrying forward more than any formula. Every essay after this one is a fight over which space an average is taken in, and the failure mode is always the same one from section 2: a space whose outcomes are not symmetric, counted as though they were. When a derivation stops making sense, check what the pebbles are.

Question  11.1 A model has 50,000 tokens and writes 20. Roughly how many possible outputs, and why does the number matter?

Answer \(50{,}000^{20} \approx 10^{94}\) — far beyond enumeration, so normalising constants and expectations must be estimated by sampling, never by summing.

Question  11.2 Which counting rule of this essay is the autoregressive factorisation of a language model?

Answer The multiplication rule: a point in the product space is chosen one coordinate at a time, so the joint probability is a product of conditional ones.

Question  11.3 Why can no generative model compute its likelihood by listing outcomes?

Answer The outcome space is \(|V|^n\), astronomically large, so the naive definition's count of a numerator and a denominator is unavailable; likelihoods must be factorised or bounded instead.

Question  11.4 What is the recurring failure mode this essay warns about, in one sentence?

Answer Counting a sample space whose outcomes are not actually symmetric, and treating the count as though they were.
