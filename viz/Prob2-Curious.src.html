Conditional probability,
Bayes, and being
fooled by base rates

What happens to a probability when you learn something, why the answer surprises almost everyone the first time, and why an integral you cannot compute is the reason variational autoencoders exist

This essay is part of a series written in what we call a mnemonic medium. Small boxes appear along the way, each holding a question and its answer. They are not decoration. They are the essay testing you — and if you take them seriously, you will remember this material for years instead of days.

When a box appears, say your answer out loud before you look. Getting it wrong is useful; the effort of a failed recall is what makes the next one stick. The cards here are deliberately small: one fact, one step, one number each.

This is the second of seven essays on probability. It needs the first one — sample spaces, events, counting — and nothing else. It is also the essay to read before any of the generative-AI material, because every one of those models is, at bottom, a conditional distribution: the distribution of the next token given the previous ones, of an image given a prompt, of a latent code given a picture.

Part I — The room gets smaller

1 · A hundred people, and a smaller room

A hundred people are in a hall. Sixty of them drink tea. Thirty of those sixty also drink coffee. You pick a person at random, and someone whispers in your ear: this one drinks tea. Now what is the chance they drink coffee?

Not \(30/100\). The whisper did something to the problem that is easy to state and easy to get wrong: it threw away forty people. The forty who do not drink tea are gone, and any probability you quote is now a probability among the sixty who remain. Thirty of the sixty drink coffee, so the answer is \(30/60 = 1/2\).

That is the whole definition. Conditioning on an event is deleting the outcomes where that event did not happen, and then renormalising so the survivors still weigh one between them.

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0 \]
Conditioning is restriction and renormalisation. Keep the 60 tea drinkers. Of those, 30 also drink coffee, so the conditional probability is 1/2. Rectangle areas represent population shares.

The numerator is doing the obvious thing — keeping the people who are both. The denominator is doing the non-obvious thing, and it is the part worth naming. Dividing by \(P(B)\) is not bookkeeping; it is the act of pretending that \(B\) was the whole world all along. Without it, the numbers would not sum to one and none of the machinery would work.

Notice also what conditioning does not do. It does not change the truth about any person; it changes the question. This person's coffee habit is a fixed fact. What moved is your information.

Question  1.1 Write the definition of \(P(A \mid B)\) and say in words what the denominator is doing there.

Answer \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)\). The denominator renormalises: conditioning deletes the outcomes outside \(B\), and dividing by \(P(B)\) makes the survivors sum to one.

Question  1.2 Of 100 people, 60 drink tea and 30 of those drink coffee too. Given that a person drinks tea, what is the probability they drink coffee?

Answer \(30/60 = 1/2\).

Question  1.3 Why must \(P(A \mid B) + P(A^c \mid B) = 1\) for any \(B\) with positive probability?

Answer Because inside the shrunken space \(B\), the two events are complementary and cover it, and the conditional weights sum to one by construction.

2 · Trees, and the multiplication rule again

Two cards are dealt from a well-shuffled deck. What is the chance both are aces?

The second card is an ace with unconditional probability \(4/52\), by symmetry. But the draws are dependent: once the first card is known, the probability changes. Conditioning computes the chance of two aces by multiplying the first-ace probability by the second-ace probability given that the first was an ace.

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) \]

Read it as a chain: the chance of both is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second given the first. For the aces, the first card is an ace with probability \(4/52\). Given that, three aces remain among 51 cards, so the second is an ace with probability \(3/51\).

\[ P(\text{both aces}) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} = \frac{1}{221} \approx 0.00452 \]

The same product read the other way round — second card first, then first card given the second — gives the identical number, which is worth checking once because it is the identity that Bayes' rule is built on.

A two-stage tree. Branch labels are conditional probabilities; the bold fractions are joint path probabilities. Multiply the first-card and second-card probabilities along each path.

The multiplication rule is where the phrase “a chain of conditional probabilities” comes from, and it is the reason autoregressive language models work at all: the probability of a sentence is the probability of its first token, times the second given the first, times the third given the first two, and so on down a tree with one level per position.

Question  2.1 State the multiplication rule in both of its symmetric forms.

Answer \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A)\).

Question  2.2 Two cards are dealt. What is the probability both are aces?

Answer \(\frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{221} \approx 0.00452\).

Question  2.3 Why does a tree's path weights always sum to one, no matter how deep it is?

Answer Because each node's outgoing probabilities sum to one, so the leaves partition all the mass --- the tree is just the multiplication rule applied level by level.

3 · The law of total probability

Two urns. The first holds 3 red and 2 blue balls; the second holds 1 red and 4 blue. You flip a fair coin, take an urn, and draw one ball. What is the chance it is red?

You cannot answer directly, because the answer depends on the urn and you do not know which urn you got. But the coin is the missing piece, and it partitions the world into two cases you can compute.

\[ P(\text{red}) = P(\text{red} \mid \text{urn 1})P(\text{urn 1}) + P(\text{red} \mid \text{urn 2})P(\text{urn 2}) \] \[ = \tfrac{3}{5} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{5} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.4 \]

Four in ten. And the shape of the calculation is the general statement: if \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) split the sample space into disjoint pieces that together cover it, then

\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i) \]

This is the law of total probability, and it deserves its name. It says a probability you cannot compute directly can be assembled from pieces you can. It is the single most useful computational move in the subject, and it is what makes Bayes' rule possible: read the formula right to left and it is a numerator; read it as a whole and it is the denominator.

Both conditions matter Disjoint is not enough, and covering is not enough. If the pieces overlap you will double-count — the same error as adding \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) without subtracting the intersection. If they fail to cover the space you will silently drop mass, and the answer will be too small with no warning. When a total-probability computation gives a suspiciously round or surprisingly small number, check the partition before checking the arithmetic.

Question  3.1 State the law of total probability for a partition \(B_1, \dots, B_k\).

Answer \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)P(B_i)\), given the \(B_i\) are disjoint and exhaust the sample space.

Question  3.2 Urn 1 has 3 red of 5, urn 2 has 1 red of 5, and the urn is chosen by a fair coin. What is \(P(\text{red})\)?

Answer \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac35 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac15 = 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.4\).

Question  3.3 What two properties must the conditioning events have before the law applies, and what goes wrong if one fails?

Answer They must be disjoint and cover the space. Overlap double-counts mass; a gap drops it, and both errors are silent.

Part II — Turning it around

4 · Bayes' rule, and the version you can do in your head

A test comes back positive. You want to know the chance you have the disease. The manufacturer has told you the reverse: the chance of a positive result, given the disease. Those are two different conditional probabilities, and confusing them is the most expensive mistake in applied probability.

Bayes' rule is the exchange rate between them, and it is nothing but the multiplication rule written twice and divided.

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A)} \qquad\text{and, with total probability in the denominator,}\qquad P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)} \]

Four quantities with names worth owning. \(P(B)\) is the prior: what you believed before the test. \(P(A \mid B)\) is the likelihood: how often the evidence appears when the hypothesis is true. \(P(A)\) is the evidence: how often it appears at all. And \(P(B \mid A)\) is the posterior: what you should believe now.

The odds form, and why it is the one that fits in your head

Divide the posterior by the posterior of the negation and the \(P(A)\) cancels:

\[ \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B^c \mid A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A \mid B^c)} \times \frac{P(B)}{P(B^c)} \qquad\text{posterior odds} = \text{likelihood ratio} \times \text{prior odds} \]

This is the form people can actually reason with, because odds compose by multiplication and because the likelihood ratio is the one number a test can legitimately advertise. It also explains immediately why a great test can still leave you unconvinced: multiplying odds by 19 does almost nothing to odds of 1 to 99.

Concretely. A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 95% of cases and falsely flags 5% of healthy people. You test positive. What is the chance you have it?

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0095}{0.0095 + 0.0495} = \frac{0.0095}{0.059} \approx 0.161 \]

Sixteen percent. In odds: prior 1 to 99, likelihood ratio \(0.95/0.05 = 19\), posterior odds \(19\) to \(99\), which is \(19/118 \approx 0.161\). Same number, and the odds version shows where it came from in one line.

Coherency: the order of evidence cannot matter

Learn \(A\), then \(B\). Or learn \(B\), then \(A\). Bayes' rule applied twice gives the same posterior both times, because both are \(P(B \mid A \cap B)P(A \cap B)/P(A \cap B)\) in disguise. This is the coherency property of §2.6, and it is worth knowing because it is a test: any belief-updating scheme that depends on the order in which you heard things is wrong, and you can detect it without knowing the right answer.

Question  4.1 Write Bayes' rule and name its four parts.

Answer \(P(B \mid A) = P(A \mid B)P(B)/P(A)\): prior \(P(B)\), likelihood \(P(A \mid B)\), evidence \(P(A)\), posterior \(P(B \mid A)\).

Question  4.2 Prevalence 1%, sensitivity 95%, false-positive rate 5%. What is \(P(D \mid +)\)?

Answer \(0.0095/0.059 \approx 0.161\) — about 16%.

Question  4.3 In the odds form of Bayes' rule, what does the likelihood ratio multiply, and what is its value in the 95%/5% test?

Answer It multiplies the prior odds; here \(0.95/0.05 = 19\).

Question  4.4 State the coherency property and why it is a useful check.

Answer Bayesian updating is order-independent: hearing \(A\) then \(B\) gives the same posterior as \(B\) then \(A\). Any scheme whose answer depends on the order is therefore wrong.

5 · A rare disease, in full

One person in ten thousand has a condition. The test is excellent: it catches 99% of cases, and it falsely flags 1% of healthy people. You test positive.

Almost everyone says the chance is about 99%. It is about one percent, and the gap between those two answers is the single most consequential error in this chapter. Here is the arithmetic.

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.0001}{0.99 \times 0.0001 + 0.01 \times 0.9999} = \frac{0.000099}{0.000099 + 0.009999} = \frac{0.000099}{0.010098} \approx 0.0098 \]

Under one percent. Now the same fact in whole people, which is how it should have been explained in the first place. Take a million people.

Natural frequencies reveal the base rate. False positives outnumber true positives by about 101 to 1. The boxes show counts, not population-proportional areas.

The lesson generalises into a rule of thumb. When the prior is tiny, the likelihood ratio has to be enormous to move the posterior, because it is fighting a hundred-to-one or ten-thousand-to-one head start. A test that multiplies your odds by 99 is impressive, and 99 times one-in-ten-thousand is still about one-in-a-hundred.

The corollary is uncomfortable and worth stating: the same positive result means very different things for a person with symptoms and a person picked at random. The test did not change. The prior did.

Question  5.1 Prevalence 1 in 10,000, sensitivity 99%, false positives 1%. What is \(P(D \mid +)\)?

Answer \(0.000099/0.010098 \approx 0.0098\), under one percent.

Question  5.2 In a million people, how many true positives and how many false positives does that test produce?

Answer 99 true positives and 9,999 false positives — out of 10,098 positives.

Question  5.3 The same test is given to two people and both test positive. Why can the two posteriors differ enormously?

Answer Because their priors differ — symptoms versus a random screening — and Bayes' rule multiplies the likelihood ratio into the prior odds.

6 · Conditional probabilities are probabilities

A short section with a large payoff. Fix an event \(B\) with \(P(B) > 0\) and define a new set of weights by \(A \mapsto P(A \mid B)\). Then that function satisfies all three axioms.

Non-negative, because both numerator and denominator are. The whole space gets weight \(P(S \mid B) = P(S \cap B)/P(B) = 1\). And for disjoint \(A_1, A_2\), the intersections with \(B\) are disjoint too, so the weights add.

What this buys is not a new theorem but the licence to reuse every old one. Inclusion– exclusion, the union bound, complementary events, independence — all of it transfers inside the shrunken space, for free, without re-proving anything. It is the same reason a change of units does not invalidate an equation.

Question  6.1 Fix \(B\) with \(P(B) > 0\). Why is \(A \mapsto P(A \mid B)\) a probability in its own right?

Answer It satisfies all three axioms: non-negative, \(P(S \mid B) = 1\), and additive over disjoint events.

Question  6.2 What does that fact let you do that you otherwise could not?

Answer Apply every theorem already proved about probabilities — inclusion–exclusion, the union bound, complements — inside the conditional space without re-deriving them.

Part III — Independence, and four ways to be fooled

7 · Independence is an assumption you make

Flip a coin twice. Does the first result tell you anything about the second? No — and that “no” is not a theorem about coins. It is a claim about the world that you are entitled to assert and that could turn out to be false.

\[ A, B \text{ independent} \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) \iff P(A \mid B) = P(A) \]

Independence means conditioning tells you nothing: learning \(B\) leaves \(A\)'s probability exactly where it was. The multiplicative form is the one to remember, because it is the form that extends to three events and to random variables.

The most useful consequence is a freebie: independence propagates to complements. If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, so are \(A\) and \(B^c\), and \(A^c\) and \(B^c\). The reason is a two-line computation with total probability, and the intuition is that a fact which tells you nothing about \(A\) also tells you nothing about its negation.

One warning, because this is where the words part company from the mathematics. In everyday speech “independent” suggests two things that do not interact. In probability it is a numerical condition on a joint distribution, and two events can satisfy it while being causally entangled in the most intimate way — see the next section, where the assumption is precisely what fails.

Question  7.1 Give two equivalent definitions of independence for events \(A\) and \(B\).

Answer \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) when \(P(B) > 0\).

Question  7.2 Is independence of two coin flips a theorem or an assumption?

Answer An assumption about the experiment, asserted from symmetry — it is never derived.

Question  7.3 If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, what follows for \(A\) and \(B^c\)?

Answer They are also independent: a fact that says nothing about \(A\) says nothing about its complement either.

8 · Pairwise is not mutual, and conditional is not either

Mutual independence for three events means every subset factors, not just the pairs:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B), \quad P(A \cap C) = P(A)P(C), \quad P(B \cap C) = P(B)P(C), \quad P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C) \]

All four, or it is not mutual. This is not pedantry. Toss two fair coins and define \(A\) = “first is heads”, \(B\) = “second is heads”, \(C\) = “the two coins match”. Each pair is independent: \(P(A \cap B) = 1/4 = P(A)P(B)\), and similarly for the other two pairs. But \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = 1/4\), not \(1/8\), because the first two already force the third.

Now the harder and more useful distinction. Conditional independence means

\[ P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C) \]

and it is genuinely orthogonal to plain independence — neither implies the other. Both directions have a standard example, and both are worth carrying.

Question  8.1 What must hold for three events to be mutually independent?

Answer Every pair must factor and the triple must factor: \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)\), not just the three pairwise conditions.

Question  8.2 Two fair coins, \(A\) = first heads, \(B\) = second heads, \(C\) = they match. Which condition fails, and what are the numbers?

Answer The triple: \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = 1/4\) while \(P(A)P(B)P(C) = 1/8\). All three pairs do factor.

Question  8.3 Give an example of two events that are independent but conditionally dependent given a third.

Answer Two fair coins, conditioned on “exactly one is heads”: the coins are marginally independent, but given that, either result determines the other.

Question  8.4 Why is “dependent, but conditionally independent given a common cause” the shape that latent-variable models rely on?

Answer Because it says observations are linked only through a hidden variable, so conditioning on that variable factorises them — which is what makes a latent model tractable at all.

9 · Conditioning as a way to solve problems

How many tosses of a fair coin until you see two heads in a row?

You cannot list the ways, because the process can run arbitrarily long. But you can condition on the first toss and get an equation rather than a sum. This is called first-step analysis and it is the most powerful use of conditioning there is.

Let \(x\) be the expected number of further tosses from a clean start, and \(y\) the expected number of further tosses given that the last toss was a head. Condition on the next toss.

\[ x = 1 + \tfrac12\,y + \tfrac12\,x \qquad\qquad y = 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12\,x \]

From the first, \(x = 2 + y\). Substitute into the second: \(y = 1 + \tfrac12(2+y)\), so \(y = 4\) and \(x = 6\). Six tosses on average. The first equation says a tail puts you back where you started; the second says a head after a head ends the game with no further tosses.

The same move solves gambler's ruin. A fair game, you start with 1 unit, you stop at 3 or at 0. Let \(p_i\) be the chance of reaching 3 from \(i\). Condition on the next step: \(p_i = \tfrac12 p_{i-1} + \tfrac12 p_{i+1}\), with \(p_0 = 0\) and \(p_3 = 1\). The solution is a straight line, \(p_i = i/3\), so \(p_1 = 1/3\). With a fair coin, your chance of doubling from 1 to 3 before busting is exactly one in three.

Where this breaks First-step analysis needs the future to depend on the present only through a summary. For “two heads in a row” the summary is “did the last toss show a head” — two states suffice. For “three heads in a row” you would need three. Choose the summary badly and you get equations that are not closed, which is the tell that your state is too coarse.

Question  9.1 How many tosses of a fair coin, on average, until two heads in a row?

Answer Six.

Question  9.2 In first-step analysis, what does conditioning on the first step actually buy you?

Answer A recursion instead of an infinite sum: the quantity you want is expressed in terms of itself, evaluated in a simpler state.

Question  9.3 In a fair game starting from 1 unit with absorbing barriers at 0 and 3, what is the chance of reaching 3 first?

Answer \(1/3\), because the solution of \(p_i = \tfrac12(p_{i-1}+p_{i+1})\) with those boundaries is \(p_i = i/3\).

10 · Four ways to be fooled

Each of these is the same error in a different costume: a conditional probability quoted with the conditioning event silently swapped.

Monty Hall

Three doors, one prize. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens a different door showing nothing, then offers you the swap. Your door holds the prize with probability \(1/3\); the remaining unopened door holds it with probability \(2/3\). The host's action was not random — it was conditioned on where the prize is — and that is why the two doors are no longer symmetric. Switching wins twice as often.

The prosecutor's fallacy

A DNA profile matches one person in a million. A suspect is found in a city of two million and matches. What is the chance he is innocent? Not one in a million.

The quoted number is \(P(\text{match} \mid \text{innocent})\), which is \(10^{-6}\). The question is \(P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{match})\). Among two million innocent people you expect about two matches, so with one guilty party there are roughly three matching people in the city and the chance this one is innocent is about \(2/3\). Same number, inverted conditional, and the answer moves from one in a million to two in three.

The two-children problem

“At least one of my two children is a boy.” The chance both are boys is \(1/3\), because the surviving sample space is \(\{BB, BG, GB\}\). “My elder child is a boy.” The chance both are boys is \(1/2\), because the surviving space is \(\{BB, BG\}\). The two sentences feel like the same information and are not; the first is a statement about a set of outcomes, the second about a named individual.

Simpson's paradox

Treatment A beats treatment B in the mild cases and in the severe cases, yet loses overall.

The reversal is not a trick of arithmetic; it is that A was given mostly to severe cases and B mostly to mild ones. The overall rate is a weighted average of the two strata, and the weights differ between the arms. Whenever a marginal comparison and a conditional one disagree, the conditional one is the causal one — provided you know which variable to condition on, which is exactly the hard part.

Question  10.1 In Monty Hall, what is the probability of winning if you switch, and why is the host's knowledge essential?

Answer \(2/3\). The host's choice is conditioned on the prize's location, so the two unopened doors are not symmetric.

Question  10.2 A profile matches one in a million; the city holds two million. Roughly what is the chance a matching suspect is innocent, and which conditional was misquoted?

Answer About \(2/3\). The quoted \(10^{-6}\) is \(P(\text{match} \mid \text{innocent})\), not \(P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{match})\).

Question  10.3 Why does “at least one is a boy” give \(1/3\) for two boys while “the elder is a boy” gives \(1/2\)?

Answer The first leaves three equally likely outcomes \(\{BB,BG,GB\}\), the second leaves two \(\{BB,BG\}\).

Question  10.4 What mechanism produces Simpson's paradox, in one sentence?

Answer An unadjusted confounder whose distribution differs between the groups, so the marginal rate is a weighted average with different weights on each side.

Part IV — Where this lands in generative AI

11 · The integral in the denominator

Train a model on pictures. The model has a latent variable \(z\) — a compressed description — and the generative story is \(p(x, z) = p(z)\,p(x \mid z)\): draw a code, then draw a picture from it. To score a picture you need \(p(x)\), and total probability says

\[ p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz \]

That integral is over every possible code, in however many dimensions the latent space has, and it has no closed form for any interesting model. Everything in the variational essay follows from this one sentence. The posterior you actually want,

\[ p(z \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid z)\,p(z)}{p(x)} \]

is Bayes' rule with an uncomputable denominator — which is why you approximate it with a second network and why the approximation turns the log-likelihood into a lower bound. The ELBO is not a modelling choice; it is what you do when the evidence in Bayes' rule will not evaluate.

One more connection, because it is the cleanest example of Bayes' rule surviving intact in a deep model. Classifier-free guidance asks for \(p(x \mid c)\) for a condition \(c\), and gets it by combining a conditioned and an unconditioned score. Written as gradients of log-densities, Bayes' rule gives

\[ \nabla_x \log p(x \mid c) = \nabla_x \log p(x) + \nabla_x \log p(c \mid x) \]

so the guided update is the unconditional direction plus a multiple of the classifier direction. The guidance scale is the multiple. Nothing about the conditional-probability structure changed; only the amount of the second term.

Question  11.1 Why can no VAE compute \(p(z \mid x)\) exactly?

Answer Because Bayes' rule needs \(p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)p(z)\,dz\), an integral over the whole latent space with no closed form.

Question  11.2 The GAN discriminator \(D^*(x) = p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)\) is which probability from this essay?

Answer A posterior: the probability that \(x\) came from the data, given that it came from one of the two distributions.

Question  11.3 In classifier-free guidance, what plays the role of the likelihood and what of the prior?

Answer \(\nabla_x \log p(c \mid x)\) is the likelihood term (the classifier direction) and \(\nabla_x \log p(x)\) the prior term (the unconditional score).

Question  11.4 Why is the isotropic prior \(p(z) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}(z_i; 0, 1)\) an assertion from section 7 rather than a derived fact?

Answer It asserts that the latent coordinates are independent, which is a modelling choice made for symmetry and convenience, not a consequence of anything.
